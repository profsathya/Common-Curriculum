Test and Commit

Submission 2 of 2 in this half · Week 2 · due date to be set · 20 points

Purpose

Leave this page with one problem worth nine weeks of your attention: real, live, the right size, and one you would have worked around or left alone before. Pinned down this precisely, it becomes something you can actually solve, with AI doing the parts it can see and you bringing the parts it cannot.

Tasks

Paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it.

Each + opens guidance, one example, a reflection question and a box to write in. Nothing under a + is required.

Throughout: your uncertainty marks, still marked. Read back through what you write and put a question mark at the end of any sentence you are not certain of. Do not go back and make those sentences sound more confident.

  1. 1.Your four checks, run against each candidate problem, with unanswerable checks noted rather than guessed.
    1. Check 1. Are the people real and specific?
    2. Check 2. Is something already handling it, and falling short?
    3. Check 3. Is it live right now?
    4. Check 4. Is it the right size?
    Before you start — how to run the checks

    Go through your list one at a time. A few sentences per check. Rough is fine.

    Where you cannot answer a check, write that instead of guessing. “I do not know who else this costs” is a result, and a more useful one than an invented answer.

    Each check below has its own box. Answer it for every candidate problem on your list, one paragraph per candidate.

    1a. Check 1. Are the people real and specific?
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    You already named who is paying in your gap statement. This check tests whether that answer is specific enough to act on.

    Name people or roles, not categories.

    If you cannot get more specific than a category, one of two things is true. Either the problem is bigger than you can see from where you sit, or the cost is mostly yours and you have been generous in describing who else pays.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy
    Does not passPasses
    The teamThe four account managers who cover renewals
    Everyone gets frustratedThe shift lead, who improvises a job for every new arrival
    Our clientsThe two clients who have asked twice this quarter who owns their account

    Reflection

    Which candidate did you name most easily, and is that because you know it best or because it costs you most?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Every candidate problem on your list has a paragraph.
    • Each names people or roles, not categories — or says plainly that you cannot get more specific than a category.
    • Where it is a category, it says which of the two things is true: the problem is bigger than you can see from where you sit, or the cost is mostly yours.
    1b. Check 2. Is something already handling it, and falling short?
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Complete this sentence: “X handles this today, but it does not ___.”

    Something already handles almost every real problem, usually badly, and often it is a person absorbing it manually. That person is your best lead for Sprint 3.

    If nothing handles it at all, look twice. Sometimes that means you found something genuinely neglected. More often it means nobody has needed it handled, which is a sign the cost is smaller than you thought.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    The outgoing owner handles this today by telling the incoming owner directly, but it does not reach anyone else on the team.

    The shift lead handles this today by finding something for the new person to do, but it does not connect them to anything that continues after that day.

    Reflection

    For each candidate, is the thing handling it today a process, or a person absorbing it by hand?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Every candidate problem has the sentence completed: who or what handles it today, and what it does not do.
    • Where nothing handles it at all, you have looked twice and said which reading applies: genuinely neglected, or nobody has needed it handled.
    1c. Check 3. Is it live right now?
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Is this happening currently, or is it a memory or a hypothetical?

    The test is simple: could you go watch this happen in the next two weeks? If not, it will not survive Sprint 3, where you have to talk to someone about the current state.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy
    • A problem from a job you left is a memory. You cannot go look at it, and you cannot talk to anyone about how it works today.
    • A problem that would appear if the team doubled is a hypothetical. There is nothing to observe yet.
    • A problem that was fixed six months ago is a memory even if it still annoys you.

    Reflection

    Which candidate could you go and watch this week, and have you?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Every candidate problem is called live, a memory, or a hypothetical.
    • For each live one, you have said where you could go watch it happen in the next two weeks.
    1d. Check 4. Is it the right size?
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    This is the check that decides whether the next nine weeks have anywhere to go, and it is the one that most often goes wrong quietly. Wrong size fails in two directions.

    Too small looks like: only you touch it. One conversation or one decision would resolve it. There is nothing you would have to find out, because you already know everything about it.

    Too big looks like: you cannot describe how it works now in a single paragraph. Nobody owns it. Everyone is affected, which usually means you cannot name anyone.

    Neither is a reason to drop a candidate problem. Both are reasons to move it.

    So this check is a calibration rather than a gate. You adjust the altitude until the problem fits, and it fits when all three of these are true:

    • You can describe how it works now in one paragraph.
    • There is at least one person other than you with a stake in it, who would talk to you about it.
    • There is at least one thing you would have to find out to move on it.

    Those three are not arbitrary. The first is what makes the problem writable in your frame next week. The second is what Sprint 3 requires, when you go talk to someone. The third is what Sprint 4 requires, when you name a gap in your own knowledge and go close it. A problem that fails any of the three will stall in a specific week, and you can see which one from here.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy
    ProblemDiagnosisThe moveResult
    The export format for my monthly report is wrongToo small. Only I touch it, and one ticket would fix itGo up. What is this an instance of?Nobody owns export definitions when the source system changes, so every reporting team patches by hand
    Our culture resists changeToo big. Cannot describe the current state, cannot name who paysGo down. Pick one instance you have actually seenDecisions made in the Tuesday leadership meeting do not reach the people who execute them until the following week

    Reflection

    Which candidate did you move, in which direction, and what did it say before the move?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Every candidate problem is tested against all three conditions: one paragraph, one other person with a stake who would talk to you, one thing you would have to find out.
    • A candidate that is too small or too big is moved, up or down, and the moved version is written out.
    • A moved candidate has the checks run again on the moved version.
    Reading the results

    Not every failure means dropping something.

    • Fails outright. Nothing to name for Check 1, or it is a memory. Set it aside.
    • Murky. You could not answer, but you can imagine finding out this week. Keep it live and note what you would need.
    • Wrong size. Passes in principle but is too small or too big. Move it up or down and run the checks again on the moved version.
    • Passes cleanly. Rarer than you expect. If more than two do this, look again, because you may be answering from assumption rather than knowledge.
  2. 2.Two or three challenges from your AI exchange, and your response to each.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Now bring AI in, and notice what you are using it for. Not to find problems, and not to run the checks. You have already done both. You are using it to find out where you answered from assumption instead of knowledge, which is difficult to see in your own writing.

    Give it your two or three strongest candidate problems and your check answers, and ask something like the prompt in the example.

    Then read what comes back critically. Some of it will be generic and worth ignoring, because it cannot see your situation. Some of it will land, and the ones that land are usually the places you wrote quickly.

    Record two or three challenges it raised and your response to each. Your response can be “fair, I do not actually know that,” or “no, I have seen this directly and here is how.” Both are good answers. Only agreeing with everything is a bad answer, and so is dismissing all of it.

    An example of what to ask, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    Here are candidate problems I am considering and my answers to four checks. For each one, tell me which claims I am asserting rather than actually knowing, and where someone who works in this area would likely disagree with me. Do not tell me which to pick.

    Reflection

    Which challenge landed, and was it on something you had written quickly?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • There are two or three challenges, and each one is something the AI actually raised about your candidates or your check answers.
    • Each challenge has your response, and the response says either that you do not actually know that, or that you have seen it directly and how.
    • You have not agreed with everything, and you have not dismissed all of it.
  3. 3.Your committed problem, written as a gap.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Pick the candidate problem that came through the checks in the best shape. Not the one you like most, and not the one that would be most impressive if you solved it.

    Write it in this shape: Right now, how it works today. It could, the state you think is possible. The gap costs, who is paying, and what it costs them. This has been going on, roughly how long. It has not been fixed because, your best current guess.

    If nothing survived. It happens, and it is recoverable. Go back to your list first and look for something you set aside that was the wrong size rather than the wrong problem. Most apparent dead ends are size problems, and Check 4 tells you which direction to move. If that genuinely does not work, go back to the two other places to look and spend two days hunting rather than recalling. Work-arounds are where the surviving problems usually hide. If you get here, say so in your submission rather than forcing a choice. Committing to a problem you do not believe in is worse than being a few days late to one you do.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    Right now, account handovers happen in a conversation between the outgoing and incoming owner, and nothing is recorded or announced. It could be that the rest of the team knows within a day of a change. The gap costs clients, who repeat context to someone who should already have it, and the four other people on the team who act on stale ownership for days at a time. This has been going on as long as I have been here, at least two years. It has not been fixed because each individual handover feels small enough to handle informally.

    Reflection

    Which of the five lines changed most between your Candidate List and now, and what changed it?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • All five lines are there: right now, it could, the gap costs, this has been going on, it has not been fixed because.
    • “The gap costs” names who is paying and what it costs them, in the words that passed Check 1.
    • Any sentence you are not certain of still has its question mark.
    • If nothing survived, you have said so rather than forcing a choice.
  4. 4.Your reasons: why this one, why not the others, and what would make you switch.
    1. Why this one. Which checks it passed cleanly, and what makes it worth nine weeks.
    2. Why not the others. One or two sentences per candidate problem, naming the check that ruled it out.
    3. What would make you switch. One line.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Real choices are rarely clean. The example is what one looks like when two candidates both mostly passed.

    Notice what it does. It names the deciding check, it admits the choice was not obvious, it says out loud what the runner-up had going for it, and it names the condition that would reverse the decision. That last line is the one people skip and the one worth most in week five.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    I am going with the account handovers over the volunteer onboarding.

    Both passed Checks 1 through 3. Handovers won on Check 4. For the volunteer problem I could only name one person other than me with a stake, our board chair, and she is hard to reach and would mostly tell me what she wants to be true. For handovers I can name four account managers and two clients, and I sit next to three of them.

    The volunteer problem is arguably more important. I picked handovers because I can actually find things out about it in ten weeks, and I could not say the same for the other one.

    What would make me switch: if the handover problem turns out to be one person’s habit rather than a missing process, there is much less to find out than I think, and it becomes too small.

    Reflection

    Would the person with the biggest stake in your runner-up accept your reason for not choosing it?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • 4a names which checks it passed cleanly and what makes it worth nine weeks.
    • 4b has one or two sentences for every other candidate problem, and each names the check that ruled it out.
    • 4c is one line, and it is a condition you could actually observe.
    • Read it as a classmate would: they can see the deciding check, that the choice was not obvious, and what the runner-up had going for it.
  5. 5.Your runner-up, one line.
    Guidance, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    One line. Which candidate problem you would take up if this one turned out to be a dead end.

    You will probably not need it. It costs nothing now, and it means that if something goes wrong in week three, you have somewhere to go that is not back to a blank page.

    Reflection

    What would you need to see in week three to know it was time to switch to it?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It is one line.
    • It is one of the candidate problems from your list, and it is not the one you committed to.

Criteria

Done — the four checks run against each candidate problem, with unanswerable checks noted rather than guessed; two or three challenges from your AI exchange, with your response to each; your committed problem written as a gap, in the five-line shape; your reasons, all three parts — why this one, why not the others, and what would make you switch; your runner-up, one line; your uncertainty marks, still marked.

Strong — the checks are answered from what you already know, and “I do not know” appears where it is true; the responses to the AI’s challenges are neither agreeing with everything nor dismissing all of it; the reasons do what the worked example does — name the deciding check, admit the choice was not obvious, say out loud what the runner-up had going for it, and name the condition that would reverse the decision; the committed problem is one you could go watch happen in the next two weeks.

Points — 20. Most of the weight is on the reasons in task 4, since that is what a defensible commitment actually looks like.

Checklist — one line per task. Run it on your own draft; your reviewer runs the same list.

  1. Run all four checks on each candidate problem; where you cannot answer a check, write that instead of guessing.
  2. Record two or three challenges the AI raised and your response to each.
  3. Write your committed problem as a gap: right now, it could, the gap costs, this has been going on, it has not been fixed because.
  4. Write why this one, why not the others, and what would make you switch.
  5. Name your runner-up, one line.
  6. Leave your uncertainty marks in.

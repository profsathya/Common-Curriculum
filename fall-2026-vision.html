North star — agreed 2026-07-18 · our wording, not what students will see

A student builds a repeatable process for finding — and continually updating — the place where something they genuinely care about, something they can get good at, and something the world needs overlap, then follows through on what that turns up, past comfort, past friction, for real.

The honest goal: every student gets one real experience of starting from a surface interest, going deeper, and staying with it when it gets hard. A student's first answer is a starting point, never a wrong answer. And we treat caring as real when the student keeps going past the hard parts — not because they say they care.
Open question (Sathya, 7/20): do students need particular understanding, attitudes, or abilities before those three questions (care about / good at / world needs) are even answerable — or is trying to answer them the best way to start? The design's current answer: we never ask students these questions directly; we build up to each one through activities and evidence (see the design principles). Measuring where each student starts, in the first two weeks, will tell us who arrives ready and who needs more support.

The weekly cadence

This is not part of the checklist above, because it never gets finished. One block per week: opened Monday, closed Friday, then the next one. Only the current week is worth looking at.

Week of ____

  • Read the Own-your-progress submissions against the floor. Every submission is checked against that item's minimum-quality line. Clears the floor, full points and a short note on what to push further. Falls short, the guidance says what is missing. No judgement call beyond the floor — that is the point of the floor.
  • Read the Graded items, with a person deciding. Same read, but the score is not automatic: the draft is prepared, Sathya confirms or changes it before anything is released.
  • Send what the week showed to the Commons. Who is stuck, who went quiet, who is ready for a harder conversation — that is what triage needs, and the weekly read is where it comes from.
  • Get next week ready before it starts. Finish its content, read it through, then take off the work-in-progress tag and the grey. A week goes live only after that check.
Closing a week means all four are done. Open the next block on Monday.

How this design was builtFour layers: who the student becomes → the teaching moves → the proof → the build.

Method

The four layers

  • 1 · Outcome — who the student becomes, in one sentence per course. Agreed 7/8.
  • 2 · Design — the few teaching moves that produce that outcome. Agreed 7/18.
  • 3 · Evidence — how we will know it is actually happening. Agreed 7/18–7/20.
  • 4 · Build — the pages, assignments, schedule, and tools. In progress; planned in the other tabs.

Two working rules

  • Agreeing on a principle is not the same as agreeing on how to implement it. Implementation gets worked out during the build — and if something turns out not to work, it comes back here as an open question instead of being quietly changed.
  • Everything on this page is written in our own working language. Before anything is shown to students, it gets rewritten in words that make sense to students. That rewrite is a required step, tracked in the Shared Build tab.

Layer 1 · Who the student becomesOne sentence per course, agreed 7/8.

Agreed 7/8
Both coursesThe north-star sentence at the top of this page. We deliberately say "something the world needs" rather than "your career" — it points at the same thing without pushing students to perform career-mindedness.
CST286A student picks something they already care about, uses physics to see how it really works, and gets better at it because of that depth — then discovers the same habit of digging deeper works on any interest or goal, and starts treating depth as a discipline, not a one-off. Two clarifications we agreed on: going deep is the point and physics is the tool for practicing it — a student is not required to cross into physics for its own sake. And while computing is the natural home base (it's a CS course), a student's genuine interest matters more than staying inside the major.
CST349A student learns to direct their own growth — turning a vague interest into a staked, specific pursuit with weekly milestones they set, measure, and adapt. "Growth" means more than one thing: the student learns to define what growth means for them across several dimensions, measure it, plan for it, and adjust as they go. Agreed 7/20: CST349 is the north-star process applied to a student's professional life.

Layer 2 · The teaching movesFour moves shared by both courses, plus three per course. We call them "design principles."

Agreed 7/18

One rule over all of them

Every principle must turn into a process students can repeat on their own, after the course. That repeatability is where adaptability comes from.

Shared — both courses

  • 1 · Build caring from evidence, not from one quick answer. Ask each student for three moments in their life when they lost track of time, or got annoyed enough to actually do something — from different periods, including childhood. One answer is usually the comfortable default ("I like video games"); three answers force a real pattern to show. We give memory-jogging categories so nobody stares at a blank page, and AI helps surface each moment and name the common thread. Why this way: asking "what's your passion?" directly produces either the easy answer or paralysis.
  • 2 · Widen from caring to the overlap — then build the skill, don't test for it. Two steps, in strict order: first find what the student cares about (move 1), then ask where that meets something the world needs. We never combine them into one question — asking "what do you care about that the world needs" up front makes students filter their real interests by usefulness. The student lands on one to three possible pursuits. From there the course helps them build the skill; we do not test whether they already have it. Why: students shouldn't be judged on skill they haven't had a chance to build.
  • 3 · Following through when it gets hard is the graded backbone. The proof that caring is real is that the student keeps going past friction. This is also where skill gets built and shown. We never ask students to declare how much they care. Why: declared enthusiasm is easy to fake; sticking with hard work is not.
  • 4 · Come back to it — don't just start it. The north star says "continually updating," so the course needs regular moments where students revisit their pursuit and revise it. CST349's existing Goal Plan — revised at the start, middle, and end of the semester — is the likely shared mechanism. Why: choosing once in week two and never looking back is not a process.

One structural decision

Everything in this design is course content students work through on their own time. Class sessions — online or in person — get designed later, around that work: conversation and engagement with what students are digging into. So CST286's Monday-online / Wednesday-in-person split matters when we design sessions, not at this layer.

CST286's three

  • 1 · Physics is a tool, not the destination. Success is how deep the student went and what that depth did for their pursuit — never how much physics was covered.
  • 2 · The depth has to pay off. Every deep dive must come back to the thing the student cares about: understanding how it works has to visibly change what they do, make, or choose. Otherwise depth is just trivia.
  • 3 · The habit must transfer — by design. Late in the course the student runs the same deep-dive process on a second interest. That's how "depth as a discipline" becomes something they experienced rather than something we told them.
  • Standing caution: never design activities that nudge students toward topics that "connect to CS or their career." When that happens, students chase what they think the professor wants to hear instead of what they actually care about.

CST349's three

  • 1 · The pursuit is the student's own professional growth — said openly. In 286 the career angle is a trap to avoid; in 349 it is the declared subject. Neither course sneaks it in.
  • 2 · Define growth before chasing it. Each student names what growth means for them — several dimensions, with a way to measure each. The course's existing self-directed-learning materials are the starting scaffold.
  • 3 · The weekly milestone loop is the backbone — and the student runs it. Milestones are set, measured, and adjusted by the student. The skill being graded is the steering, not the achievement. Instructor, AI, and peers act as mirrors, not judges.

Layer 3 · How we will knowOne deepening piece of work · students define their own evidence · checked in face-to-face conversations.

Agreed 7/18–20

What counts as proof

  • One deepening piece of work is the evidence. We are deliberately breaking from the usual pattern — many assignments with points for each. The evidence for the semester is one pursuit going deeper over time: fewer, connected pieces, each going further than the last.
  • Students define their own evidence. Early in the course, each student answers: "what would convince a skeptic that I went deeper and got better?" They refine that plan against examples and feedback, Sathya approves it, and then they collect and present that evidence. There is no standard evidence checklist handed out.
  • Evidence is checked in conversation. Two to four times in the semester, each student — alone or in a small group — meets Sathya face to face (Zoom with video on counts) and walks through their work. These same meetings are where students revisit and revise their pursuit (move 4 above). Real understanding shows in live conversation in a way documents can't fake.

The semester arc — both courses

Sprint 0: find your pursuit, write your evidence plan, record your starting point. Sprints 1–2: go deeper on it. Sprint 3: run the same process on a second interest — proof the method works on anything. Final weeks: put the whole story together and present it.

Assignment rhythm — both courses

One course assignment every one or two weeks. An assignment may show up in Canvas as two or three smaller submissions, but those are installments of one continuing piece of work — not separate tasks. Each check-in meeting focuses on one assignment, in depth.

How grading works — and how it stays honest in the age of AI

  • Half the points come from submitting the work. The other half are earned in the meeting, by walking Sathya through three to five pieces of past work.
  • Which pieces get discussed is chosen at the meeting. So no piece of work can be submitted and forgotten — and there is no way to win by submitting a lot and hoping the weak pieces aren't picked. The meeting score covers everything submitted since the last meeting.
  • Points lost at a meeting mostly stay lost. If a student can't explain their own work, at least half of the lost points are gone for good — they can't gamble on winning them back next time. Students should come to meetings prepared. (Still to decide: does none of it come back, or half? Working assumption: half. Also worth considering: make the first meeting a practice round with full recovery, so students learn how meetings work before the stakes are real.)
  • No student loses points because Sathya ran out of time. Meetings happen during class when possible, by appointment. A student who showed up but didn't get a turn earns full points in office hours.
  • The meeting is "walk me through your work" — not an interrogation. Using AI heavily is not an offense. The question the meeting answers is: did the understanding stick?
  • We are also testing this grading design itself. Nobody knows yet what honest assessment looks like in the age of AI. This is our experiment, and we'll measure whether it works.

Two grading standards

  • CST286 — grade the distance traveled, not the altitude reached. "Deeper" is measured from each student's own starting point, recorded in Sprint 0. A dancer digging into the physics of balance and a CS major digging into why GPUs overheat start in very different places; what we grade is how far each one traveled. There is no fixed physics bar every student must clear.
  • CST349 — grade the steering, not the scoreboard. What we grade is whether the student ran an honest cycle — set a milestone, measure, adjust. Numbers that always go up are not required; an honest miss followed by a real adjustment is strong evidence the process works.

What the evidence must show

  • CST286: (1) "I can show how it really works" — and answer follow-up what-if questions live. (2) "I'm better at the thing I care about because of that understanding" — with a clear before and after. (3) "I could run this process again on something new" — the process itself is documented, and Sprint 3 proves it.
  • CST349: (1) "I know what growth means for me" — my own dimensions, measures, and starting points. (2) "My measures moved, and I can show why." (3) "I steered it myself" — my Goal Plan revisions show the decisions I made along the way.

Next up

Goal

CF-4 · Sprint 0: find the pursuit, plan the evidence, record the startThe shared opening two weeks of both courses.

Re-scoped 7/20

Goal

By the end of Sprint 0, every student has: good working habits with AI, a real interest surfaced from their own life (not a guess at what the professor wants), one to three candidate pursuits, an approved plan for what evidence they'll collect, and a recorded starting point.

What's in it

  • Working with AI: phases where AI is off-limits so judgment gets built; norms for using it well; reflections written by hand so they can't be delegated.
  • Finding the pursuit: the three-moments exercise, the thread across them, then the widening question — "who would this matter to, and why?"

  • The starting point: a short intake that records where each student begins — this is what "distance traveled" grading measures from. Which measures to use is an open decision.
  • Intake also asks (optional, with explicit permission): phone number and consent for the TA to text, and what other courses they're taking — so a struggling student can be found and helped.

CF-6 · A tool to run the meeting systemNew 7/20 — "I will really need a tool to manage all of this effectively."

Spec first

Why

The grading design means tracking, for every student: their pursuit, their evidence plan and starting point, what they've submitted, which pieces were discussed at which meeting, meeting appointments, scores, and points that stayed lost. By hand, across 47 students in one course and 83 in the other, that fails. A small purpose-built tool makes it routine.

What it tracks

  • Per student: pursuit, evidence plan, starting point, submissions, meeting history.
  • Meetings: who's scheduled when, who was in class but didn't get a turn (they earn full points at office hours), which pieces of work were discussed.
  • Scores: submission points, meeting points, and lost points that don't come back — pushed to Canvas.

How it gets built

Alan writes a short spec (shape approved by Sathya first) → Sathya builds it with Claude Code — the same way Architect Studio went from idea to working site in a day → Alan tests it end to end before students touch it.

Agreed 7/8

The shift in identity we're after

Not "I learned physics" — but "I know how to go deeper on anything I care about, and I've felt what that depth buys me." Physics is the featured tool for practicing depth; the habit of digging is what students keep.

Two standing rules

  • Students choose their pursuit freely — inside computing or far from it. Nothing in the course may nudge them toward "topics that connect to CS or your career"; that only teaches them to perform.
  • This tab describes the course content. The Monday-online and Wednesday-in-person sessions get designed afterward, around conversation and engagement with what students are digging into.

Grading and remaining build work

▶

How grading works hereDistance traveled, not altitude reached · half on submission, half in the meeting.

Agreed 7/18–20

The three things evidence must show

  • "I can show how it really works" — and hold up to live what-if questions.
  • "I'm better at the thing I care about because of that understanding" — clear before and after.
  • "I could run this process again on something new."

Mechanics

Grades measure distance traveled from each student's own recorded starting point. Half the points come on submission; the other half are earned at check-in meetings (2–5 per semester), where the student walks through several past pieces of work. With 47 students meeting in groups of about four, each round is roughly twelve meetings — spread across class sessions. Details live in the CF-6 tool plan.

The item structure, settled 13 Aug 2026. Three kinds of work, named the same way in all three courses. Graded assignments are the work itself, 100 points each. Sprint Exams (CST286 and CST349) and the mid-term (CST499) are 200 points, hand-written and closed book — no notes, no cheat sheet — with the questions published ahead of time. Own your progress guidance runs one to three times a week; it carries due dates and point values (100 points each, settled 15 Aug) so a student can see whether they are on track, but it sits in a grade group weighted at 0% and does not count toward the course grade. This retires the earlier key item / checkpoint pair, and with it the rule that every assignment is worth 100 points.

What each course adds up to. CST286 and CST349: 9 graded assignments (900) + 3 Sprint Exams (600) = 1500 submitted, matched by 1500 in check-ins — 3000 total. CST499: 8 graded items (800) + the mid-term (200) = 1000 submitted, matched by 1000 in interviews — 2000 total. The half-and-half split holds in all three without adjustment. A sitting can cover more than one sprint; each sprint's points are scored at whichever sitting covers them.

How Canvas holds students to it, settled 15 Aug 2026 — for the three Fall 2026 CSUMB courses, not (yet) a CTI-wide policy. Graded items are required activities: the module requirement is satisfied by submitting, even late, and graded items run in sequence only within a sprint. Own your progress items each carry their own submit requirement (the record a future progress-checkbox feature reads) but are never sequential — skipping one never blocks the next. Each sprint's graded chain opens with a reflection assignment required of everyone, and no sprint is gated behind the one before it (revised 16 Aug 2026): a caught-up student reflects on the sprint they finished, a behind student names what they missed and their plan for it — nobody advances silently, and nobody is locked out of fresh work. Each of those reflections is one of the course's graded assignments rather than an addition to them, so the point structure is unchanged. Only submitted work can be defended at a sitting; unsubmitted work forfeits both its submission points and its share of the interview half. The step-by-step Canvas build recipe lives in skills/building-canvas-fall-2026/ — written for these three courses only.

Open, and needed before the course pages can be re-cut: which nine items each of CST286 and CST349 carries, which eight CST499 carries, and whether the weekly reflection continues as Own your progress guidance now that it carries no points.

The course

Taught in spring · 83 enrolled for Fall (up from ~58) · 2 units · fully built — and now being restructured, not just refreshed.
▶

What the course is nowLearn to direct your own growth — the north-star process, applied to your professional life.

Agreed 7/8 · framing 7/20

The shift in identity we're after

From a vague interest ("I want to get better at interviews," "I want to be a real engineer") to a specific, committed pursuit with weekly milestones the student sets, measures, and adjusts. Growth here means more than one thing: each student defines their own dimensions of growth, measures them, plans, and adapts. Unlike 286, the professional angle isn't avoided — it's the declared subject of the course.

The design in short

  • The student's pursuit is their own professional growth, worked on openly.
  • Growth gets defined before it gets chased — dimensions and measures first.
  • The weekly milestone loop is the backbone, and the student steers it. The steering is the skill being graded.
  • The Goal Plan — revised at start, middle, and end — is where the pursuit gets revisited and updated.
  • Sprint 3 runs the whole loop again on a second dimension of growth — proof the process is repeatable.

The restructure

The big change from the new design — this replaces "update the dates and fix known issues."
▶

From ~60 assignments to ~8–12One assignment every week or two, each an installment of one continuing piece of work.

Design 7/20

Why

The proof should have the same shape as the goal. A course about directing one pursuit shouldn't grade sixty scattered submissions. Points follow the one deepening piece of work, not the count of tasks completed.

How

  • Map the existing ~60 assignment pages onto the semester arc: keep what serves the arc as installments, merge or drop the rest.
  • Fix the documented problems from spring along the way: worked examples for 5 Whys (most chains circled instead of deepening) · "evidence of change" must name a specific moment, not a feeling · fewer frameworks in Sprint 1 · honest time estimates on every page (the Quick Start box named here on 7/2 is superseded — pages open with Purpose, per the writing-assignments skill) · no placeholders or stale dates (dates come from the schedule page).
  • Adopt the same grading design as 286 (agreed 7/20): half on submission, half earned at check-in meetings — "something along those lines needs to be tested for the age of AI." With 83 students, meetings will be in small groups; the numbers get worked out in the CF-6 tool plan.

The three things evidence must show

  • "I know what growth means for me" — my own dimensions, measures, and starting points.
  • "My measures moved, and I can show why." Graded on the steering, not the scoreboard — an honest miss with a real adjustment counts.
  • "I steered it myself" — the Goal Plan revisions show the decisions I made.

Tasks

  • Spring version fully built; problems documented; design agreed (7/8–7/20)
  • The mapping: ~60 pages → ~8–12 assignments with installments
  • Redesign the Goal Plan and milestones as the evidence plan and progress record
  • Adopt the shared page template and Sprint 0 · schedule page · student-language rewrite
  • Connect to the grading tool and daily playbook (reminders, draft grades, TA list)

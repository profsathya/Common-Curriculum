What changed at CTI this year, and the Human Value Framework

Video, about 13 minutes

Our September 2025 meeting told us we needed to move faster and more intentionally on AI, and this past year we have tried to do that. Four changes shape the work now:

  • Moving up a layer of abstraction. In our summer open source program, students could write a proposal to a project maintainer in addition to solving an existing issue. About 58 students took part, 20 submitted a proposal, and maintainers accepted 11.
  • Foundational skills for every discipline. We are widening our focus from computer science skills to the foundational skills any student needs, whatever their field.
  • Short experiments. We run programs of six to ten weeks so that we can learn from each one quickly and adjust the next.
  • Designing around human value. We start from the question of where human value comes in when people work with AI, and we build the learning from there.

These changes run through our current work: the six-week Career Intelligence program for new graduates this June, the Human Value Framework in Sathya's CSUMB courses, the two-course Applying AI at Work certificate with De Anza College for mid-career professionals, and early conversations with other disciplines, starting with kinesiology.

The Human Value Framework starts from our hypothesis that human value can grow as AI gets better. At the top are two outcomes we treat as two sides of one coin. Superagency means people can take on bigger problems with AI than they could before. Human value means a graduate can explain what would be worse about the work if AI had done it alone, and how their own involvement made it better. Beneath the outcomes is Symbiotic Thinking, the core thinking skill for working with other people and with AI. From it, students grow three capabilities: self-directed learning, integrative solving and adaptive building.

What makes the CTI approach distinct

Video, about 6 minutes

Words like judgment, taste and durable capabilities are now used by almost everyone, and people often mean slightly different things by them. So we asked the Council to test a claim:

We are doing something important in helping the large middle of society thrive through the AI transition.

  • The problem, concretely. Point A is working through short tasks with guidance at each step. Point B is taking a goal and working toward it with AI: choosing the next task, learning what it teaches, adapting the approach and choosing again. Handling goals with AI used to be expected of senior professionals, and it is becoming the expectation for new graduates.
  • A framework for both the outcomes and how to build them. The Human Value Framework describes the outcomes we want and a theory of how students get there, so each practice connects to the outcome it is meant to build.
  • Testing with the institutions that serve the middle of society. We are testing the framework with students at community colleges and CSUs: freshmen, juniors, seniors, new graduates and mid-career professionals. We learn from what works and adjust.

We said on Friday that we could be wrong at any of these steps, and that is why we asked the Council to challenge the claim. The breakout rooms took up three questions: Are we on the right path? What is not clear? What questions are we not asking?

What we heard from the Council

  • Say what each pilot is testing. Council members asked us to name the hypothesis behind each pilot, the outcomes we expect, and how we will report what we see.
  • The path into software work is changing, and the work is growing. People in roles outside computing are now building their own tools. That creates new needs, such as governance, getting systems to work together, and handing work over when people move on. An open question for us: what from computing do these workers need?
  • The vision is wide. People in other fields will expect us to understand their work first, and it helps to keep showing the results we can already point to.
  • Employers describe the same shift. The ability to take a goal and work toward it with AI is what employers increasingly look for.
  • Teaching methods matter as much as content. How are instructors, especially at community colleges, building the skills to facilitate this kind of learning?
  • Follow students over time. Following students as alumni would show outcomes beyond a single course or program.

An early look: the six qualities in our courses

CST286 and CST349 at CSUMB, Fall 2026, work submitted through 19 September

Sathya's courses this fall are one of the places we are testing the Human Value Framework. Alongside the framework, we are nurture-tracking six qualities students grow. Three are inward-facing, where what changes is the student. Three are outward-facing, where what changes is the work. The course pilot is testing whether we can see these qualities in everyday coursework, and where AI can help us read them.

For each assignment, we wrote a short ladder for the qualities that assignment asks students to show. Each ladder has four levels, described by what can be seen in a student's writing. The chart shows, for each quality, the share of the 129 students in CST286 and CST349 whose work reached each level at least once. Self-knowledge Inward-facing Level 3 or higher: 88% · Level 4: 57%

What we mean: Seeing yourself accurately: what pulls you, what you're like, and whether your read on yourself holds up.

Read in: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST286 GI: What Are My Priorities; CST286 OYP: Understand my interest; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap; CST349 OYP: Know what makes me tick; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 122 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 7 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 No edge 2 students (2%)

Claims to know it, or what is written as "not sure" / "weak" is not about their own understanding or evidence.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student wrote that none of their findings were weak, without pointing to anything they were unsure of.
Level 2 General 7 students (5%)

The gap or uncertainty is general: the whole topic or area, or a general claim about oneself with no category, occasion or reason.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student said they need to build technical skills beyond their coursework, without naming which skills.
Level 3 Specific 40 students (31%)

Names one specific thing: a category of their own time, the question they could not answer, what is missing from their own finding, a capability someone could check.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student named the one question from a classmate that they could not answer about their own project idea.
  • A student said their weakest claim about the job market was the one backed by only two postings.
Level 4 Reasoned 73 students (57%)

The specific gap plus the reasoning: why their understanding or evidence stops there, what would show them right or wrong, or an assumption about themselves tested against what happened. Confirming counts the same as revising.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student realized they had assumed they understood an activity because they do it every week, and named the specific part they could not yet explain.
  • A student compared their guess about how much they study with what their own time log showed, and explained why the guess was off.
Self-regulation Inward-facing Level 3 or higher: 57% · Level 4: 5%

What we mean: Managing your attention, effort, emotions and routines so you stay with something you chose.

Read in: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Nutrition for Mind Plan. 111 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 18 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 No change 7 students (5%)

No account of their own time or work, no change, or a change with nothing in it.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student said they would give more priority to finishing work early, with nothing that would look different.
Level 2 Resolution 31 students (24%)

A resolution (often with its source), but no approach you could watch happen.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student resolved to move time from short videos to homework, without saying how they would do it.
Level 3 Concrete approach 66 students (51%)

A concrete approach linked to a need the student observed in their own work or time.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student planned to work in a different room, without a phone or headphones, for a set block of time.
Level 4 Monitored approach 7 students (5%)

The approach plus how they will tell whether it is working, or an adjustment already made because it was not.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student noticed a new reading habit slipping in its second week and changed how they were doing it.
  • A student planned to write a prediction before each test run and compare the result against it, so they would not rely on impressions.
Owning the outcome Inward-facing Level 3 or higher: 43% · Level 4: 21%

What we mean: Stepping back from what you did to ask what it meant: to the work, to other people, and to where you're headed.

Read in: CST286 GI: What Are My Priorities; CST349 OYP: Choose your growth opportunity; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap; CST349 OYP: Nutrition for Mind Plan. 85 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 44 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 No commitment 2 students (2%)

No try or plan, the try is put off, or the plan is answered with something else (e.g. the benefit, not the action).

What this looked like in student work

  • A student listed what a change would give them, without saying what they would do.
Level 2 Wish or direction 27 students (21%)

A direction or wish with nothing anyone could look at afterwards.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student wrote that they want to spend more time on homework.
Level 3 Checkable 29 students (22%)

Checkable: an action with an amount, trigger, occasion, date or product.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student set a routine with a date and a length: thirty minutes on one Sunday each month reviewing job postings.
Level 4 Checkable and owned 27 students (21%)

Checkable, and it answers something the student found about themselves on the page (their time picture, a named discomfort, a documented limit or gap).

What this looked like in student work

  • A student found a specific gap in their own portfolio and committed to a project that closes it, with a deadline before the application season.
Initiative Outward-facing Level 3 or higher: 43% · Level 4: 12%

What we mean: Thinking ahead, setting a goal, and taking the first step toward it.

Read in: CST286 OYP: AI Dojo Setup; CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Understand my interest; CST349 OYP: Know what makes me tick; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 95 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 34 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 No owned choice 7 students (5%)

Did the step with no purpose of their own, declined it, or filled it from something other than the task.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student filled a step with phrases from the class discussion when the step asked them to look at job postings.
Level 2 As directed 33 students (26%)

Did what the page set, or named a choice or change only in general terms; nothing visibly their own.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student said they would make their question “more specific,” without saying how.
Level 3 Owned choice 40 students (31%)

A choice of their own, stated or carried out on the page, specific enough to see whether it happened.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student rewrote their own research question on the page into a narrower one they could investigate.
Level 4 Owned choice with purpose and lesson 15 students (12%)

The owned choice plus its stated purpose and what it taught them, or what in what they read led to it.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student chose simulation settings so the effect of each change could be read off, used that to predict the next result, and noted where the pattern would stop holding.
  • A student found that a narrow job search returned almost nothing, widened it, and wrote what that taught them about how to search.
Working with uncertainty Outward-facing Level 3 or higher: 70% · Level 4: 33%

What we mean: Willingness to act even without all questions answered, cultivating the skill to find the right point to act.

Read in: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Why physics matters, and how simulations model the world; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap. 118 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 11 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 Nothing open 5 students (4%)

Nothing that could be wrong: no prediction or test, the check confirms itself, or the comparison is avoided.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student called their prediction “somewhat on point” in a way that could not have turned out wrong.
Level 2 Asserted or felt 23 students (18%)

A real claim, but the outcome is asserted ("close", "correct") or doubt is a feeling, with no way to settle it; or a signal too loose to check.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student said they were not sure their idea was right, with no way to check it.
Level 3 Named test or comparison 48 students (37%)

Prediction and observation compared, or a named test or signal someone else could check; nothing on what the result can support.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student wrote down a prediction, ran the test, and stated plainly that the result did not match.
Level 4 Test with a reading 42 students (33%)

The test plus how to read it: what result would count against them, what could make the check unreliable, where the relationship stops holding, or how far they trust it.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student's prediction missed by a wide margin, and they reasoned that their model was probably missing a factor.
  • A student set up a check on how well they explain ideas to others, and named the result that would show they had not explained well.
Adaptability Outward-facing Level 3 or higher: 42% · Level 4: 19%

What we mean: Looking at your work as an opportunity to learn, learning precisely what the experience of the work is teaching, and identifying the level at which the lesson should be applied to make changes in the next iteration.

Read in: CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Why physics matters, and how simulations model the world; CST349 OYP: Choose your growth opportunity; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 81 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 48 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

Level 1 Nothing weighed 4 students (3%)

A pause answered with a bare keep or something unrelated; nothing changed or kept for a stated reason.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student kept every answer the same at each pause, with no reason given.
Level 2 General or partner-only 23 students (18%)

A change or keep in general terms, a wording change, "same answers", or an account of the partner's work only.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student said they changed the grammar of their answer but the idea stayed the same.
Level 3 Specific 29 students (22%)

Says specifically what changed, was kept, or still needs checking in their own answer.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student combined two of their goals into one plan after talking with a partner.
Level 4 Reasoned 25 students (19%)

The specific change or keep and what in the exchange led to it: what was weighed and why the answer ends where it does. A reasoned keep counts equally.

What this looked like in student work

  • A student kept their conclusion after a partner discussion because both agreed it needed more evidence, and named the evidence that would settle it.
  • A student kept a relationship they had found in a simulation and added the conditions under which the numbers would differ from it.

How to read these numbers. These are draft ratings. Alan, our AI assistant, rated each submission against the ladders. Astra, a second AI assistant, reviewed the ladders and a sample of the ratings, and the two worked through their disagreements. Sathya has not yet reviewed the ratings. Part of what we want to learn is where AI reads these qualities reliably and where a person's judgment is needed.

Each quality was read only in the assignments that ask for it, so some qualities rest on one or two assignments and others on four. A student with no rating on a quality is counted as not yet seen, never as a low level. The level descriptions differ from quality to quality, so a level 3 in one quality is not the same as a level 3 in another. The examples under each level describe what students wrote in our own words, with no student's writing quoted. CST499 students were rated on an earlier five-point scale with no written ladder, so they are not in this chart.

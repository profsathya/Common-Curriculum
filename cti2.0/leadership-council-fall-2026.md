# Leadership Council: Fall 2026 Update

*Friday 18 September 2026 · Our direction, the Council’s feedback, and what we are learning in our courses*

## How CTI’s approach is changing

[Video: https://youtu.be/YMR-yE7Ca-k] *Video, about 13 minutes*

At our September 2025 meeting, the Council urged us to respond to AI faster and with clearer purpose. Three changes now shape our work:

- **Foundational skills for every discipline.** Our focus is naturally broadening from computer science to foundational skills, led by the underlying problem we are trying to solve.
- **Short experiments.** We run six- to ten-week programs so we can learn quickly and adjust the next one.
- **Designing around human value.** We design learning around the question of how people make work better when they use AI.

The [Human Value Framework](https://computingtalentinitiative.org/framework/) starts from our hypothesis that human value can grow as AI gets better. It connects two outcomes: **superagency**, the ability to take on bigger problems with AI, and **human value**, the ability to explain how one’s involvement made the work better than AI could have done alone.

To build these outcomes, we teach **Symbiotic Thinking**, our core thinking skill for working with people and AI. It supports three capabilities: **self-directed learning, integrative solving and adaptive building**.

We are applying this approach in Sathya’s CSUMB courses and the two-course Applying AI at Work certificate with De Anza College for mid-career professionals. It also shaped June’s six-week Career Intelligence program for new graduates. We are beginning conversations with other disciplines, starting with kinesiology.

## What makes the CTI approach distinct

[Video: https://youtu.be/p88xrt9yfhE] *Video, about 6 minutes*

Our focus is the broad range of learners served by community colleges and CSUs. We asked the Council to test this claim:

> We are doing something important in helping the large middle of society thrive through the AI transition.

[Diagram: three circles (the problem, concretely · a framework for outcomes and how · testing with the middle of society), CTI in the overlap]

- **The change students need to make.** Students need to move from Point A, completing short tasks with guidance at each step, to Point B, working toward a goal with AI: choosing the tasks, learning from the results and adapting the approach. We argued that the independence once expected of senior professionals is increasingly expected of new graduates working with AI.
- **A framework that connects teaching to outcomes.** The Human Value Framework describes the outcomes we want and how we think students can develop them, so that each teaching practice connects to an intended outcome.
- **Testing across stages of education and work.** We are testing the framework with community college and CSU learners, from first-year students to new graduates and mid-career professionals, and adjusting as we learn.

We asked the Council to challenge both our direction and our assumptions. The breakout rooms discussed three questions: *Are we on the right path? What is not clear? What questions are we not asking?*

## The main points

Thank you to everyone who joined us on September 18. We shared how CTI is helping students learn to pursue goals with AI: deciding what to do next, learning from the results, and adapting their approach.

- **Our direction.** We are testing the Human Value Framework through short programs and courses, with the aim of helping students take on bigger problems with AI and explain how their own contribution improves the work.
- **The Council’s feedback.** Council members suggested that we make each pilot’s hypothesis and expected outcomes clear, share the evidence, and follow students beyond a single course. They also suggested that, as we expand into other fields, we first learn how those fields work and help instructors develop the teaching practices this requires. Members described employers asking for the same shift toward working to a goal with AI, and noted that people outside computing are now building their own software and may need help with governance, integration and handover.
- **Our early evidence.** We are looking for six learner qualities in the coursework of 129 students at CSUMB. The draft AI ratings show examples of these qualities; they do not yet establish growth or the framework’s effectiveness.

## An early look at evidence: the six qualities in our courses

*CST286 and CST349 at CSUMB, Fall 2026, work submitted through 19 September*

There was a lot of interest at the meeting in how we can verify that what we are doing helps students. Our hypothesis is that these six qualities give us the earliest feedback on whether students are developing the higher-order, durable capabilities we are aiming for. In Sathya’s fall courses, we are testing whether these six qualities can be identified in everyday coursework and whether we can use AI to assess and track them. Self-knowledge, self-regulation and owning the outcome are inward-facing qualities; initiative, working with uncertainty and adaptability are outward-facing qualities.

The chart summarizes draft ratings for 129 students in CST286 and CST349. For each quality, it shows the share of students whose work showed a specific or reasoned signal of that quality at least once. These signals are the top two levels of a four-level scale for each quality. This is an early snapshot of evidence in student work, not yet a measure of growth over time.

### Self-knowledge (Inward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 88%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Seeing yourself accurately — what pulls you, what you're like, and whether your read on yourself holds up.

**Level 1: No limit identified** (2 students (2%))  
Claims understanding without identifying a limit, or expresses uncertainty unrelated to their own understanding or evidence.

- *Example:* A student wrote that none of their findings were weak, without pointing to anything they were unsure of.

**Level 2: General** (7 students (5%))  
Describes a broad gap or uncertainty without specifying the skill, situation or reason.

- *Example:* A student said they need to build technical skills beyond their coursework, without naming which skills.

**Level 3: Specific** (40 students (31%))  
Identifies a specific aspect of their own time, an unanswered question, a gap in their evidence or a capability someone could check.

- *Example:* A student named the one question from a classmate that they could not answer about their own project idea.
- *Example:* A student said their weakest claim about the job market was the one backed by only two postings.

**Level 4: Reasoned** (73 students (57%))  
Explains the specific gap, what evidence would confirm or challenge their understanding, or how an assumption about themselves held up in practice. Confirmation and revision count equally.

- *Example:* A student realized they had assumed they understood an activity because they do it every week, and named the specific part they could not yet explain.
- *Example:* A student compared their guess about how much they study with what their own time log showed, and explained why the guess was off.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST286 GI: What Are My Priorities; CST286 OYP: Understand my interest; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap; CST349 OYP: Know what makes me tick; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 122 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 7 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

### Self-regulation (Inward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 57%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Managing your attention, effort, emotions and routines so you stay with something you chose.

**Level 1: No change** (7 students (5%))  
Gives no account of their time or work, proposes no change, or proposes a change without describing it.

- *Example:* A student said they would give more priority to finishing work early, with nothing that would look different.

**Level 2: Resolution** (31 students (24%))  
States an intention, sometimes with a reason, but gives no observable approach for carrying it out.

- *Example:* A student resolved to move time from short videos to homework, without saying how they would do it.

**Level 3: Concrete approach** (66 students (51%))  
A concrete approach linked to a need the student observed in their own work or time.

- *Example:* A student planned to work in a different room, without a phone or headphones, for a set block of time.

**Level 4: Monitored approach** (7 students (5%))  
Describes how they will check whether the approach works, or how they adjusted it after finding it was not working.

- *Example:* A student noticed a new reading habit slipping in its second week and changed how they were doing it.
- *Example:* A student planned to write a prediction before each test run and compare the result against it, so they would not rely on impressions.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Nutrition for Mind Plan. 111 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 18 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

### Owning the outcome (Inward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 43%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Stepping back from what you did to ask what it meant — to the work, to other people, and to where you're headed.

**Level 1: No commitment** (2 students (2%))  
Describes no attempt or plan, postpones action, or describes a hoped-for benefit without saying what they will do.

- *Example:* A student listed what a change would give them, without saying what they would do.

**Level 2: Wish or direction** (27 students (21%))  
States a wish or direction without an action someone could verify later.

- *Example:* A student wrote that they want to spend more time on homework.

**Level 3: Checkable** (29 students (22%))  
Specifies an action with an amount, trigger, occasion, date or product that makes it possible to check.

- *Example:* A student set a routine with a date and a length: thirty minutes on one Sunday each month reviewing job postings.

**Level 4: Checkable and owned** (27 students (21%))  
Specifies a checkable action that responds to something they discovered about their time, discomfort, limits or gaps.

- *Example:* A student found a specific gap in their own portfolio and committed to a project that closes it, with a deadline before the application season.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 GI: What Are My Priorities; CST349 OYP: Choose your growth opportunity; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap; CST349 OYP: Nutrition for Mind Plan. 85 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 44 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

### Initiative (Outward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 43%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Thinking ahead, setting a goal, and taking the first step toward it.

**Level 1: No personal purpose stated** (7 students (5%))  
Completes the step without stating a personal purpose, declines it, or responds using material unrelated to the task.

- *Example:* A student filled a step with phrases from the class discussion when the step asked them to look at job postings.

**Level 2: As directed** (33 students (26%))  
Follows the instructions or describes a choice only in general terms, without showing a specific decision of their own.

- *Example:* A student said they would make their question “more specific,” without saying how.

**Level 3: Specific personal choice** (40 students (31%))  
Describes or carries out a choice of their own, specific enough to check whether it happened.

- *Example:* A student rewrote their own research question on the page into a narrower one they could investigate.

**Level 4: Choice with purpose and learning** (15 students (12%))  
Explains their choice, its purpose, and what they learned from it or what they read that informed it.

- *Example:* A student chose simulation settings so the effect of each change could be read off, used that to predict the next result, and noted where the pattern would stop holding.
- *Example:* A student found that a narrow job search returned almost nothing, widened it, and wrote what that taught them about how to search.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 OYP: AI Dojo Setup; CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Understand my interest; CST349 OYP: Know what makes me tick; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 95 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 34 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

### Working with uncertainty (Outward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 70%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Willingness to act even without all questions answered, cultivating the skill to find the right point to act.

**Level 1: No testable claim** (5 students (4%))  
Offers no testable prediction, uses a check that can only confirm the claim, or avoids comparing the prediction with the result.

- *Example:* A student called their prediction “somewhat on point” in a way that could not have turned out wrong.

**Level 2: Claim without a clear check** (23 students (18%))  
Makes a claim or expresses doubt, but gives no clear way to check it. May call a result “close” or “correct” without supporting evidence.

- *Example:* A student said they were not sure their idea was right, with no way to check it.

**Level 3: Specific test or comparison** (48 students (37%))  
Compares a prediction with an observation, or identifies a checkable test or signal, without explaining what the result supports.

- *Example:* A student wrote down a prediction, ran the test, and stated plainly that the result did not match.

**Level 4: Test with interpretation** (42 students (33%))  
Explains how to interpret the test: what would challenge the claim, make the check unreliable, limit the relationship or affect confidence in the result.

- *Example:* A student's prediction missed by a wide margin, and they reasoned that their model was probably missing a factor.
- *Example:* A student set up a check on how well they explain ideas to others, and named the result that would show they had not explained well.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 GI: Physics Learning Goal; CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Why physics matters, and how simulations model the world; CST349 GI: Define what growth means for you; CST349 OYP: Find Your Gap. 118 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 11 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

### Adaptability (Outward-facing) — Specific or reasoned signals: 42%

<!-- LOCKED DEFINITION: do not edit without Sathya -->
**What we mean:** Looking at your work as an opportunity to learn, learning precisely what the experience of the work is teaching, and identifying the level at which the lesson should be applied to make changes in the next iteration.

**Level 1: No reason given** (4 students (3%))  
Keeps an answer without explaining why, or gives an unrelated response when asked to reconsider it.

- *Example:* A student kept every answer the same at each pause, with no reason given.

**Level 2: General response or partner’s work** (23 students (18%))  
Describes keeping or changing an answer only in general terms, changes wording alone, or discusses only a partner’s work.

- *Example:* A student said they changed the grammar of their answer but the idea stayed the same.

**Level 3: Specific** (29 students (22%))  
Identifies specifically what they changed, kept or still need to check in their own answer.

- *Example:* A student combined two of their goals into one plan after talking with a partner.

**Level 4: Reasoned** (25 students (19%))  
Explains what they changed or kept and how the exchange informed that decision. Keeping an answer with a reason counts equally.

- *Example:* A student kept their conclusion after a partner discussion because both agreed it needed more evidence, and named the evidence that would settle it.
- *Example:* A student kept a relationship they had found in a simulation and added the conditions under which the numbers would differ from it.

Assignments reviewed: CST286 OYP: Explore Physics Loop; CST286 OYP: Why physics matters, and how simulations model the world; CST349 OYP: Choose your growth opportunity; CST349 OYP: Read the Market. 81 of 129 students had at least one rating on this quality; the other 48 are counted as not yet seen, which is different from a low level.

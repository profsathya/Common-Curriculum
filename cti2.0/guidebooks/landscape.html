What we are doing: testing the CTI 2.0 framework — in its entirety or one component at a time — through small 4–8 week pilots, and collating what we learn into a comprehensive picture that informs how human development adapts in the age of AI.

The student's journey — the highest-level view

Every CTI program, seen as one experience. Click any step for what actually happens in it.

1 · Arrive A student joins any CTI program — course, cohort, or pathway.
more

One account follows them across all CTI programs, so nothing is repeated and growth accumulates. First contact includes a short piece of real work — never an entry test.

→
2 · Reflect A first reflection-survey: finding the qualities already in them.
more

Our stance: everyone has curiosity, agency, and drive — the environment just hasn't sparked them yet. The reflection-survey helps a student see where they're starting, in their own words plus a few short scales. It is never used to select or gate anyone.

→
3 · Learn & build The program runs the full capability loop with AI as a partner.
more

Self-Directed Learner → Integrative Solver → Adaptive Builder, integrated by Symbiotic Thinking. Practice happens in the Dojo (an AI coach that pushes back) and, soon, the Commons (learning together). Students produce real work: goal plans, reflections, projects.

→
4 · Demonstrate Students present and defend real work. Their card grows.
more

Growth shows in the work itself — defended projects, revised goal plans, judgment visible in what they made. Each program adds to a personal "certificate card" of demonstrated qualities: not a formal credential yet, but built from real artifacts and human validation, aiming to become one.

→
5 · Return Next CTI program: reflect again, and the growth is visible.
more

A returning student's reflection-survey takes ~3 minutes, not 15 — the core was done once. Comparing reflections across programs is where the student sees their own arc, and where CTI sees what no single program can show (like habits actually forming, which takes months).

The three questions above everything

Every pilot asks a specific form of F1 — some also test F2. F3 is why we do it.

F1 · Framework Do the CTI 2.0 capabilities — Self-Directed Learner → Integrative Solver → Adaptive Builder, integrated by Symbiotic Thinking — describe what AI-age work actually requires? And does developing them produce human-valuable performance in real work?
F2 · Diffusion Can colleges, community partners, and industry adopt the framework in forms that keep it working — spreading with the substance intact, not as fashion?
F3 · North Star Does the middle of society participate and thrive in the AI age? Downstream of F1 and F2, on a longer clock than any pilot. It justifies the work; no single pilot is measured by it.

How the learning flows back up

Each pilot runs in short loops and captures what it learns in the team repo. The formal close of each run is the EDFG Phase 4 retrospective — hypotheses assessed against the criteria set before launch. Findings flow up the layers: pilot evidence accumulates on F1 and F2, cross-pilot patterns get promoted into the framework and methodology, and the foundational questions themselves get sharpened — or falsified. Reflection-surveys — our stance on measurement

We call them reflection-surveys, and the name is the design. Our working hypothesis: everyone has curiosity and agency — these are human qualities, and the environment just hasn't sparked them to grow. The reflection-survey helps a student be intentional about evaluating themselves: learning which conditions nurture these qualities in them, and which conditions impede their growth. The form is a Dojo activity — an adaptive, AI-guided conversation that explores the same territories with everyone but follows each person's own path — rather than a fixed questionnaire.

How this stays rigorous (click)

Two layers, kept separate on purpose. The student-facing layer is the reflection experience and the growing certificate card — built from real artifacts and human validation, never from scores. The research layer sits quietly inside: a few short validated question sets, asked with their exact original wording, analyzed only at cohort level. Students never see scale scores to perform against, and growth claims come from the work students produce — goal plans, reflections, defended projects — not from surveys alone.

And the build is research-gated (July 2026): the dojo starts from the validated question sets in the library below, evolves them into situation-based prompts carefully enough to keep what made them trustworthy, and fixes the conversation's shared anchor points and scoring guides before any student uses it — so an adaptive conversation still produces evidence we can rely on and compare across pilots.

Who arrives — the working set of dimensions

Refined July–August 2026 — draft for team reaction; nothing runs until a pilot's first run adopts it. The change from the earlier nine-item list: agency is now the umbrella, not a list item — and the list is six qualities a student grows, with the remaining former items folded in or reclassified below. Scope (Aug 2026): these six are the learner qualities of self-directed action — the things a student brings and grows. They are deliberately not a complete account of everything that matters; what sits outside them is placed in the conditions, evidence and outcome layers described below.

Agency — the whole we are growing. "The capacity to set a goal, reflect and act responsibly to effect change — acting rather than being acted upon" (OECD's definition of student agency; their words too: malleable and learnable, not a personality trait). Psychology's classic account (Bandura) gives agency four working parts — and the dimensions below carry them, so a student's agency is read from the dimensions together, never asked as a survey question.

  • Care — "Noticing what pulls you, letting it matter, and working to make it good." Definition locked. The foundation — the north star runs on it. Measured from episodes and behavior, because many students have real care they cannot yet name: someone with no answer to "what do you care about?" can still tell you about losing track of time. An intake that finds little is read as "care not yet surfaced," never "low care."
  • Self-knowledge — "Seeing yourself accurately enough to direct your own work — your cares, patterns, strengths and gaps" (the Know Yourself habit). Sharpened Aug 2026 to calibration about yourself: knowing whether something is inside or outside your reach, where you are likely to struggle, when you need another source or person. It does not cover judging whether a piece of work is any good — that is read from the work itself. Seen in behaviour and in the match between what a student says and what their evidence shows, never a questionnaire alone. Definition in progress.
  • Self-regulation — "Managing your attention, effort, emotions and routines so you stay with something you chose" (the Slow Down habit). Absorbs the separate habit-automaticity item. Two refinements from Aug 2026: emotions are named explicitly, and maturity is low-cost consistency plus the ability to redesign a routine that has stopped working — maximum automaticity is not the goal, because a routine that stops being examined can outlive its purpose. Changing the goal itself belongs to adaptability, not here. Definition in progress.
  • Initiative — "Thinking ahead, setting a goal, and taking the first step toward it." Definition locked. Agency's core, with two faces the reflection-dojo can tell apart: aiming (thinking ahead) and starting (making the move) — because students who start without aiming and students who aim without starting need opposite help.
  • Working with uncertainty — "Willingness to act even without all questions answered, cultivating the skill to find the right point to act." Renamed from "comfort with uncertainty" in Aug 2026: feeling the discomfort and proceeding anyway counts, so comfort described the wrong thing. The disposition behind knowing when to trust an AI's output. Definition in progress.
  • Adaptability — "Looking at your work as an opportunity to learn, learning precisely what the experience of the work is teaching, and identifying the level at which the lesson should be applied to make changes in the next iteration." Absorbs abstraction as a separate item, and owns revising the goal or strategy when evidence and conditions change. Definition in progress.

How they are read (Aug 2026): the target is whether a response fitted the situation, not how much of a quality a student displayed. Each of the six has a version that goes too far — care into an inability to let go, self-regulation into rigidity, initiative into steamrolling, adaptability into churn, working with uncertainty into skipping the checks. So the situations we use include moments where the capable move is to pause, ask for help, stop, hold a sound plan, or decline a risk — and those count as capable.

Reclassified, not dropped: belonging and the weeks-2–3 motivation check are conditions — things the environment must provide, measured at entry and watched so we can reach a disengaging student early, never part of a student's quality profile. Judgment shown in defended work is outcome evidence. Conversation quality is technique evidence. Reading what a task actually demands is exercised at UMPIRE's Understand step and shown in plans and build logs. AI fluency becomes the context where the six qualities show — deciding what the work is before you prompt (initiative), AI-free discipline where judgment is being built (self-regulation), knowing when to trust the tool (self-knowledge) — with each student's AI experience recorded at intake as background, like prior coursework.

The common structure — what every pilot does

The key points of our current thinking, held in common across every pilot. More stable than the details — but nothing is set in stone; it changes when we decide together to change it.

  1. Runs a common intake. One short reflection-survey plus a first sample of student work, the same in every pilot — completed once at a student's first CTI contact, with a ~3-minute refresh at each later program.
  2. Collects the same kinds of student work all the way through. Goal plans, written reflections, and the final work students present and defend — scored with the same shared scoring guides. This student work is our main evidence of growth.
  3. Designs for the full SDL → IS → AB loop. No pilot tests one capability without the others; what a pilot chooses is where the stress lands (table below).
  4. Completes its experiment design (EDFG) before launch. The learning question is the pilot's EDFG Section 2 — a core question plus 2–4 hypotheses, each with criteria that would prove it wrong; Sections 3–4 name the measures and data sources.
  5. Never uses intake results to select students. Measurement is for design and understanding, never admission.
  6. Captures findings to the team repo after every run, so evidence rolls up into the foundational questions.

Everything below this line — exact question sets, scoring guides, the final dimension list — is still being worked out, and pilot leads own those calls.

What this is for: building a course currently takes longer than the calendar allows, and each one starts close to scratch. That only works if the design method is written down, the repeatable parts are handed to AI, and a person sits at the gates where drift does the most damage.

The process — eleven steps, two human gates, one loop

Colour shows who is driving: frame & check AI drafts you edit, by hand the weekly loop. Open any step for what actually happens in it.

1 · Frame
Step 1 — Experiment design Work the EDFG first: the core question, two to four hypotheses, and the criteria that would prove each one wrong.
more

Sections 3–4 name the measures and the data sources. Nothing downstream is trustworthy if this is skipped — the alignment checks at steps 7 and 10 have nothing to check against.

Step 2 — Nurture-track qualities Name the qualities this course is trying to develop, and how each would show up in student work.
more

Closely tied to the EDFG rather than separate from it. Note that the qualities framework is itself still moving, so this step is currently a judgement call rather than a lookup.

2 · Shape
Step 3 — Course Gantt Lay the weeks out end to end, in a shared design doc, in the shape used for CST499.
more

This is the skeleton: what runs when, what has to be finished before what, and where the fixed dates sit — census, breaks, the last week that can carry new material.

Step 4 — Sprints, weeks, goals, activities Fill the skeleton with sprints, weeks, learning goals and activities, in the same doc. Every activity is one of two types: completion credit only, or completion credit plus an interview.
more

Two types rather than many is what makes the rest tractable — the grading model, the workload estimate and the Canvas build all follow from which type each activity is. Once this level of design is settled, the doc has done its job — from here on, the pages themselves are the working copy.

3 · Front door
Step 5 — Home page, drafted by chat Build the course home page by working it out in conversation with AI.
more

The home page comes first because it is where the whole course has to make sense to a student in one screen. If it cannot be written clearly, the design underneath it is not finished.

Step 6 — Gate: review and edit the page in an HTML editor The built page is opened in a visual HTML editor and revised directly — the page itself, not a copy — until you are comfortable with it.
more

This is the first of two human gates. Chat is good at producing a draft and bad at knowing when to stop; direct editing is where voice, judgement and the things only you know get put back in. Editing the page itself, instead of a doc copy, means nothing is lost between what you approved and what students see.

Step 7 — Check: EDFG and metrics Read the built page back against the EDFG, and confirm the right things are being tracked.
more

Two questions, not one: does what got built still serve the hypotheses, and will the data actually be captured when students move through it?

4 · Body
Step 8 — Sprints, one at a time, by chat Build the sprints in conversation with AI, one sprint per pass rather than all at once.
more

One at a time keeps each sprint reviewable and keeps the course shippable at any point — the first sprints can be live while the later ones are still being built.

Step 9 — Gate: review and edit in the HTML editor Same gate as step 6, applied to each sprint.
more

The second gate matters more than the first, because sprint content is where the volume is and where unreviewed AI drafting accumulates fastest.

Step 10 — Check: EDFG and metrics Alignment and measurement check again, now against the built sprints.
more

Same two questions as step 7. Running it twice is deliberate: the front door and the body drift apart if only one of them is checked.

5 · Run
Step 11 — Weekly iteration Every week the course is running, read what students actually submitted and what the metrics say, and change the design from it.
more

This is the step that makes the other ten worth doing, and it is the one with the least method behind it today. It is not the same as grading: grading judges the student, this judges the design. What it produces is a specific change to a later sprint, made while there is still time to make it.

⟲ The loop: step 11 feeds back into step 8 every week while the course runs, and back into step 1 at the end of the term, where the EDFG hypotheses are assessed against the criteria set before launch.

What every one of these skills has to carry

  • A closing checklist, but only of things that can be checked by looking. "Every activity page names its EDFG hypothesis" or "every module has one activity of each of the two types" works, because the artifact can be inspected. "Did you write plainly?" does not, because the answer will always be yes. Published guidance splits on this: Anthropic still recommends validation steps inside a skill, while Trail of Bits now says end-of-prompt self-checks make output worse and the check belongs somewhere it runs automatically.
  • The writing-quality checks belong to people, not to checklists. That is what the two direct-editing gates at steps 6 and 9 are for.
  • A trigger that names a moment, not a level. Skills are chosen by matching a description against what is happening right now. The seven levels are the map for a person; they are not the filenames.

Settled, and still open

Settled

One skill file per scale — module, sprint, course — with design, implementation and flow as sections inside each, rather than nine separate files whose descriptions would compete with each other.

Each level depends on the level below, and that dependency has to be visible in the skills themselves: a sprint skill assumes finished modules, a course skill assumes finished sprints.

Each of the three scales carries four sections, not three — design, implement, flow, and alignment. Flow asks whether the thing hangs together on its own; alignment asks whether it serves the experiment.

Weekly iteration from student submissions and metrics is step 11 and closes the loop — not an addendum.

Still open

The design direction and the build direction are reverses of each other. The process runs top-down, from the experiment design to the activity; the ladder composes bottom-up, from components to the course. Only one of those is drawn here.

Reuse across courses — what CST286, CST349 and CST499 share versus what belongs to one course — is not represented yet.

The translation pass from internal working language into student-facing language is a gate in the existing method and is missing from the eleven steps.

Which parts of this are skills, which are templates, and which are scripts. The Gantt doc and the HTML-editor gate look more like tooling than like skills.

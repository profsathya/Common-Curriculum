The CTI Plan, One Idea at a Time

A walkthrough for new team members. About 10 minutes. Each step is one idea; dig deeper where you want, skip where you don't. At the end, you'll be able to explain the whole plan in two minutes.

Step 1 of 10

Start with one student

Call her Maya. She's a CSUMB senior. She joins a CTI program — maybe Career Intelligence, maybe a course.

Here is her whole experience in one line: she arrives, she reflects on where she's starting, she learns and builds real things with AI as a partner, she demonstrates what she made, and when she joins her next CTI program, she returns and can see how far she's come.

Everything CTI does is a version of that journey. The rest of this walkthrough explains each piece.

Step 2 of 10

The reflection-survey

When Maya first arrives, she completes a short reflection-survey (about 12 minutes, once ever — later programs take 3 minutes).

Here's our belief, and it shapes everything: Maya doesn't lack curiosity or agency. These are human qualities everyone has. The environment just hasn't sparked them yet. The reflection-survey helps her be intentional about evaluating herself — learning which conditions nurture these qualities in her, and which conditions impede their growth.

Dig deeper: what's in it

Short, research-validated question sets on things like agency (do I direct my own path?), curiosity, comfort with uncertainty, and how she currently works with AI — plus a first small piece of real work. Each question set exists to track a specific part of CTI's framework. The full list, with links to every instrument, is on the landscape page's "How we measure" tab.

Step 3 of 10

What Maya practices: the loop

Every CTI program runs Maya through the same three-part loop:

  • Self-Directed Learner — she picks what matters to her and drives her own learning.
  • Integrative Solver — she connects ideas across areas to solve real problems.
  • Adaptive Builder — she builds things, and rebuilds when conditions change.

Holding the loop together is Symbiotic Thinking: working with AI while keeping your own judgment — knowing when to trust it, when to check it, when to override it.

One rule we never break: every program runs the whole loop. Programs differ only in where they push hardest.

Dig deeper: why the whole loop

Because the parts feed each other. Self-direction without building is planning forever; building without self-direction is following instructions. A program that "just teaches one part" would be testing something CTI doesn't believe in.

Step 4 of 10

The work is the evidence

How do we know Maya grew? Not mainly from surveys. From what she makes: her goal plans, her written reflections, and the final work she presents and defends.

Every program collects these same kinds of work and scores them with the same shared scoring guides. The reflection-survey helps us read the work — it never replaces it.

Over time, Maya's demonstrated qualities build into a personal card she can use with her resume. It's not a formal credential yet — but it's built from real artifacts and real human judgment, which is what could make it one.

Step 5 of 10

We run six versions of this journey

Each one is a pilot — a small 4–8 week experiment with a different group of people:

  • Career Intelligence — graduating students learning to run their own job search.
  • Mid-Career Pilot (with De Anza College) — professionals whose jobs AI is reshaping.
  • SOSE-SWE Micro-Internships — students doing short real work for real employers.
  • Salinas Golden Ticket (with Digital NEST + Hartnell) — opportunity youth, in person, community-rooted.
  • CST286 and CST349 — two CSUMB courses running the full journey inside regular classes.

Same journey, same loop, same kinds of evidence — different people, different places. That's on purpose, and the next step explains why.

Step 6 of 10

Why so many versions? The three questions

CTI is a research effort. Three questions sit above everything:

  • F1 — Does the framework work? Do the loop and Symbiotic Thinking describe what AI-age work really requires — and does developing them make people genuinely valuable?
  • F2 — Does it spread? Can colleges, community groups, and employers run it themselves — without it turning into an empty fashion?
  • F3 — The North Star: does the middle of society thrive in the AI age? This is why we do it. No single pilot is measured by it.

Each pilot asks a specific, smaller form of F1 (and some test F2). Different people and places means: if the framework only works for one group, we find out.

Step 7 of 10

How we keep ourselves honest

Before any pilot launches, its lead writes down — in a shared format called the EDFG — three things: the question this pilot asks, the 2–4 claims it's testing, and what result would prove each claim wrong.

That last part matters most. Writing down in advance what failure looks like is what makes this research instead of wishful thinking.

After each run, the lead records what actually happened, against those claims. Those findings roll up into the three big questions — sharpening them, or proving parts of them wrong.

Dig deeper: where findings live

Everything goes into the shared team repo — the same place this page's source lives. If it's not in the repo, the team can't learn from it. The Team Guidebook shows exactly how to put things there.

Step 8 of 10

Two environments we're building

The pilots run inside shared spaces:

  • The Dojo — live now. An AI coach that pushes back, so students practice judgment, not just prompting. They set it up once and carry it into any AI tool.
  • The Commons — taking shape. A shared space where students across all programs exchange work and push each other's thinking. Learning together, not alone.

Step 9 of 10

Where you come in

If you lead a pilot, your part is five things: check your pilot's card on the landscape page, complete your EDFG, leave room in week one for the shared reflection-survey, collect the three kinds of student work, and close each run in the repo. The landscape page's "Your part" tab walks through each.

Everyone: do your weekly stand-up (tell Athena "I want to do my weekly stand-up"), and use the Team Guidebook whenever you're unsure how to get something into the shared repo. Five minutes of setup, then it's easy.

Step 10 of 10

Now say it back — the two-minute version

You're done when you can explain the plan to someone else. Here's the skeleton — try saying it in your own words, out loud or to Athena:

"CTI takes people through one journey: arrive, reflect, build real things with AI, demonstrate, return and see your growth. Every program runs the same capability loop and collects the same kinds of student work as evidence. We run six pilots of this journey with very different groups, because we're testing three big questions: does the framework work, does it spread, and — long term — does the middle of society thrive in the AI age. Every pilot writes down in advance what would prove it wrong. What we learn flows back and sharpens the questions."

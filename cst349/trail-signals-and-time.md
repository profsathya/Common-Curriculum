## Box 3 - Before you could impress that hiring manager, how did a human actually see your work?

**1. Write your first answer: how did you get the interview?**
- Applying online through job boards
- An internship at that organization that turned into a job
- A referral or conversation with an alum, mentor, faculty member, or someone working in the field
- Work that real people use (a campus or local organization, an open-source project, a game jam, a lab)

Describe in a line or two how your name and your work reached a person who makes hiring decisions.

**2. See what a recent study found.**
- Quick guess: A 2026 study of 65 million career histories found that about 70% of students at selective public universities did an internship. What do you think the share was at less-selective public universities? (About 65% · **About 47%** · About 25%)
- **What the study found:** An internship helps, and there are other ways to gain credible experience. If a formal internship is hard to get, you can build credible experience in other ways:
  - contribute to an open-source project,
  - do a project for a client or a community organization, or
  - take a short micro-internship.
- **For your first answer** (by the option picked):
  - Applying online: Online applications are crowded: hiring data from 2026 shows more than 300 applications for each hire. Applying online can be part of your plan, and it works better alongside a second way for a person to see your work.
  - Internship: An internship is a strong path, because large employers hire about half of their interns. Fewer than half of students at less-selective public universities get an internship, so it helps to plan a second path alongside it.
  - Referral or conversation: This path helps your work reach a person. Someone who knows how you think can bring your work to a hiring manager directly, while online applications wait in a queue of more than 300.
  - Work that real people use: This path gives you proof that other people can check. When real users or partners depend on your work, you gain experience with real consequences and people who can speak for you.
- **What this study cannot tell you:** Its graduate records end in 2023, before AI tools were widely used, and it shows a pattern without proving that internships caused the better outcomes.
- Expander: *The Internship Imperative* (Burning Glass Institute, Nov 2026; 65 million career histories, graduates 2016 - 2023); 9.9 entry-level job ads per internship ad; small and mid-sized firms offer about 13% of internships; interns about half as likely to be underemployed, and large employers hire about half of their interns; Ashby Talent Trends (May 2026): more than 300 applications per hire, 60% of its startup customers use AI in hiring software; other ways to gain experience from *The 'Consequence' Gap* (pp. 24 - 25).

**The question bank** (the interviewer asks from it in the page's own words, fitted to the student's chain: in the check-in at most one question per idea, the easier one, in the order the page gives)

1. **Work that runs is not enough.** Work that runs does not, by itself, show how well someone can check it, explain a choice and change it.
   - Easier: A demo runs. Identify one check that would make you trust it more, and explain why.
   - Harder: Two fixes pass the same tests. What would you compare before choosing between them, and what change in the requirements might reverse your choice?
   - What to listen for (private, for the interviewer and the summary): a concrete check, and a choice the student can defend; working output alone does not settle it.
2. **A person has to see your work.** There is more than one path to that, and a plan needs a second one.
   - Easier: You have a small, useful piece of work and no internship. Identify one person or community that could realistically look at it, and say how you would get it to them.
   - Harder: The contact you were counting on cannot reply for a month. Give a second path that keeps the useful part of your plan and does not depend on knowing another insider.
   - What to listen for (private, for the interviewer and the summary): a reachable audience and a piece of work to show; no claim that it guarantees a hiring outcome.
3. **Judgment comes from practice and feedback.** It grows from work on something real or messy, from checking what AI produced, and from someone questioning your choices.
   - Easier: An AI draft works on the example you gave it. What would you try yourself before trusting it, and what would you ask a reviewer?
   - Harder: A reviewer disagrees with a choice you made. What evidence could change your mind, and how would you check both suggestions in the time you have?
   - What to listen for (private, for the interviewer and the summary): testing, asking for specific feedback, and keeping responsibility for the decision.
4. **A chain.** Each step of your plan leads to the next, from this week to Day 1.
   - Easier: Point to one thing you will do this week, and explain which later step it supports.
   - Harder: Your time drops by half and your contact cancels. Change one activity in your plan and keep a believable link to Day 1.
   - What to listen for (private, for the interviewer and the summary): a reason that connects one step to the next, an action someone could see, a realistic amount of time, and uncertainty said out loud.
5. **A study has limits.** For each finding, there is something it cannot tell you about your own case.
   - Easier: A study finds that people who had an internship did better afterwards. Does it show that an internship guarantees your outcome? Explain.
   - Harder: Two studies give different explanations for why fewer junior people are being hired. What plan stays sensible under both, and what would you want to find out locally?
   - What to listen for (private, for the interviewer and the summary): a pattern across many people is not a forecast for one person; one thing going with another is not the same as causing it; a plan that still holds when the cause is uncertain.

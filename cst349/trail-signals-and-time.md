## Box 2 - What convinced the hiring manager to pick you over other finalists?

**1. Write your first answer.** Go back to the week the hiring team chose you over the other candidates. What do you think mattered most to them?
- Several working projects or demos I built
- Solving technical problems in the interview without AI help
- Improving a system that had problems, and explaining the tradeoffs I made
- Understanding the users and the problem the team is solving

Write a line or two about what you showed that the other candidates did not.

**2. See what a recent study found.**
- Quick guess: A security company (Veracode) tested code written by AI tools. More than 95% of it ran without errors. About how much of it also passed the security checks? (About 90% · **About 55%** · About 25%)
- After the guess: More than 95% of the AI-written code ran, and only about 55% of it was secure. The security number has stayed about the same from year to year.
- **What the study found:** Many applicants can now show working code. Employers look for people who can check it and explain it. Employers in this study said they look for people who can:
  - find what is wrong in AI-written work,
  - explain why they chose one approach over another, and
  - say what they would change if the requirements changed.
- **For your first answer** (one of these, by the option picked):
  - Working projects: Your projects stand out when you can show what you checked and fixed, and explain why your design was a good choice.
  - Solving problems without AI: This still matters: in a 2026 survey, 62% of engineering leaders said they do not allow AI in technical interviews. Some teams also ask candidates to work through a messy, realistic problem, so it helps to practice both.
  - Improving a system: This is close to what employers in the study described: checking work that has problems, working within real limits, and explaining what you would change if the requirements changed.
  - Understanding users: Understanding the users and the problem helps you decide what to build, which employers in the study valued. Pair it with technical skill you can show without AI, because 62% of engineering leaders in a 2026 survey do not allow AI in technical interviews.
- **For your path** and **What this study cannot tell you:** one short note each, by path (see "Path notes" below).
- Expander, "See the numbers and where they come from": *The 'Consequence' Gap* (Burning Glass Institute and Break Through Tech, Nov 2026; 1.03 million job ads, 54 practitioners at 46 organizations); code review in job ads 7.8% (2020) to 18.5% (June 2026), software testing 17.9%; Karat survey (Jan 2026, 400 engineering leaders): 71% say skill is harder to assess, 62% do not allow AI in interviews; the report's six capabilities (pp. 22 - 23); Veracode figures on p. 13.

**3. Update your answer.** Rewrite or keep your answer about what convinced the hiring manager in your path. Include one thing you could do without AI, and one thing you checked or one tradeoff you can explain.

## Box 3 - Before you could impress that hiring manager, how did a human actually see your work?

**1. Write your first answer: how did you get the interview?**
- Applying online through job boards
- An internship at that organization that turned into a job
- A referral or conversation with an alum, mentor, faculty member, or someone working in the field
- Work that real people use (a campus or local organization, an open-source project, a game jam, a lab)

Describe in a line or two how your name and your work reached a person who makes hiring decisions.

**2. See what a recent study found.**
- Quick guess: A 2026 study of 65 million career histories found that about 70% of students at selective public universities did an internship. What do you think the share was at less-selective public universities? (About 65% · **About 47%** · About 25%)
- **What the study found:** An internship helps, and there are other ways to gain credible experience. If a formal internship is hard to get, you can build credible experience in other ways:
  - contribute to an open-source project,
  - do a project for a client or a community organization, or
  - take a short micro-internship.
- **For your first answer** (by the option picked):
  - Applying online: Online applications are crowded: hiring data from 2026 shows more than 300 applications for each hire. Applying online can be part of your plan, and it works better alongside a second way for a person to see your work.
  - Internship: An internship is a strong path, because large employers hire about half of their interns. Fewer than half of students at less-selective public universities get an internship, so it helps to plan a second path alongside it.
  - Referral or conversation: This path helps your work reach a person. Someone who knows how you think can bring your work to a hiring manager directly, while online applications wait in a queue of more than 300.
  - Work that real people use: This path gives you proof that other people can check. When real users or partners depend on your work, you gain experience with real consequences and people who can speak for you.
- **What this study cannot tell you:** Its graduate records end in 2023, before AI tools were widely used, and it shows a pattern without proving that internships caused the better outcomes.
- Expander: *The Internship Imperative* (Burning Glass Institute, Nov 2026; 65 million career histories, graduates 2016 - 2023); 9.9 entry-level job ads per internship ad; small and mid-sized firms offer about 13% of internships; interns about half as likely to be underemployed, and large employers hire about half of their interns; Ashby Talent Trends (May 2026): more than 300 applications per hire, 60% of its startup customers use AI in hiring software; other ways to gain experience from *The 'Consequence' Gap* (pp. 24 - 25).

**3. Update your answer.** Describe your plan for getting a person to see your work, given how far out your Day 1 is. Include a second path you would use if online applications or internships are crowded.

## Box 4 - How did you actually build that portfolio, and what help & resources did you use?

**1. Write your first answer: what went into your work?** Select every way you picture building that body of work:
- Solo projects I started from scratch
- Maintaining or extending an existing codebase or a messy dataset
- Learning a second field (health, finance, operations, education, players)
- Using AI to produce fast drafts and prototypes
- Checking and questioning what AI produced (security, cost, unusual cases)
- Feedback from peers, mentors, alumni, or real users

Briefly describe what you built or improved, and how AI, courses, peers or mentors were part of how you worked.

**2. See what a recent study found.**
- Quick guess: A Stanford study of payroll records found that companies have been hiring fewer 22 to 25 year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI. Which kind of work was affected most? (**Work that follows written procedures you can learn from a textbook or tutorial** · Work that depends on judgment learned through experience · All kinds of work equally)
- After the guess: Employment fell among young workers in jobs built on written, textbook knowledge. It rose among experienced workers in jobs built on judgment learned through practice and mentorship.
- **What the study found:** Judgment that comes from real practice and feedback keeps its value. You build this kind of judgment by working on real problems and getting feedback from other people.
- **For your first answer** (at most two of these, by what was selected):
  - You selected fast AI drafts without checking what AI produced. Adding the checking step gives you work you can explain and stand behind.
  - Consider adding work on an existing codebase or a messy dataset, because it lets you practice fitting into what is already there.
  - Consider adding feedback from peers, mentors, alumni or real users, because judgment grows faster when other people question your choices.
  - Consider where a second field (health, finance, education, operations or players) could enter your work, so that you practice working with real-world limits.
- **What this study cannot tell you:** The authors say their results describe a pattern and do not prove that AI caused it. A St. Louis Fed study (June 2026) found that the overall drop in job openings explains more of the weaker job market for young workers than AI does.
- Expander: Stanford *Canaries in the Coal Mine?* (revised Aug 2026; payroll records, 730+ occupations; about a 19% gap for ages 22 - 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs, through slower hiring; codified and tacit knowledge); St. Louis Fed (June 2026); Narayanan and Kapoor (June 2026) on deciding, writing and being accountable.

**3. Update your answer.** Describe the existing or messy problem (or the second field) you will build around, how you will check AI-assisted work, and whose feedback you will ask for.

## Box 5 - What do you start doing in the next month - and the next 7 days?

**From your chain to your calendar:** your three answers describe what needs to be true by Day 1. In this box you turn them into hours.

(The page shows the student's three answers from Boxes 2, 3 and 4 here.)

**1. Divide your weekly hours for the next 30 days.** Set the hours per week you can realistically give outside your required coursework (4 to 20). Then divide all of them across the six kinds of preparation. The page shows the monthly total, for example "10 hours a week is about 40 hours over the next 30 days."

**How to decide:** each kind of preparation supports one of your three answers. Ask yourself which answer needs the most work, and give that one the most hours.

Supports your Box 2 answer: what convinced the hiring manager
- **Fundamentals without AI** - Practice solving problems in your main language, tool or engine without AI help, because many technical interviews do not allow AI.
- **Checking and improving work** - Find and fix problems in work that already exists, including work AI helped you write (review, test, secure, speed up).
- **Writing down decisions** - Keep short notes on why you made each choice, so that you can explain your work in an interview.

Supports your Box 3 answer: how a person saw your work
- **People and real-world work** - Internships, campus, community or open-source work, conversations with alumni, and reaching out to people in your field.

Supports your Box 4 answer: how you built your work
- **Building new projects** - Start a new project of your own (an app, a small game, a notebook, a lab script).
- **Learning the field** - Learn about the people and the setting your target job serves (the users, the business, the threats, or the players).

Expander, "See one example of dividing 10 hours": A student who is about a year from Day 1 might divide 10 hours a week like this:
- 5 hours for the Box 2 answer: 2 on fundamentals without AI, 2 on checking and improving work, and 1 on writing down decisions, because that answer depends on explaining choices.
- 2 hours for the Box 3 answer: 2 on people and real-world work, because that answer depends on a person seeing the work.
- 3 hours for the Box 4 answer: 2 on building a new project and 1 on learning the field, because that answer depends on work in a real setting.

Your split follows your own three answers, so it may look different.

**2. Identify one step you will finish in the next 7 days.** Write one step that is small enough to finish in the next 7 days and that starts your chain. Make it specific enough that a classmate or mentor could confirm it happened. For example: you looked through a specific repository or dataset, started your decision notes, messaged a specific person, or tested your project with a real user.

What Would I Do Anyway

Due Friday 4 September, 11:59 p.m. · about 30 minutes

Where this is going

In two to three months, Prof. Sathya may send an email about you to one of his industry contacts. [slot — Sathya’s exact wording on the promise goes here] He has the final say, and he is open to doing this for as many of you as he can. “May” means there is a bar, and meeting it is yours to do.

Next week you draft that email yourself. Everything you do this week is the raw material for it: what the market is asking for gives the email something specific to point at, what you would be doing anyway gives it a story worth reading, and the gap you find gives it the honest line about what you are building next.

Would Prof. Sathya actually send this? Would his contact actually be spurred to action by it?

The second question is the harder one, and it is the reason this week starts with job postings. A busy person acts when they can see a specific opportunity and a specific reason you fit it. Neither is something you can write without having read the market first.

It also has to be short enough to read in one go and sharp enough to hold attention from the first line — under 150 words [slot — length is Sathya’s call]. That is a high bar, and holding yourself to it is the point.

This page feeds the story in the middle of the email — the part a contact remembers after they have closed it.

Purpose

Work out what you would be doing even if nobody asked you to, so the market you aim at is one worth hitting. This is the question that decides whether the effort you put in over the next few years is effort you can actually sustain.

You come out of this with several concrete moments where the work stopped feeling like work, and an honest attempt at what runs through them.

What you are building on

From Wednesday: your market read. Have it open for the last box — not for the first three. You want to answer this without checking whether the answer is convenient.

Assumed: nothing. You do not need to have decided anything about your career to answer this. If anything, having decided makes it harder.

Why this is not the soft part of the week

A question about purpose, sitting in the middle of a week about the job market, looks like the part you can skip. It is the opposite. It is the part that makes the rest work, and here is the argument.

AI is already good at the surface layer of most technical work — the first draft, the standard implementation, the obvious answer, the version that would have taken you an afternoon. What is left is the layer underneath: the problem you keep turning over for three weeks, the detail nobody asked you to fix, the question you follow past the point where it stopped being assigned. Think about the last time you fixed something nobody had asked you to fix. Nobody made you do that, and nobody does it on work they do not care about.

So caring is not a nice extra sitting on top of skill. It is what buys you the depth, and the depth is what makes the work yours rather than something anyone could have generated. That is why this question sits between the market questions instead of somewhere after them: the market tells you where the openings are, and this tells you which of them are worth your effort. Skip it and you end up optimising for a snapshot — aiming very precisely at whatever employers happened to be asking for in the week you looked.

There is a second reason, and it is the email. A contact reads a lot of messages about capable people. What they remember is the one that sounded like a person rather than a profile — and that only comes from something the student actually cares about.

What to work out

What would my work purpose be? What would you have to be doing so that it never quite feels like you are working? Answer it honestly, not impressively.

That is the whole question. The four boxes below are how you get at it without landing on a slogan — you collect the evidence first, take one honest run at what it means, and then argue with yourself.

Two ways to work on what you write

Use one, or both. Under every box there is a Get AI feedback button that reads what you actually wrote against what a strong answer to that question looks like, and a Check it yourself list with the same questions, for you to run on your own. They go at the same thing from two directions and they end in the same box.

Working through the questions yourself is where a lot of the thinking happens, and it is worth doing for its own sake. Having something push back on what you just wrote is a different skill, and that is worth practising too. If you want both, answer first, run the check yourself, then press the button and see what it caught that you did not.

Your writing is saved in this browser as you type, so you can close the page and come back. Nothing is sent anywhere until you press the button or copy your summary.

Six times you lost track of the hour. They do not all have to be about computing, or about work, and school counts. For each one: what were you doing, who was it for, and what kept you going when it got tedious? Go for concrete moments, not adjectives — “I like solving hard problems” is true of almost everyone and tells you nothing you can use.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Are these episodes you could date, or descriptions of the kind of person you are?
  • Did you get to six? The obvious ones run out around three, and that is where the useful ones start.
  • Did any of them get picked because they sound employable? Those are the ones to check.
  • For each: what kept you going when it got tedious? That answer is usually the real content.
In one or two sentences, what do those moments have in common? This box is allowed to be the presentable answer — the one you would give in an interview. Write it, and leave it here.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Would this fit half the people in the room? That is fine for this box.
  • Did you write it down rather than skipping ahead? Box 3 needs something to argue with.
Read your six moments again as if they belonged to someone else. What is actually going on in them that box 2 missed? If the moments do not have anything in common, write that instead, and write which two are furthest apart — that is a real finding and it is worth more than a tidy sentence you do not believe.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Does this disagree with box 2 anywhere? If not, you have written box 2 again in longer words.
  • Is it something you would be slightly reluctant to say out loud? That is usually the sign it is yours.
  • If you went for the two-furthest-apart answer: what does having both of them tell you?
Now open Wednesday’s work. Look at the two things employers are asking for that you found. Does any of this sit inside them, outside them, or partly both? Any of the three is a real answer, and Tuesday is where you do something about it.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Did you force an overlap because an overlap seemed like the right answer?
  • If it sits outside: is the market asking somewhere you have not looked, or is this a thing you keep for yourself and aim at something next to it?
  • Could a contact tell, from this, the difference between someone aimed at their world and someone aiming at any job?

Your own AI DojoFor when this page’s questions ran out and you are still working.

The condition. You have answered the boxes, run the feedback, and you are still circling something. A conversation is better than a box for that, and your own Dojo keeps the context for the rest of the semester.

I am working out what kind of work I would do even if nobody asked me to. Interview me about it — one question at a time, and wait for my answer before you ask the next one.

Start from concrete moments rather than adjectives: ask me about times I lost track of the hour, what I was actually doing, who it was for, and what kept me going when it got tedious. When an answer of mine sounds like something I would put in a cover letter, say so and ask again. Do not summarise me until I have given you at least six real moments. Then play back what you heard, and I will tell you where you are wrong.

Stay past the first ten minutes, and correct it when it gets you wrong. If the playback is off, say so and keep going until it matches what you meant. That correcting is the work — you are the one who knows.

What done and good look like

This one is not graded, and it still matters more than most graded things this month, because the growth dimensions you set in Sprint 1 are supposed to come out of it.

Done is several concrete moments and an honest attempt at what runs through them.

Good is where the second pass disagreed with the first. If what you ended up writing is what you would have written before you started, you stopped at the presentable answer.

Before you finish

Two questions, worth answering after the work.

What did you write down and then decide not to say out loud — and what made it feel unsayable?

If a contact of Prof. Sathya’s read one of your six moments, which one would make them want to meet you? What is in that one that is not in the others?

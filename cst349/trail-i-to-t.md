# What shape are you, on the evidence?

A trail to explore · Stop 1 takes about 45 minutes · The other stops take 15 to 25 minutes each, in any order

This trail is here to help you plan your growth. Nothing on it is graded, and what you type stays in your browser.

Depth in computing is where your professional value starts. That value grows when you can take your depth into a room with a nurse, a teacher or a product manager, understand what they need, and build the right thing with them.

A common picture for this is a letter. The vertical stem of a T is your depth: your skill in computing, together with your understanding of the systems where computing is used, such as health care, education or transportation. The horizontal bar of the T is your ability to communicate and work with people across disciplines. An I has a stem and no bar yet.

On this trail you find out what shape your own experiences support today, and then you choose one thing to build next.

## Stop 1 · Place yourself

**Why this stop.** A growth plan works when it starts from where you are today, so the first stop is to find that out from your own experience.

**After this stop you can** describe your current shape and point to the experience behind each part of it.

### Step 1. Draw the shape you believe you have

Set the two sliders to match how you see yourself today. The page draws your shape and keeps it for step 3.

- **Stem height.** How deep is your computing skill, and how well do you understand one system where computing is used?
- **Bar width.** How well do you communicate and work with people outside computing?

Page note: two sliders, each 0 to 100. The drawing updates as they move.

### Step 2. Find the evidence, one experience at a time

**Why this is worth learning.** A software interview includes more than coding problems. Amazon's own interview guide says: "Our interviews are rooted in behavioral-based questions." These questions ask about a situation you have already faced and how you handled it, because what you did is stronger evidence than what you say you would do. The same guide says that Amazon avoids brain teasers, and it recommends the STAR method for answering.

Behavioral questions usually begin with "Tell me about a time when…". Here are two places to read real ones:

- [Amazon's interviewing guide](https://amazonstores.turtl.co/story/interviewing-at-amazon-guide/page/5) lists questions about choosing between several possible solutions, taking a risk or failing, taking the lead on a project, motivating a group, and using data to decide.
- [Indeed's guide to the STAR technique](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/interviewing/how-to-use-the-star-interview-response-technique) lists fifteen questions, including ones about working with other departments, persuading someone, disagreeing with your manager and delivering bad news.

Read the two lists. How many of the questions could you answer with code alone?

Note for Sathya: both sources were read on 3 Oct 2026. I described the questions in my own words and kept one short quote from Amazon. If you would rather show a few of the questions word for word, tell me which.

**The STAR method** is a way to answer these questions so that the listener hears the whole story:

- **Situation.** Where you were and what was going on.
- **Task.** What you were responsible for.
- **Action.** What you did yourself, step by step.
- **Result.** What changed because of your action, including something another person could confirm.

In the second video one student answers the same question three times. Notice what she adds each time, because you will use the same three levels to mark your own answers. The two videos suggest different lengths for an answer. Aim for about two minutes when you say it out loud.

**The three levels**

| Level | What the answer has | In the video |
|---|---|---|
| **Full** | All four STAR parts. The action is something you did, told specifically enough that a listener can picture it. The result is something another person could confirm. | Third attempt: she set an alarm every 30 minutes, worked through 50 cases at a time, submitted early, and her supervisor commended the work. |
| **Partial** | A real situation of your own, with one part still general. Most often it is the action or the result. | Second attempt: the task is clear, and the action is "some hard work". |
| **Not yet** | You do not have a situation for this part yet. | Leave the box empty and choose "Not yet". |

**Answer the eight questions.** Each question belongs to one part of the T, and the questions are modeled on the kinds of questions in the two lists above. Answer with a real situation of your own from a class, a project, a job, a club or your family. Write the answer in STAR form, then mark it Full, Partial or Not yet using the table above.

*The bar*

1. **Communication.** Tell me about a time you explained technical work to someone outside computing. What did they do with what you told them?
2. **Teamwork.** Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision your team made. What did you do, and what happened to the team's work?
3. **Working with another discipline.** Tell me about a time you worked with someone from another field, such as business, biology, design or health care, to get something done.
4. **Bringing a different perspective.** Tell me about a time you saw a problem differently from the rest of a group, and it changed what the group did.
5. **Ethical decision making.** Tell me about a time you thought about how your work or your choice would affect other people, and it changed what you did.
6. **Mentoring and professional relationships.** Tell me about a time you built a working relationship with someone further along than you. What came of it?

*The stem*

7. **Depth in computing.** Tell me about a project you are proud of. What was the hardest technical problem in it, how did you solve it, and where could someone see the work?
8. **Understanding a system.** Tell me about a time you learned how a field that uses computing works, such as health care, education, transportation or agriculture, well enough to talk with someone who works in it.

Page note: each question has one answer box with the four STAR labels as light prompts, a **Get AI feedback** button, a collapsed **Check it yourself** list, and a three-way choice: Full · Partial · Not yet.

**Get AI feedback (optional).** Each answer box has a button. The AI tells you which of the four STAR parts it can see in what you wrote, and it asks you one question. You decide the mark.

**Check it yourself.**

- Can a listener tell where you were and what you were responsible for?
- Does the action say what you did yourself, in steps someone could picture?
- Does the result include something another person could confirm?

**Practice out loud (recommended).** We recommend that you say your answers to your AI Dojo, to a partner, or to both, and ask them to challenge you. An answer that has been questioned is easier to mark.

*With your Dojo.* Paste this:

> Act as an interviewer for an entry-level software role. Ask me this behavioral question: "[paste one of the eight questions]". After I answer, tell me which of the four STAR parts (Situation, Task, Action, Result) you heard and which you did not. If my action is general, ask "What did you do, yourself?". If my result is general, ask "How do you know it worked, and who could confirm it?". Keep asking until I give specifics or I say I do not have them. Do not write or improve the answer for me.

*With a partner.* Your partner asks the question, listens, and then asks these three follow-ups:

- What did you do, yourself?
- How do you know it worked?
- Who could confirm that?

### Step 3. See the shape your evidence draws

The page now draws a second shape from your marks alone and places it beside the shape from step 1.

- Each bar question marked Full adds one full section of the bar. Partial adds half a section. Not yet adds nothing.
- Question 7 fills the lower half of the stem and question 8 fills the upper half, in the same way.

Compare the two drawings. What do you notice between them?

**Your note.** What you notice between the shape you drew and the shape your evidence drew.

The sections that are not drawn yet show where the next stops can add the most. Stop 6 turns one of them into an action for the next two weeks.

## Stop 2 · Why one deep skill is half the picture

**Why this stop.** Your stem is real value. This stop shows what employers report about the skills around it, so that you can decide where your next hour of effort adds the most.

**After this stop you can** set a target for your own T and explain it with data.

**Watch.** [T-shaped professional](https://youtu.be/2ExIRk7nJ9M), Prof. Sathya, 12:11 · [Slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1ojbtaW0Mhk4NjcPqg0osfdpPhb5OBTQsS6xDv4f-1s8/edit?usp=sharing) · [PlayPosit version](https://www.wevideo.com/interactive/player_v2?type=share&bulb_id=230132), no points

**Read the data.** In 2024 the National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) asked 20,482 students to rate their own proficiency. It asked 237 employers to rate recent graduates on the same skills. The table shows the share who answered "very" or "extremely" proficient.

| Skill | Students rating themselves | Employers rating recent graduates |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | 78.1% | 53.5% |
| Critical thinking | 80.8% | 55.9% |
| Professionalism | 79.7% | 50.3% |
| Leadership | 66.0% | 31.0% |
| Teamwork | 83.5% | 81.5% |
| Technology | 60.5% | 72.0% |

Source: NACE, [The gap in perceptions of new grads' competency proficiency](https://naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/the-gap-in-perceptions-of-new-grads-competency-proficiency-and-resources-to-shrink-it), January 2025.

Find the one row where employers rated graduates higher than students rated themselves. Then find the row with the largest difference in the other direction. What do the two rows together suggest about where another hour of your effort would add the most?

**Set your target.** The stem and the bar together make 100% of your T. Set where you are today and where you want to be when you graduate.

| Bar | Stem | What this mix fits |
|---|---|---|
| 40–50% | 50–60% | Most development and management roles |
| 30–40% | 60–70% | A specialist in one area, often with graduate study |
| 60–70% | 30–40% | Management and leadership, built on strong expertise |

**Case to explore.** A developer ships a feature that works exactly as the ticket described. The product manager, the designer and the customer each expected something different. Take this case to your Dojo and work out who in that room could have caught the difference, and what they would have needed to ask.

**Ask your Dojo.**

> I am a computer science student. Here is a case: a developer shipped a feature that matched the ticket, and the product manager, the designer and the customer each expected something different. Ask me questions, one at a time, that help me work out what the developer could have asked before building, and which skills outside coding those questions use. Do not give me the answer.

> Here is my current T: [bar %] bar and [stem %] stem, and my target is [bar %] and [stem %]. Ask me what evidence I have for the current numbers, then ask what one experience in the next month would move me toward the target.

## Stop 3 · Collect the evidence as you go

**Why this stop.** In stop 1 some boxes were hard to fill because the experience was not written down anywhere. Folio thinking is the habit of reflecting on your work each week, so that the evidence is ready when someone asks for it.

**After this stop you can** keep a weekly record that turns your work into STAR situations.

**Watch.**

- [Motivation for folio thinking](https://youtu.be/TMniFCzyGQQ), Prof. Sathya, 5:47 · [Slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/17EGQas8bxQ0IfzOLewmlHu6-TM4PCfi_FsMGoJ6RJus/edit?usp=sharing) · [PlayPosit version](https://www.wevideo.com/interactive/player_v2?type=share&bulb_id=230128)
- [Introduction to folio thinking](https://youtu.be/eD_SvAbLZ3A), Prof. Sathya, 8:19 · [Slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1hQQIznNIK5iD_3DjKpiSh_LGIPwxF19yA9AJ5_SDTdI/edit?usp=sharing) · [PlayPosit version](https://www.wevideo.com/interactive/player_v2?type=share&bulb_id=230130)

**Try the habit once.** Set aside 15 minutes. Write down one thing you worked on this week, what was hard about it, what you did, and what came of it. You have just written the first draft of a STAR answer.

With the help of AI, almost anyone can now produce a project that runs. So the evidence a listener trusts most is your account of the work: why you made each decision, what you tried that did not work, and what you would change. Add those to your record.

Note for Sathya: the paragraph above is new. The old videos treat a GitHub page or a published app as evidence on its own, which was recorded before AI tools could build one. Please edit or cut.

**Optional background.** [Understand our thinking process](https://youtu.be/5GfaityIDu0), Prof. Sathya, 8:06, with a short [animation on how practice rewires the brain](https://youtu.be/ELpfYCZa87g), 2:03. The video explains fast and slow thinking. Some of the research behind it is debated, as the video says.

**Case to explore.** Two students have the same GPA and built the same class project. One can show a weekly record of decisions and results. The other has the finished project only. Ask your Dojo to play a recruiter who has ten minutes with each of them.

**Ask your Dojo.**

> Act as a recruiter with ten minutes. I will describe one project from my portfolio. Ask me why I made two of the decisions in it and what I would change now. Then tell me which of my answers gave you evidence of my own thinking and which did not.

> I want to start a weekly 15-minute reflection on my work. Ask me four questions about this week, one at a time, that would help me turn one piece of work into a Situation, Task, Action and Result. Keep my wording when you summarize.

## Stop 4 · How others see you

**Why this stop.** The bar of the T is built with other people, so it helps to know how your strengths look from their side.

**After this stop you can** identify three qualities people associate with you, describe how each could come across differently, and choose one action for each.

**Watch.** [Brand YOU](https://youtu.be/UTNQkaXBcic), Prof. Sathya, 9:26 · [Slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1Q92IWOZdNy_fHH3gWzOI_mipUyB0Fgw1Qie_FMIoR5c/edit?usp=sharing) · [PlayPosit version](https://www.wevideo.com/interactive/player_v2?type=share&bulb_id=230131)

**Work through the three questions.**

1. **Brand YOU.** Identify the top three qualities your closest friends or teammates would associate with you. Ask one of them if you can.
2. **The flip side.** Describe how each quality could come across differently to someone who does not know you yet. For example, a person who is passionate can come across as someone who does not take feedback.
3. **Action.** Write one precise action for each quality that keeps the strength and addresses the flip side. "I will be on time" is general. "I am in my seat five minutes early with my notebook open" is precise.

Page note: three rows, each with boxes for quality, flip side and action. Each row has a **Get AI feedback** button.

**Case to explore.** A teammate describes you in three words you would not have chosen. Ask your Dojo to play that teammate, so that you can practice asking what they saw and listening to the answer.

**Ask your Dojo.**

> Play a teammate from a group project who is giving me honest and kind feedback. I will tell you three qualities I think I am known for. For each one, describe one way it could come across differently to a new teammate, and then ask me for an example where that might have happened. Do not tell me who I am. Ask me.

## Stop 5 · Say what you bring

**Why this stop.** Once you have evidence, you need a short way to say what it adds up to. A personal value proposition is two or three sentences that tell a specific audience what you offer and what backs it up.

**After this stop you can** state your value proposition for one target role and support it with one STAR situation.

**Watch.** [Get started with a personal value proposition](https://youtu.be/UkuUXRmrRoY), CTI, 10:25 · [PlayPosit version](https://www.wevideo.com/interactive/player_v2?type=share&bulb_id=230079)

**Read.**

- [Build your personal value proposition](https://hbr.org/2011/11/a-value-proposition-for-your-c), Bill Barnett, Harvard Business Review, 2011
- [What self-awareness really is (and how to cultivate it)](https://hbr.org/2018/01/what-self-awareness-really-is-and-how-to-cultivate-it), Tasha Eurich, Harvard Business Review, 2018

**Draft yours in four moves**, following the first article:

1. Choose one target role or team.
2. Identify two or three strengths that matter for that target.
3. Connect each strength to what the target needs.
4. Support each strength with one of your STAR situations from stop 1.

Page note: four boxes, then the page assembles them into one paragraph the student can copy. The assembled paragraph has a **Get AI feedback** button.

Note for Sathya: YouTube has no transcript for the PVP video, so I wrote this stop from the two readings. Please check the four moves against what the video teaches.

**Case to explore.** You have thirty seconds with someone who can open a door for you. Ask your Dojo to play that person and to ask "Why you?".

**Ask your Dojo.**

> Play a hiring manager I have just met at a career event. Ask me "What do you do, and why should I talk to you?". After my answer, tell me which part you would remember tomorrow, and ask one question about the evidence behind it. Then let me try again.

## Stop 6 · Choose one stretch

**Why this stop.** A section of your bar gets drawn when you have done something that fills it. This stop turns one empty section into one action that is a little beyond what is comfortable and still doable in two weeks.

**After this stop you can** carry one specific action into your growth plan.

**Choose and write.**

1. Choose one question from stop 1 that you marked Not yet or Partial.
2. Write one action for the next two weeks that would give you a situation for it. A useful action involves at least one other person, has a date, and ends in a result someone else could confirm.
3. Write the question you will be able to answer when it is done.

Example: "By 16 October I will explain my class project to my cousin who works in nursing, and ask her to tell me back what it does. Then I can answer question 1."

Note for Sathya: the example is mine. Please replace it with one you prefer.

Page note: three boxes, a **Get AI feedback** button on the action, and a **Copy to my growth plan** button.

**Case to explore.** The action you wrote feels uncomfortable. Ask your Dojo to help you size it, so that it is a real stretch and you can still finish it in two weeks.

**Ask your Dojo.**

> Here is one action I plan to take in the next two weeks to build a skill outside coding: "[paste your action]". Ask me what makes it a stretch for me, who else is involved, and how someone could confirm the result. If it is too easy or too large for two weeks, ask me questions until I resize it myself.

## AI feedback on this page (page notes, not shown to students)

The buttons use the same AI feedback service as the other CST349 pages. Feedback is optional everywhere. Each reply is three or four short sentences: one thing already present, quoting the student's words; one requested element not yet visible; one concrete question. It uses only the criteria listed for that box.

**Instructions that apply to every button on this page**

- Never write or reword the student's answer, and never supply an example situation for them.
- Never judge the person, their readiness or their prospects, and never say how an interviewer or employer would react.
- MARKING STANCE: the student chooses Full, Partial or Not yet. Never assign or suggest a mark. Say which STAR parts are visible and let the student decide.
- STARTING-POINT STANCE: having no situation yet for a part of the T is an expected state at this point in the degree. Never treat it as a shortfall, and never push the student to stretch a weak example to fill a box.
- SHAPE STANCE: never tell the student what shape they are, and never say that one kind of growth is worth more than another.

**Stop 1, questions 1 to 8**

- REQUESTED ELEMENTS: a real situation of the student's own that fits the question; the task they were responsible for; the action they took themselves; the result, with something another person could confirm.
- OBSERVABLE CRITERIA: a specific time and place is identified; the student's own responsibility is stated; the action uses "I" and gives at least one concrete step; the result states what changed and who or what could confirm it; the situation fits the part of the T the question belongs to.
- COMMON OMISSIONS: the action given as a general effort with no step; "we" throughout with no account of the student's own part; a result with nothing a second person could confirm; a description of what the student would do in place of what they did.
- Per question, the fit to check: 1 the listener was outside computing · 2 a team decision and the student's response to it · 3 the other person's field is identified · 4 the group's view before and after · 5 the people affected are identified · 6 the relationship and what came of it · 7 the hardest technical problem and where the work can be seen · 8 the system is identified and what the student learned about how it works.

**Stop 4, each row**

- REQUESTED ELEMENTS: one quality; one way it could come across differently; one precise action.
- OBSERVABLE CRITERIA: the flip side describes how another person could experience the quality; the action states what the student will do, when, and how someone could see that it happened.
- COMMON OMISSIONS: a flip side that restates the quality; an action with no time or observable behavior.

**Stop 5, the assembled paragraph**

- REQUESTED ELEMENTS: one target role or team; two or three strengths; the link between each strength and what the target needs; one supporting situation.
- OBSERVABLE CRITERIA: the target is specific enough that another person could find such a role; each strength is tied to a need of the target; at least one strength is backed by a situation with an action and a result.
- COMMON OMISSIONS: strengths with no target; a strength stated with no situation behind it.

**Stop 6, the action**

- REQUESTED ELEMENTS: one action for the next two weeks; the question from stop 1 it will let the student answer.
- OBSERVABLE CRITERIA: the action involves at least one other person; it has a date; it ends in a result another person could confirm; it matches the question chosen.
- COMMON OMISSIONS: an action done alone; no date; a result only the student could verify.
- NOTE: never choose the stretch or suggest a different one. If the action seems too small or too large, ask what makes it a stretch and whether it fits in two weeks.

## The four questions

Behavioral interview questions ask you to describe something you actually did. Interviewers ask some variation of these four questions often, because the answers show how you learn, how far you are willing to go for the work, and how you work with other people.

1. Tell me about a time you had to learn something unfamiliar to finish work that other people were depending on.
2. Tell me about a time you took on work outside your role or your area because the team needed it.
3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone who may have had more knowledge about the subject than you did.
4. Tell me about a time you noticed that the project was not going in the right direction and you had to go above and beyond to get it on the right track.

### Question 1. Tell me about a time you had to learn something unfamiliar to finish work that other people were depending on.

**Answer A.** In my software engineering course, our team of four was building a scheduling app and I was assigned the database. I had never written SQL, and my teammates needed the tables within a week to connect their pages. I spent two evenings on a tutorial and realized I was following along without understanding it. So I stopped and built a small practice database from our own project data, because I learn faster when I can break something and see why. When my queries ran slowly, I read about indexes and tested the speed before and after adding them. I delivered the tables on day six and all three pages connected to them. Now when I have to learn something new, I start with a small version of the real problem.

**Answer B.** I work part-time at the campus IT help desk. My supervisor asked me to take over the spreadsheet that tracks laptop loans, because the student who built it had graduated. The staff used it every day and it was full of formulas I could not read. I told my supervisor what I did not know yet and asked for three days. I asked two coworkers which parts they used the most and what usually broke, and I emailed the student who built it with three specific questions. Each afternoon I posted a short note in the team chat saying what I had fixed and what was still open. By the end of the week the sheet was working again, and I wrote a one-page guide so the next person would not start from zero.

**Answer C.** I am on the software side of the robotics club. A month before our competition the robot's arm kept missing its target, and the member who understood the mechanical design had left the club. The rest of the team was waiting on a fix. Nobody in software knew much about gears or torque, including me. I started going to the mechanical subteam's meetings, read the chapter on torque from a physics textbook, and asked the engineering lab technician to check my calculation. I found that our code assumed a gear ratio that did not match the arm that was actually built. I corrected the value and the arm hit the target in eight out of ten tries. The team used that version at the competition.

### Question 2. Tell me about a time you took on work outside your role or your area because the team needed it.

**Answer A.** I was the backend developer on a team project for a community partner. Two days before our final demo, the teammate who was going to present got sick. I avoid public speaking, but I knew the system best, so I volunteered. I called her and asked her to walk me through what the partner cared about the most. Then I practiced in front of my other two teammates and asked them to interrupt me with the questions the partner might ask. I rewrote three slides based on where they got confused. At the demo I covered everything in the time we had, and the partner asked for a follow-up meeting to talk about using the system. I still do not enjoy presenting, but I know now how to prepare for it.

**Answer B.** I was a cashier at a small store. The owner's online order page stopped charging the correct sales tax, and there was nobody to fix it, so I offered to look. I had never used that store platform. I read the platform's help pages, made a copy of the store's settings, and tested each tax setting on the copy before touching the real one. I kept a list of every change so that I could undo it. It took me most of a week of evenings to understand how the tax rules were layered, and the problem was one rule that was overriding the others. Orders were charging correctly again by the weekend. The owner then asked me to check the settings every month.

**Answer C.** At a hackathon, my team of four programmers was building an app for food bank volunteers. By the end of the first evening I noticed that none of us had talked to a volunteer. I am usually the one who writes the most code, but I left the code to the others for the first morning and went to the food bank. I followed a sorting shift and wrote down each step the volunteers did and what they had in their hands. I learned that they cannot hold a phone while they sort. I brought that back to the team, and we changed the design from a phone app to a screen on the wall. We placed second, and the judges mentioned the visit in their comments.

### Question 3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone who may have had more knowledge about the subject than you did.

**Answer A.** In a three-week class project, our team lead was a senior who had used a large web framework during his internship, and he wanted us to use it. I thought it was too much for a project this short, but I did not know enough to argue the point. So over a weekend I built our login page twice, once with his framework and once with a simpler tool. I measured how long the setup took and how many files each version needed, and I wrote down what I got stuck on. I showed him both versions. He looked at the numbers and agreed to use the simpler tool for this project. I also learned enough about the framework to see when it would be the better choice.

**Answer B.** I work as a tutor. The lead tutor, who has three years of experience, asked all of us to show students a worked solution first. I had noticed my students copying the steps without understanding them, and I disagreed with her. Before I said so, I asked her why that approach had worked for her, and I listened. Her students were mostly in a course with exams every two weeks, so they needed speed. Mine were not. I proposed trying my approach, where I ask the student questions first and show the solution afterwards, with two students for two weeks, and I offered to report what happened. She agreed. After the two weeks we talked it through, and the tutoring center now uses both approaches depending on the student.

**Answer C.** I volunteered to build the website for a small community clinic. The director, who has run the clinic for twenty years, wanted an intake form with thirty questions. I thought people filling it in on a phone would give up. She knew the patients far better than I did, so I had to understand her side first. I read the reporting rules from the clinic's funder to learn which questions were required. With her permission, I spent an afternoon in the waiting room watching how long the paper form took people. Then I proposed asking eight questions online and the rest during the visit. She changed two of my eight and accepted the plan. I do not know yet how many people complete the new form.

### Question 4. Tell me about a time you noticed that the project was not going in the right direction and you had to go above and beyond to get it on the right track.

**Answer A.** I was the treasurer of a student club. Three weeks before our annual event I noticed that almost nobody had registered. Publicity was not my job, but the budget I managed depended on attendance. I visited classes in two other departments and asked students why they were not coming. I learned that our date was the night before a midterm in a large course that most of our audience takes. I brought this to the officers and we moved the event by one week. I also asked a student from the design program to help me redo the flyer, since ours was hard to read. Sixty people came, compared to twenty-five the year before.

**Answer B.** In a team project, each of us built one part of an app. Two weeks before the deadline I noticed that every part worked alone and nobody had tried running them together. Nobody was assigned to that. I had heard of automated testing but had never set it up. I spent a weekend with the documentation and a video series, and built a set of tests that ran every time someone uploaded code. The first run showed eleven failures. I fixed the ones in my part and sent the team a list of the rest with the error for each. We submitted a working project. I set up testing in the first week of every project now.

**Answer C.** In a four-person group project, I noticed that our shared document had not changed in a week, and two teammates had stopped answering in the group chat. We were going to miss the deadline. I could have done their parts myself. I messaged each of them separately and asked what was in the way. One was confused by the dataset and embarrassed to say so, and the other had picked up extra shifts at work. I set up a twenty-minute call, and we divided the work again by what each person could do that week. I took the data cleaning, and we agreed to check in every two days. We submitted on time, and all four of us contributed to the final report.

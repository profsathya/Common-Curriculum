Demonstrate growth in your Sprint 1 goal

Week 5 · due Monday 28 September, 11:59 p.m. · 100 points · Graded assignments

Purpose

Demonstrate what you learned and how it helped you grow. Evaluate it rigorously, so you can confirm that you are able to demonstrate it in an interview. Being able to verify your own growth is what lets you steer it yourself instead of waiting for a review to tell you.

Tasks

If you would like to work on the assignment in a Google doc without the guidance from this assignment, you can paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it. The guidance structure provided in this page is the recommended approach to ensure you are being guided and challenged along the way.

Each + opens guidance, examples, a reflection question and a box to write in. The guidance and reflection are provided to support you to do a high quality job of answering the questions.

  1. 1.Restate the growth opportunity you chose in week 3 and the milestone you set. Describe how either of them changed as you worked, and explain why.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Open your week-3 submission and copy out the growth opportunity and the milestone as you wrote them then. Underneath, write them as you would set them now. If they are the same, say so — that is an answer. If either changed, the useful part is the why: what you found out in the last two weeks that made the old version too broad, aimed at the wrong thing, or bigger than four weeks.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Changed — Week 3: get better at explaining my work. Now: be able to present something unfinished and take questions without deferring to someone else. It changed because presenting finished work turned out to be easy; it is the unfinished part I avoid, and that is where the fear actually is.

    Milestone changed — I had said “present to two people outside my team”. After the first one I moved it to “present twice and ask each of them what did not land”, because doing it once told me nothing about whether I had improved.

    Reflection

    What did you have to find out before you could set the goal properly?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • The week-3 growth opportunity and milestone are both given.
    • It says how either changed, or that it did not.
    • If something changed, it says what you found out that changed it.
  2. 2.Demonstrate what you learned and how it helped you grow. What can you do now that you could not in week 3? Show it against the evidence you said in week 3 would count — the data, or what the person outside this class said when they saw it.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Two halves, and the second is what makes this a demonstration rather than a claim. First: what can you do now that you could not two weeks ago? Say it as something you did, with when and where. Second: put it against the bar you set yourself in week 3. If your evidence was a person, what did they actually say — their words, not your summary of them. If it was data, give the numbers. If the evidence came back against you, write that; an honest miss is worth more here than a claim nobody could check, and it is the thing you can act on in Sprint 2.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    What I can do now: On 24 September I presented an unfinished prototype to two people at the Salinas meetup and answered their questions myself.

    Against my evidence: I said Priya would tell me what she took away. She said she understood the problem but not why I had built it that way — so half of it landed. That is not what I hoped she would say, and it tells me the part I still cannot explain is my reasoning, not my work.

    Another — What I can do now: ask for help within a day of getting stuck. My evidence was the waiting times I wrote down: three times in two weeks, waits of 1 day, 4 hours and 2 days. The last one was the week I was busiest, which is when I most need it.

    Reflection

    If someone read only your evidence, would they reach the same conclusion you did?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what you can do now that you could not in week 3, as something you did.
    • It puts that against the evidence you set in week 3 — the data, or the person’s own words.
    • Where the evidence came back against you, it says so.
  3. 3.Go to your AI Dojo and ask it to interview you about this the way a hiring manager would. Keep asking it for harder follow-up questions until you can answer without hedging. Write down the question you could not answer at first, and what you changed. If you would rather work with a person, find someone who will question you rigorously.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Give it what you wrote for task 2 and ask it to interview you as a hiring manager would — one question at a time, following up on your answers rather than moving down a list. When an answer comes out vague, ask it to push on that one. The point of this task is the question you cannot answer: that is the part of your growth you can feel but not yet show, and finding it now is far better than finding it in a real interview. Write down that question and what you changed because of it.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    With the Dojo — It asked what I would do differently if the audience disagreed with my approach, and I had nothing. I had prepared to explain my work, not to defend it. I went back and worked out which of my choices I could actually defend and which I had made by default, and now I can say which is which.

    With a person — My manager asked how I would know my explanation had worked. I said “people nod”, which is not an answer. I changed my evidence to asking one person afterwards what they took away.

    Reflection

    Which question would you least want to be asked in a real interview?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names a specific question you could not answer at first.
    • It says what you changed because of it.
  4. 4.Did moving out of your comfort zone get easier as you worked at it? If yes, what made the difference; if not, why not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Think about the first time you did the uncomfortable thing and the most recent time. Was it different? If it got easier, what actually changed — the preparation, the people, having done it once, knowing what to expect? If it did not, say so and say why: too few repetitions, the wrong kind of practice, a bad first experience that made the second harder. Both answers are useful, and the second is often more useful than the first.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Yes — The first time I could not look up from my notes. The third time I could. What made the difference was practising out loud with someone rather than rereading what I had written; the fear was about being watched, not about the material.

    No — It did not get easier, and I think it is because I only did it twice. Two goes is not enough to know whether the first one was a fluke or the second one was.

    Reflection

    What would have to happen for it to get easier next sprint?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It compares the first time with a later time.
    • It answers either way, with what made the difference or why not.
  5. 5.Did you have a partner who helped move your work? If yes, how; if not, why not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Answer for what actually happened. If a partner helped, say what they did — the question they asked, the thing they spotted, the meeting that made you finish something. If you did not have one, or it never got going, say why: no one available, schedules that did not meet, you did not ask, it did not seem worth it. Both answers tell us something we need for the rest of the semester.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Yes — Diego asked me why I was practising on people who already knew the project. That one question is why I went to the meetup instead.

    No — We swapped goals in week 4 and then never met. I work Thursdays and Saturdays, the two times we tried did not come off, and I did not chase it.

    Reflection

    What would you want from a partner in Sprint 2?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It answers either way, with the how or the why not.
    • If yes, it says what the partner actually did.
  6. 6.Explain how you used AI, if you did: how it helped, what you checked that it got wrong, and what you brought that it did not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Describe what you actually did with it, not what it is for. What did you ask, and what came back? The checking is the part that matters most: name a place where you tested what it said against something real — a person, a posting, your own experience — and say what happened. Then say what was yours: the judgement, the situation you chose, the thing you knew was off, the part that needed you to be in the room. If you did not use AI for this, say so; that is a complete answer.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    I used it to rehearse and to generate questions I might be asked. It was good at the questions and bad at judging my answers — it called an answer strong that Priya later did not follow at all, so I stopped using it to tell me whether something landed and started using it only to make the questions harder. What I brought was the room: the actual people, and knowing which of their reactions mattered.

    Reflection

    What did you do that the tool could not have done for you?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what you asked for and what came back, or that you did not use AI.
    • It names something you checked, and what happened when you checked it.
    • It says what you brought that the tool did not.
  7. 7.Identify what still remains — where you are not yet where you want to be on this, and what you will do about it in Sprint 2.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Two things: where you are still short, and what you will do about it. Be specific enough to start in the first week of Sprint 2. “Keep practising” is not a next step; “present at the October meetup and ask two people afterwards what they took away” is. If the goal turned out to be bigger than you thought, say so — a goal that grew as you worked on it is a sign you understand it better, not a sign you failed at it.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    I can explain what I built. I still cannot defend why I built it that way when someone pushes, which is the harder half and the one interviews actually test. In Sprint 2 I am taking one design decision from the project and writing the case for it and the case against it, then putting both to Priya.

    Reflection

    Is what remains a bigger thing than what you started with?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names something specific you are still short on.
    • It names a next step you could start in the first week of Sprint 2.

Criteria

Check it before you submit
  • It restates the growth opportunity and the milestone from week 3, and says how either changed and why.
  • It says what you can do now that you could not in week 3.
  • It shows that against the evidence you set in week 3 — the data, or what the person outside this class said.
  • It names the interview question you could not answer at first, and what you changed.
  • The comfort zone question is answered either way — what made the difference, or why not.
  • The partner question is answered either way — the how, or the why not.
  • It says how you used AI, or that you did not.
  • It names what still remains, and what you will do about it in Sprint 2.

What makes it strong — what changed in 1 is a change in the goal itself, not a change of wording; task 2 shows the thing rather than reporting it — a recording, a message you sent, a note from the person who saw it, a number you can point at — and where the evidence came back against you, it says so; the interview in 3 got as far as a question you could not answer, and a critique that never found one did not push hard enough; tasks 4 and 5 are honest when the answer is no, and a no with a real reason is worth as much here as a yes; what you name in 7 is specific enough to start on in the first week of Sprint 2.

Grading — 100 points, in Graded assignments. The Sprint 1 Exam on Wednesday works from these same questions: three of them are handed out, you answer two — question 2 and one of the others — closed book, about half a page each. What you write here is your preparation for it.

How this page was made

The purpose and the seven tasks are Prof. Sathya’s, word for word, settled on 8 September 2026. The guidance, the examples, the reflection questions and the notes behind the AI feedback button were drafted by AI from that design; he reads them and edits by hand where they miss.

What that looks like — what he decided, what AI built, and how it is checked

What he decided

  • The purpose and the six tasks are his wording from 8 September 2026. Task 1 is the sprint-opening look back — every sprint’s first graded item starts with one. Nothing on this page rewrites, reorders or adds to them.
  • The two layers. The tasks stand on their own; everything under a + is optional. A student who copies the tasks into a document and answers them there has done the whole assignment.
  • Evidence, not a claim. Task 2 asks you to put what you can do now against the bar you set yourself in week 3. An honest miss is worth more than a claim nobody could check, and it is the thing you can act on in Sprint 2.
  • A “no” is a real answer. Tasks 4 and 5 ask whether the discomfort eased and whether a partner helped. If they did not, saying so with the reason is worth as much as a yes, and it is what tells us what to change.
  • The check-before-you-submit list is one list, two uses — you run it on your own draft, and your partner runs the same list on theirs in the week-4 session.

What AI built

  • The guidance under each + — a process for thinking the question through, rather than more explanation of it.
  • The examples — more than one per task, so that no single one becomes the thing to copy. They are illustrations, not answers.
  • The reflection questions and the criteria notes the AI feedback button reads from.

How it is checked

  • Every page is written against the course’s own writing rules and then read by him; where a line misses, he edits it directly.
  • The AI feedback button is held to the observable criteria for each box. It never judges you, never rewrites your answer, and never hands you wording.
  • The Check it yourself list under each box is the same criteria without the AI, so the button is never the only route.

In Sprint 3 you began checking the quality of your own work before anyone else saw it. This learning trail is practice at that, on a piece of work that is not yours. You will check the same one-page update several times, each time in a different way. Before each check you will write down what you expect to find, and afterwards the page will show you what was there. By the end you will know how close your expectations were, and you will have three checks to use on your own work.

About 70 minutes, with a place to stop after about 35. Your work is saved in this browser as you go.

## Level 2: The list

**A read checks the update against whatever you happen to remember of the request. A list checks it against the request.**

When you read something you wrote, you already know what you meant. So where a part is missing, you fill it in without noticing, and your eyes move on. A list written from the request does not know what you meant. It asks one small question at a time, and each question has to be answered from the page in front of you.

**Are you wondering which way of checking is the best one?**

Each one found a kind of fault that the others walked past. The list found what was missing. Reading as Alex found a word and a claim. Following the steps found a step that could not be done. Trying the numbers found a wrong number. A check finds the kind of fault it is built to find, so the useful question is which kinds your work tends to have.

**Are you wondering how anyone has time for all of this?**

On this page the three ways took a few minutes for twelve sentences. On your own work, start with the one that fits the work: numbers for a report with data, steps for instructions or code, the receiver's eyes for anything a person has to act on.

**Doing each level and explaining the whole thing are different skills, and explaining is the one this learning trail is meant to leave you with.** Here you teach a classmate in two short conversations. After that, each idea that is still open gets one question.

### The five ideas of this trail

1. **Finished is not checked.** A check against what was asked finds things that one read of your own work passes over, because on your own work you read what you meant.
2. **Say what "good" is before you check.** Turn the request into a short list of things that must be true, each one small enough to answer with yes or no.
3. **Check in a different way than you wrote.** Read it as the person who receives it, follow your own steps exactly, and try each number against its source. Each way finds its own kind of fault.
4. **How sure you are is not how right you are.** Write down what you expect before you check and compare afterwards. The gap tells you how much checking your work needs.

### Check what you can explain: the question bank (the "sound answer" lines are private)

#### 1. Finished is not checked (taught at Levels 1 and 2)

**Easier:** You finish an assignment late at night and read it through once. Why can that read miss something that the person grading it sees in a minute?

- A sound answer contains: the writer knows what they meant and reads that, so a missing or wrong part gets filled in without being noticed; the grader reads it against what was asked, or without knowing what was meant.

**Harder:** A teammate says: "I ran my code and it worked, so it is done." Name one thing that "it worked" does not tell them, and the check that would.

- A sound answer contains: one thing "it worked" leaves open (whether it does everything that was asked; whether it works for another input or on another machine; whether someone else can run it), paired with a check that would show it (ticking it against the list of what was asked; trying another case; running it from a fresh copy).

#### 2. Say what "good" is before you check (taught at Level 2)

**Easier:** A request says: "Send me the meeting notes, with the decisions we made and who is doing what by when." Write the list of things that must be true before you send them.

- A sound answer contains: at least three items that can be answered yes or no, among: the decisions are listed; each task has a person; each task has a date. Splitting "who" and "by when" into two items is the point.

**Harder:** Nobody gave you a request: you are writing the README for your own project. Where does your list come from, and what would two of its items be?

- A sound answer contains: the list comes from the reader and what they need to do with the README (install it, run it, know what it does), or from asking someone who will use it, or from an example of a good one; and two items that can be answered yes or no.

#### 3. Check in a different way than you wrote (taught at Level 3)

**Easier:** Name two ways of checking your own work that are different from reading it again, and say what kind of fault each one finds.

- A sound answer contains: any two of these, each with a kind of fault: reading as the person who receives it (a word they do not know, a claim with nothing behind it); following your own steps exactly or from a fresh start (a step that cannot be done or is missing); working each number out again from its source, or trying one case (a wrong number, a case that fails).

**Harder:** (a) Why did checking against the list not find the wrong number in sentence 2 of Casey's update? (b) What does that tell you about using only one way of checking?

- A sound answer contains: (a) the list checks whether each thing that was asked for is there, and the number was there; it was wrong, which the list does not test; (b) each way of checking finds its own kind of fault, so one way alone leaves the other kinds in.

#### 4. How sure you are is not how right you are (taught at Level 3)

**Easier:** Before each check on this trail you wrote down what you expected to find. What do you learn from doing that which you would not learn from checking alone?

- A sound answer contains: you see the gap between what you expected and what was there; that gap, seen a few times, tells you how far to trust the feeling of being done, or how much checking you need.

**Harder:** A student feels "about 90% sure" every time they submit, and their work usually comes back with three or four things wrong. What should they do the next time they feel 90% sure?

- A sound answer contains: treat the feeling as a poor guide, since for them it has gone with several faults; run their checks anyway, or more of them; keep writing down what they expect so they can see whether the gap closes. "Be less confident" alone is not enough.

#### 5. A second checker, used with care (taught at Level 4)

**Easier:** You are about to ask an AI assistant to check your work. (a) What do you give it besides the work? (b) What do you ask it to do?

- A sound answer contains: (a) what the work was supposed to do: the request, the rubric, or the data it draws on; (b) to list faults or mismatches against that, and not to say whether the work is good.

**Harder:** A checker gives you five points about your report. Two of them look wrong to you. What do you do with each of the five before you change your report?

- A sound answer contains: check every one of the five against the request and the source, including the two that look wrong, since a feeling is not a check; change only what holds up; a checker can be wrong and sound sure.

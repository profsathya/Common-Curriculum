CST349 · What Are My Priorities
CST349 · Fall 2026 · Week 1 · Graded item · 100 points

What Are My Priorities

Due Monday 31 August, 11:59 p.m. · about 30–40 minutes

Purpose

Explore our priorities in detail, based on what we spend our time on and how much we find them useful. Understanding our priorities is the best place to start before we do anything else when there are more options to explore.

You come out of this with a picture of one whole day across six categories — sleeping, nutrition for the body, work, learning, nutrition for the mind (what you read, watch, listen to and scroll), and entertainment/fun — with a percentage and a quality rating on each. Then a closer look at one of them, nutrition for the mind. Then one small thing you want to try.

This works when the numbers are close to your real day. The more real you are, the more you get out of it. If four hours went to scrolling, say four hours — the points come from working through it carefully, and the numbers themselves are yours.

Two ways to do this

Use one, or both. They end at the same picture, go in the same Canvas box, and are worth the same points. Whichever you do, give it 30–40 minutes in one sitting. Open the ones you want with the +.

Writing your own answers out is where a lot of the thinking happens, and it is worth doing for its own sake. Having an AI ask you the next question while you work is a different skill, and that is worth practising too. If you want both, write your stages out first, then take what you wrote into the practice Dojo and let it question your numbers.

The practice DojoA prepared conversation asks you the questions one at a time, and you answer.

This runs in a practice Dojo, which is a different thing from the CST349 AI Dojo you set up on the Set Up Your CST349 AI Dojo page. There is nothing to configure. You click a link, and a conversation prepared for this one question starts asking you things.

The practice Dojo behaves the way this whole course behaves: it asks, you think, you answer. It has more guardrails and has prompts implemented across the conversation to keep it on track. As with everything else, this is an experiment to see how we could help students explore their priorities in a meaningful way.

Stay past the first ten minutes. The answers worth having usually show up once the obvious ones are out of the way.

Say so when it gets you wrong. If it sums you up and the summary is off, tell it. Correcting it is part of the work. Keep going until the picture matches what you meant.

In writing (Without using AI)You take the same questions, in the same order, and write your answers out.

Work through four stages in this order, writing your answers as you go — what you write is what you hand in.

Stage 1 — Your first numbers. Start with this question, and answer it before you check anything:

Most people spend their time in a 24-hour period on: sleeping, nutrition for the body, work, learning, nutrition for the mind, and entertainment/fun. How would you break down your percentages? And how would you rate the quality of each?

Write the six percentages. They should come to about 100. Then rate the quality of each in your own words — a word or a short phrase is enough.

Stage 2 — Make the numbers real. Go back through the six and name what filled them. Types and sources, so a rough shape of the day is all you need:

  • Sleeping — the hour sleep starts, and the hour you get up.
  • Nutrition for the body — what kind of meals, and when they happen.
  • Work — which tasks, paid or unpaid.
  • Learning — which classes, which tutorials, and what you were learning for.
  • Nutrition for the mind — which apps, which channels, which people, which books.
  • Entertainment/fun — which shows, which games, and who you were with.

Then revise your own percentages. Naming the sources usually moves at least one of them, and the revised set is the one that counts.

Some things sit in two places. A 2 a.m. scroll could be entertainment or nutrition for the mind. Decide where it goes and write one line on why — that decision is part of the work.

Stage 3 — Nutrition for the mind, closer up. Take this one category further. Write:

  • Every source you get information and ideas from, listed by name.
  • What each one feeds — what you come away knowing, thinking about, or feeling.
  • Why you gave this category the quality rating you did.

Then one line on the physical side — how eating, exercise and sleep showed up in your numbers. One line is enough. What you do with it is yours.

Stage 4 — The try. Finish with this:

From everything you just wrote, is there one small thing you want to try in the next few weeks? It can be small — it just needs to be real enough that you’d notice yourself doing it.

Write it in one sentence, in a form you could check on Friday. “Read one long article before I open TikTok” is checkable. “Be better about my phone” leaves you nothing to look at. If nothing comes to mind, write that, and write why.

Submit

One text box in Canvas, whichever way you did it.

1. Your six categories. A percentage and a quality rating for each, using the numbers you landed on.

2. Nutrition for the mind, closer up. Your sources, what each one feeds, and why you rated it the way you did.

3. The one thing you will try. One sentence, in a form you could check.

4. What surprised you. Two or three sentences — something you had never put into words before, or a category that turned out different from what you assumed.

5. Your record, underneath. If you used the practice Dojo, paste the whole conversation, unedited. It has a download / copy-to-clipboard option built in — use that, then paste it straight into the Canvas box. If you wrote it out, paste your writing, as you wrote it. If you did both, paste both.

How this is graded

Diligent and detailed work earns the points, whichever way you did it. Any picture that is honestly yours will do.

Done is all six categories with percentages and quality ratings, the closer look at nutrition for the mind, and a try you could check.

Strong is where naming the types and sources moved something — a percentage you revised, a source you had not been counting, a rating you changed your mind about — and you say what moved and why.

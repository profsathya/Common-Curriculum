## Level 1: The interviewer's chair

**You are hiring a student assistant for a campus lab. You have two candidates and time for a few questions each.** You ask both of them the same one:

> Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate.

Read both answers once, as you would hear them.

**Avery**

> Last spring four of us built a scheduling app for a campus clinic in our software engineering class. I was in charge of testing. Two days before the demo, my teammate Lee wanted to skip the tests for the booking screen and use the time to build a calendar view. I thought that was a mistake, because booking was the one part the clinic would use every day. I did not argue it out in the group chat. That evening I wrote three tests for booking and ran them, and two failed: a patient could book a slot that was already taken. The next morning I showed Lee the two failures and asked which he would rather show the clinic, a calendar or a booking screen that works. We dropped the calendar view and fixed the double booking, and it held up in the demo. Lee and I still disagree about plenty, but now we start with what we can run.

> I think disagreements are normal on any team, and I have had my share. In one group project, a teammate and I saw things differently about how to divide the work. We were able to find a compromise that worked for everyone, and we finished the project on time. It taught me that being open-minded and respectful always leads to a better outcome.

Nothing in Devon's answer is wrong. Devon may have handled a disagreement as well as Avery did. The trouble is that you, in the interviewer's chair, cannot tell. Every sentence of it could be said by anyone about any project.

**An interviewer who asks "tell me about a time" is listening for three things: one real situation, what you yourself did in it, and what came of it.** The question is not a test of whether you have opinions about teamwork. It is a request for one example, because how a person acted once tells an interviewer more about the choices they make than what they say they believe.

**Are you wondering whether this is the STAR method?**

Yes. Situation, Task, Action, Result is often called STAR. The name matters less than the sizes: a short situation and task, an action that takes up about half of the answer, and a result that can be seen or counted.

**Are you wondering what counts as a result when the project was not a success?**

Something still came of it: what you fixed, what you learned and did differently the next time, what the other person said. "It failed, and the next time I asked for the requirements in writing before starting" is a result.

**A follow-up asks about the one thing the answer left unclear.** It does not test whether the story is true, and a smooth reply does not prove anything by itself. It gives the candidate a second chance to say what was missing. In a real interview you may not get that second chance: an interviewer with twenty minutes and six questions often moves on. So a prepared story has its details ready before anyone asks: what exactly the disagreement was, what you did next, what the other person thought.

### The five ideas of this trail

1. **What the interviewer listens for.** One real situation, what you yourself did in it, and what came of it. One example shows the choices you make better than a statement of what you believe.

4. **A follow-up settles something.** It asks about the one thing the answer left unclear. A prepared story has that detail ready, because the follow-up may never come.
5. **One story, several questions.** The middle of a true story stays the same. A new first and last sentence make it answer a different question, so a few stories cover most of them.

## Your side trail

**You have one story ready. An interview usually needs more than one.**

Prepare a second story, from a different part of your life than the first: if the first was a class project, take a job, a club, a family responsibility or a team.

1. Write the story as an answer to one question of your choice.
2. Then write a new first sentence and a new last sentence that make it answer a second, different question.
3. Say it out loud once, to another person or to your phone's recorder, and note how long it took.

**Your plan.** Write three things before you start: the story in one line, the two questions, and which of the four parts you expect to be the hardest to tell.

### The interview: the question bank (the "sound answer" lines are private)

#### 1. What the interviewer listens for (taught at Level 1)

**Easier:** An interviewer asks "Tell me about a time you worked under pressure." What three things is the interviewer listening for in the answer?

- A sound answer contains: one particular situation, what the candidate themself did, and what came of it. Any words that mean these three.

**Harder:** A candidate answers "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate" with: "I believe every disagreement is a chance to learn, and I always try to see the other side." The answer is sincere and well said. Why does it not help the interviewer?

- A sound answer contains: it states a belief and gives no particular occasion, so the interviewer cannot see what the candidate did or what came of it; anyone could say it. Saying that an example shows the choices a person actually makes is welcome and not required.

#### 2. The four parts and their sizes (taught at Level 2)

**Easier:** Name the four parts of an answer to a behavioral question in order, and say which one should be the longest and why.

- A sound answer contains: situation, task, action, result, in that order; the action is the longest, because what the candidate did is what shows the interviewer how they work.

**Harder:** An answer has 150 words: 90 on the project and how the team was set up, 20 on what the candidate did, 40 on how well it turned out. (a) What will the interviewer still not know? (b) What should the candidate cut, and what should they add?

- A sound answer contains: (a) what the candidate themself did, step by step, and so how they work; (b) cut most of the background down to a sentence or two, and add to the action.

#### 3. "I", and something you could picture (taught at Level 2)

**Easier:** Rewrite this sentence so that an interviewer can picture it. You may invent the details: "We communicated well and solved the problem as a team."

- A sound answer contains: a sentence in the first person that names what the person did and at least one thing that could be pictured or checked (what was said or made, to whom, when, a number). The invented details do not matter.

**Harder:** A classmate says: "Saying 'I' all the time sounds arrogant. I would rather say 'we' and give my team the credit." What would you tell them?

- A sound answer contains: the question is about what the candidate did, and "we" leaves the interviewer unable to tell; saying "I did this part" is accurate and not a claim to everything. Giving the team credit for its parts while saying "I" for one's own is a fine answer.

#### 4. A follow-up settles something (taught at Level 3)

**Easier:** At Level 3, one question to Devon got a real story and two did not. What was different about the one that worked?

- A sound answer contains: it asked for the particular thing the first answer had left out (what the disagreement was and what Devon did next); the other two asked for an opinion or a yes or no.

**Harder:** You have told your story to a friend and they have no questions. Give two follow-up questions an interviewer might still ask about almost any story, and say what you would do about them before the interview.

- A sound answer contains: two questions of the kinds taught (what exactly happened; what did you do as opposed to the team; why that and not something else; what did the other person think; what would you do differently), and that the answers should be put into the story or prepared, since the follow-up may not be asked.

#### 5. One story, several questions (taught at Level 4)

**Easier:** You have a good story about a group project where the plan fell apart in the last week. Name two different behavioral questions that the same story could answer.

- A sound answer contains: two questions that are different from each other and that the story fits, for example about something going wrong, working under time pressure, a disagreement, or learning something quickly.

**Harder:** When you use one story for a second question, what do you change and what do you leave alone, and why?

- A sound answer contains: the opening and the ending change, to point at what the new question asks about; the middle, what happened and what you did, stays, because it is what happened. Saying that the emphasis inside the action may shift is welcome and not required.

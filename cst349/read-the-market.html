Where this week is going

This week you connect three things: what the market is asking for, work that holds your attention, and the evidence you want to build next. Together they shape your direction this semester.

In two to three months, Prof. Sathya may send an email about you to one of his industry contacts. You draft that email yourself in Find Your Gap, the third of this week’s three activities, from the work you do in the first two. Two questions set the standard for it:

Would Prof. Sathya actually send this? Would his contact actually be spurred to action by it?

A contact acts when they can see a specific opportunity and a specific reason you fit it — which is why the week starts with real job postings.

Learning outcome

Find two things about the market I did not know: one that provides an opportunity and one that is a challenge to overcome. For each, say what in the postings told me so, and what I could do differently because of it.

Two worked examplesWhat the outcome looks like when it is met — written by us as models, not student work.

Example 1 — six new-grad software and data postings.

The challenge — the finding. Two of the six postings say AI screens applications before a person sees them.

The evidence. Sigma Computing’s says “This hiring process utilizes artificial intelligence tools to assist in candidate screening and assessment”, and DoorDash names the tools it uses. I had assumed a person read every application.

What I would do. Use the posting’s own words in my resume, because something is matching text before anyone reads for meaning.

The opportunity — the finding. Underdog lists “Track record of shipping high-quality, feature-rich products with a portfolio of side projects” as a qualification, next to the degree.

The evidence. I did not know side projects were listed as a qualification rather than a nice-to-have.

What I would do. Use the rest of my time in college on projects with real impact, so that what I can show is the qualification.

Example 2 — five cybersecurity postings.

The challenge — the finding. Both TikTok graduate security roles — Product Security Engineer, Seattle and Application Security Engineer, Singapore — set a minimum of “writing and reviewing code in at least two of the following programming languages: Kotlin, Swift, TypeScript, Go, Python, Rust” — and none of those is the language most of my coursework is in.

The evidence. Two languages off that list, as a minimum, for a role aimed at new graduates.

The opportunity — the finding. The same two postings list “CTF or Live Hacking Event experience” and “Bug bounty experience” as preferred qualifications — things I can do on my own this semester without waiting for an internship.

What I would do. Use the rest of my time in college on projects with real impact — here, pick up Go or Rust properly and enter events where the work is public.

2 · Say what kind of work you are looking for

One sentence: a kind of work you would want to do, in a kind of company, solving a kind of problem. It can change later — you need something specific enough to search for. “Backend work on data pipelines at climate-tech companies” gives you something to type into a job board; “software engineering” returns two hundred thousand openings.

Futures thinking, properlyThe method behind the driver and signal in box 3.

Finding the force behind a change and then finding the thing you can watch is the core move in futures thinking. The Institute for the Future teaches it in Ready, Set, Future! Introduction to Futures Thinking on Coursera, part of a longer Futures Thinking specialization. Nothing on this page depends on it.

The Know the Market practice DojoFor when you have postings open but the terms are not resolving into a pattern.

What it needs from you first: 3 to 5 real postings pasted in as text. It cannot open links or browse, so bring the actual words.

What it does. It walks you through harvesting the exact terms in five passes — titles, skills, tools, qualifications and domain, then whole phrases that repeat — one per turn. Then it groups them into a keyword set, states your segment as a one-sentence working hypothesis from a small sample, and builds a search you can click to find more postings like them.

What it does not cover: box 3. It reads the market as it is now; the driver and the signal stay yours.

Open https://dojo.symbioticthinking.ai/?topic=know-market — if it asks for a key, use the link from the week 1 email, subject Symbiotic Thinking Dojo API Key. It ends by handing you a summary block and a JSON block: those are working material to draw on for the boxes above, in your own words. What goes to Canvas is the boxes and the copy button.

Your own AI DojoFor carrying this further than the page asks.

Your own Dojo keeps your context across the semester, which this page does not, and it is where the standing routine from Tuesday will live. A prompt to start from:

I am an early-career computing student aiming at [YOUR SPACE]. Find 12 real, currently open job postings from different companies where that space is explicit in the responsibilities or requirements. For each, give me the company, title, link, and quote the 3–5 exact phrases that describe the work. Then tell me which phrases recur across at least half of them, and show me the count. Do not invent postings — only ones you can link to.

Then, separately: what has changed in postings like these over the last two years, what is driving that change, and what observable signal would tell me in three months whether it is continuing? For each claim, show me your evidence, and tell me where you are inferring.

What comes back is material for your answer, not the answer. You supplied the space; you decide which repeated phrases matter for the work you want. Pick one of its claims and check it against a posting you open yourself.

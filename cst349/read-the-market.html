Where this is going

In two to three months, Prof. Sathya may send an email about you to one of his industry contacts. [slot — Sathya’s exact wording on the promise goes here] He has the final say, and he is open to doing this for as many of you as he can. “May” means there is a bar, and meeting it is yours to do.

Next week you draft that email yourself. Everything you do this week is the raw material for it: what the market is asking for gives the email something specific to point at, what you would be doing anyway gives it a story worth reading, and the gap you find gives it the honest line about what you are building next.

Would Prof. Sathya actually send this? Would his contact actually be spurred to action by it?

The second question is the harder one, and it is the reason this week starts with job postings. A busy person acts when they can see a specific opportunity and a specific reason you fit it. Neither is something you can write without having read the market first.

It also has to be short enough to read in one go and sharp enough to hold attention from the first line — under 150 words [slot — length is Sathya’s call]. That is a high bar, and holding yourself to it is the point.

This page feeds the opportunity the email points at — what the market is actually asking for, in the market’s own words.

Purpose

Learn to read a job market well enough to aim at it, instead of applying harder at it. Telling evidence apart from guesswork about what employers want is a skill you will use every time you change jobs.

You come out of this with three things: two things employers in a space you care about are asking for right now, one thing you expect to change in the next year or two, and your reason for each.

What you are building on

From week 1: your AI Dojo, set up on the Set Up Your CST349 AI Dojo page. You do not need it for the boxes below — the feedback button here is built into the page — but it is where you go if you want to keep working past what this page asks. Setting it up takes about fifteen minutes if you have not yet.

Assumed: nothing about the market. If you have never read a job posting closely, this is the right place to start — the reading is the activity.

Where the market actually is

Start with the numbers, because they change what a sensible plan looks like. When remote work opened jobs to national and global applicant pools, applications per posting roughly tripled. Applications per hire went from around 100 in 2021 to around 300 now, and one recent data-team opening at Ashby drew about 1,500 people. On average, about 18 applicants per role get a scheduled interview. Recruiters run AI at the top of that funnel to move faster, but a human still makes every final call — Ashby does not let AI reject anyone. Meanwhile big companies pulled back on junior hiring and startups picked it up, so some of the openings moved to companies of fifteen people.

Here is what those numbers mean, and it is not what most people assume. If 300 people ask for a table at a restaurant with 18 seats, the 282 who do not get in were not judged and found wanting — the room ran out. Hiring teams optimise for picking a strong twenty, not for reading everyone carefully, so a genuinely good fit gets cut without a call all the time. That is a numbers problem rather than a verdict on you, and numbers problems have a different answer than working harder at the same thing. The answer is to aim: fewer applications, into a space you can describe, with evidence that you understand it. Henry, one of the recruiters in the fireside session, shortlisted 2 people out of 300 on the strength of cover letters a human had actually written.

Those figures come from two sessions, and what is quoted here is a slice of each, chosen to make one point. Both are worth going through in full on your own time. Inside Hiring with Ashby is eight short clips, about 33 minutes total, with Kevin Connolly’s reference slides. Fireside Chat with Recruiters is a chaptered 89-minute recording with two recruiters. The parts this page skips — how long hiring takes, what survives a thousand resume reads, negotiating, the student questions — answer things you will want to know later this semester.

What to work out

Four things, in four boxes. Write straight into them — your work is saved in this browser as you go.

Boxes 1 to 3 are the market read. Box 4 is what happens in the Wednesday session: you run this live on The Commons, and your partner’s job is not to agree with you. It is to push on your reasons — which of these is evidence, and which is a guess? Could that signal actually be checked in three months, by someone who is not you? Bring whatever you have when you walk in, even if it is rough. Rough is easier to push on than nothing.

This way of reading a change — find the force, then find the thing you can watch — is the core move in futures thinking, and there is a lot more of it than we use here. If you want the method properly, the Institute for the Future teaches it in Ready, Set, Future! Introduction to Futures Thinking on Coursera, part of a longer Futures Thinking specialization. It is optional, and nothing here depends on it.

One sentence: a kind of work you would want to do, in a kind of company, solving a kind of problem. It does not have to be right, and you are not committing to it — you need something specific enough to search for. “Backend work on data pipelines at climate-tech companies” gives you something to type into a job board. “Software engineering” describes two hundred thousand openings and helps you search for none of them.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Could you type this into a job board and get back a coherent set of postings, rather than everything?
  • Does the sentence stop being true if you swap the industry out? If not, it is not specific yet.
  • Is it a kind of work, or is it a job title or a technology standing in for one?
  • Could a contact of Prof. Sathya’s read this and know which of their colleagues to forward it to?
In your space, with your reason for each. Read 10 to 15 real, currently open postings from different companies where the space is explicit in the responsibilities, and quote the phrases rather than paraphrasing them. Something counts when it repeats across at least half of what you read — so say how many, out of how many.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Are these the postings’ words, or your summary of them?
  • Did you write the count — how many out of how many?
  • Is either of these a technology you already know and were pleased to spot? That is a hope, not a finding.
  • Would a hiring manager recognise their own vocabulary in what you wrote?

Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI

  • Does your driver identify a force — a cost, a rule, a capability, a change in who is buying — or is it “AI is changing everything”?
  • Are the driver and the signal two different things, or the same sentence written twice?
  • Is this something a contact has already heard from thirty other people this month?
From the Wednesday session. What did they challenge, and what did you do about it? Deciding they were wrong is a fine answer, as long as you say why.
Check it yourself — the same questions, without the AI
  • Did you write what they challenged, or a summary of their answer instead of yours?
  • If the answer is “they agreed with me”, did you ask them to find the weakest of your three?
  • What changed because of it? If nothing, say that and say why.

If you get stuck, or want to go further

Two things sit outside this page, and each is for a specific situation rather than a preference.

The Know the Market practice DojoFor when you have postings in front of you but the terms are not resolving into a pattern.

The condition. You have opened the postings, and the words are not turning into anything you could call a pattern. That is when this helps. It is not the place to start if you have nothing yet: it asks you to paste in 3 to 5 real postings as text before it does anything, and it cannot open links or browse, so bring the actual text.

What it does. It walks you through harvesting the exact words in five passes — titles, then skills, then tools, then qualifications and domain, then whole phrases that repeat — one per turn. Then it groups them into a keyword set, states your segment as a one-sentence working hypothesis from a small sample, and builds you a search you can click to find more postings like them.

What it does not do. Nothing about box 3. It reads the market as it is now; the driver and the signal are yours to work out.

Open https://dojo.symbioticthinking.ai/?topic=know-market — if it asks for a key, use the link from the week 1 email, subject Symbiotic Thinking Dojo API Key.

What you do with what it gives you. It ends by handing you a summary block and a JSON block. Those are raw material, not your submission — paste the parts you agree with into the boxes above, in your own words, and let the rest go. What goes to Canvas is the boxes and the button at the bottom of this page.

Your own AI DojoFor when this page’s questions ran out and you are still working.

The condition. You have answered the boxes, you have run the feedback, and you want to go further than this page asks. Your own Dojo keeps your context for the rest of the semester, which the button on this page does not — and it is where the standing routine from Tuesday will live.

A prompt to start from, with your own space filled in:

I am an early-career computing student aiming at [YOUR SPACE]. Find 12 real, currently open job postings from different companies where that space is explicit in the responsibilities or requirements. For each, give me the company, title, link, and quote the 3–5 exact phrases that describe the work. Then tell me which phrases recur across at least half of them, and show me the count. Do not invent postings — only ones you can link to.

Then, separately: what has changed in postings like these over the last two years, what is driving that change, and what observable signal would tell me in three months whether it is continuing? For each claim, show me your evidence, and tell me plainly where you are guessing.

What comes back is a draft of your answer, not your answer. You supplied the space. You decide which repeated phrases actually matter for the work you want to do. Ask it directly what it is guessing about, then pick one of its claims and check it yourself against a real posting you open. If the posting does not say what it said, that tells you how much of the rest to trust.

What done and good look like

This one is not graded. It is the market half of the picture you finish on Tuesday, and the growth dimensions you set in Sprint 1 come out of it.

Done is four boxes with something real in each: a space, two current asks with quoted phrases and counts behind them, one expected change with a driver and a signal, and what your partner pushed on.

Good is where the evidence moved you. Maybe the phrases that kept repeating were not the ones you expected, or the space you started with turned out to be two different spaces, or your partner found the guess hiding inside your signal. Say what moved and why.

Before you finish

Two questions, worth answering after the work rather than before it.

What did you believe about this market before you opened the postings, and where did the postings disagree with you?

Which of your answers would be the most embarrassing for Prof. Sathya to put in front of a contact who works in this space — and what would you have to look at to fix it?

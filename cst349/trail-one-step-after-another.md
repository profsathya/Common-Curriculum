## The opening paragraph of the page

**One step after another**

In Sprint 1 you set a goal and worked on it one step at a time. This learning trail is about the craft underneath that: how to word a goal so that you can tell when it is done, how to cut it into steps you can finish, and what to do when a week does not go as planned. You will plan a four-week sprint for a classmate named Quinn and run it, several times, changing one thing each time. Before each run you will say what you expect, and then you will see what four weeks do to the plan.

About 70 minutes, with a place to stop after about 35. Your work is saved in this browser as you go.

**The rules this sprint runs on**

(Page note: a closed section with this heading, on every level. Opened, it reads:)

- Work on a step adds up from week to week. A step is finished only when all of its hours are in.

## Level 2: The goal

**At the end of week 4, was Quinn's goal done? With Quinn's wording, nobody can say.** "Make good progress" has no test. Quinn can always answer yes, and so the goal never tells Quinn that something has to change.

Here are three ways Quinn could word the goal.

- **A.** "Work on my budget tracker project and make good progress."
- **B.** "Finish my budget tracker."
- **C.** "By Friday of week 4, the club's treasurer has entered last month's receipts into my tracker and told me one thing to change."

**Predict.** A stranger looks at Quinn's work at the end of week 4. With which wording could the stranger tell, without asking Quinn, whether the goal is done?

- Only A
- Only B
- Only C
- B and C

(Page note: one choice, then the page shows what each wording does to the run the student already saw:)

**The same four weeks, judged by each wording**

- **A, "make good progress":** cannot be told. Quinn says yes. A stranger has nothing to check.
- **B, "finish my budget tracker":** it depends on who you ask. Does "finished" mean that it adds up expenses, or that it has every feature Quinn thought of? Two people can look at the same tracker and give different answers.
- **C, "the treasurer has entered last month's receipts and told me one thing to change, by Friday of week 4":** not done, and anyone can see that. The treasurer has not touched it.

Wording C does two things that the others do not. It names a result that someone other than Quinn can see: a person, doing a thing, with the tracker. And it has a date. With those two, the end of week 4 gives an answer, and the answer does not depend on how Quinn feels about the month.

Wording C is also harder to be pleased with. Under A, Quinn's first plan was a success. Under C it failed. The plan and the four weeks are the same; the wording is what makes the failure visible while there is still a next sprint to fix it in.

**Choose the goal for the rest of this trail.** Use wording C, or write your own for Quinn.

**Are you wondering whether a goal you can check has to be small?**

No. Wording C is not smaller than wording B. It asks for less of the tracker and for something B never mentions: another person using it. What makes a goal checkable is that someone could look and say yes or no. How large it is, is a separate choice.

**Are you wondering what to do with a goal like "get better at interviews", where there is no thing to show?**

Ask what someone could see if it were true. "By the 20th I have answered three practice questions out loud with a classmate, and they have told me which answer was weakest" can be checked. "Get better" cannot.

**Are you wondering how small a step should be?**

Small enough that you can finish it in one sitting, and that when the sitting ends something exists that was not there before: a list, a version that runs, a page. If you cannot say what will exist at the end, the step is still too large or too vague.

**Are you wondering why step a comes first in every order?**

Because every other step needs it, and it needs nothing. Look for that step in your own plans: the one you can start today with what you already have. If no step in your plan is like that, the plan has no first step yet.

**Are you wondering whether showing unfinished work early is risky?**

Step f does not show unfinished work. It shows a finished small thing: a tracker that takes an expense and gives a total. It does less than Quinn hopes the tracker will do in the end, and it is complete at what it does.

## Check what you can explain (the shared ending)

The heading, the lead, the checklist, the buttons and the messages are the same words as on the bird glide.

### The five ideas of this trail

1. **A goal you can check.** It names a result that someone other than you could see or try, and a date. Then the end of the sprint gives a yes or a no that does not depend on how you feel about it.
2. **A step you can finish.** Small enough for one sitting, and when the sitting ends something exists that was not there before.
3. **The order of the steps.** Some steps need others first. The first step is one you can start today, and the step that another person can react to comes as early as it can.
4. **A plan meets the week.** Fewer hours arrive than were planned. With the same work, the plan that has a finished piece early loses less, and room to spare lets you decide in advance what the lost hours cost.
5. **A finished step brings something back.** Show it, and what comes back changes the next step. Changing the plan is not giving up on the goal.

(Page note: taught at: idea 1, Level 2; idea 2, Level 3; idea 3, Level 3; idea 4, Levels 1 and 4; idea 5, Level 4.)

## Your side trail (about 20 minutes)

**You have planned Quinn's month. Now do one sitting of your own.**

Take one real piece of work that is due this week, in any class. This is not a new plan for the month: it is one piece of work and one sitting.

1. Cut it into three steps, each small enough for one sitting, and say what will exist when each is finished.
2. Write how many minutes you expect the first step to take.
3. Do the first step now. Note the time when you start and when you stop.
4. Come back and write what happened.

**Your plan.** Write three things before you start: the piece of work (describe it; do not paste it), your three steps with what each leaves behind, and the minutes you expect the first one to take.

**Your report.** Write how many minutes the first step took against what you expected, what exists now that did not before, and whether you would cut the other two steps differently after doing the first.

### The question bank (the "sound answer" lines are private)

#### 1. A goal you can check (taught at Level 2)

**Easier:** Here is a goal: "Improve my resume." Rewrite it so that a stranger could tell, on a date, whether it is done. Then say what you added.

- A sound answer contains: a rewritten goal that names a result another person could see or try (for example a named person has read it and marked what is unclear; it has been sent to three postings) and a date; and it says that these two things are what was added.

**Harder:** A classmate says: "If I word my goal so that it can fail, I will just feel bad at the end of the month. A loose goal keeps me motivated." What does the loose goal cost them? Use Quinn's first plan.

- A sound answer contains: under the loose wording Quinn's first plan counted as a success although nothing was finished or seen, so nothing told Quinn to change the plan; a goal that can fail shows the problem while there is still time to act on it. Saying that the checkable goal need not be larger is welcome and not required.

#### 2. A step you can finish (taught at Level 3)

**Easier:** A plan has the step "Write the report (10 hours)". Cut it into three or four steps of one sitting, and for each one say what exists when it is finished.

- A sound answer contains: three or four steps, each of a size that fits one sitting, each with something that exists at the end (an outline, the table of results, a draft of one section, a version a classmate has read); the hours need not be given.

**Harder:** Cutting Quinn's 14-hour step into five pieces did not make the work smaller: it was still 14 hours. So what did the cutting change?

- A sound answer contains: when something finished exists: a piece is finished after a few hours and not only after all 14, so lost hours leave finished pieces and not one unfinished whole, and a finished piece can be shown.

#### 3. The order of the steps (taught at Level 3)

**Easier:** In Quinn's plan, which step has to come first, and how can you tell from the table of steps?

- A sound answer contains: step a, asking the treasurer what the tracker must do; it needs nothing first, and every other step needs it directly or through step b. Naming the idea is enough: the step that needs nothing and that others need.

**Harder:** Quinn's order (a b c d e f g h) and the order a b f g c d e h have the same steps and hours. Under the first, four steps were finished; under the second, five. That is one step of difference. Why is the second so much better for Quinn?

- A sound answer contains: in the second the treasurer tried the tracker in week 2, so someone other than Quinn saw it and the goal was met; in the first nobody saw it at all. The count of steps is not the point; which step is finished early is.

#### 4. A plan meets the week (taught at Levels 1 and 4)

**Easier:** Quinn planned 20 hours and 13 arrived. With the first plan, nothing was finished. Name two things about a plan that decide how much those 7 lost hours cost.

- A sound answer contains: two of: the size of the steps (whether finished pieces exist when the hours run out); the order (whether what matters most was done before the hours ran out); room to spare (whether the plan already said what would be left out).

**Harder:** You have 10 hours of work to do this week and you believe you will get about 10 hours. How would you plan the week differently because of what happened to Quinn?

- A sound answer contains: plan as if fewer hours will arrive; put first the steps that must be finished, each of one sitting; say in advance what is left out if hours are lost. "Work harder" or "find more hours" is not an answer.

#### 5. A finished step brings something back (taught at Level 4)

**Easier:** After the treasurer tried the tracker, Quinn's plan grew by two hours and a step had to be left out. Why is Quinn better off than if the treasurer had first seen the tracker in week 4?

- A sound answer contains: Quinn learned in time that the treasurer would not use the tracker without splitting, and could spend the remaining hours on that; in week 4 the same news would have come with no hours left, after the hours had gone to features the treasurer did not need.

**Harder:** A classmate says: "I changed my plan in week 2, so I failed at my goal." What would you say, and when would they be right?

- A sound answer contains: the plan is the route and the goal is the result; changing the route after learning something is how the result stays reachable. They would be right if what they changed was the goal itself, to something easier, so that it could be called done.

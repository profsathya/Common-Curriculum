Computing Talent Initiative  ·  Pilot concept

Fall 2026 Micro-Internship

A work-based learning experience in real, open, interdisciplinary projects.

Draft for discussion  ·  July 2026  ·  prepared for Tyler & Leslie

A 4–6 week, sprint-based experience in which a student works solo on a real, unresolved gap inside a real open project — mentored by an AI partner and a human domain mentor — and makes a genuine contribution a real audience can react to. Applicants apply to one of the five interdisciplinary projects below. The pilot is designed to exercise the full capability loop — Self-Directed Learner → Integrative Solver → Adaptive Builder: learn an unfamiliar domain fast, work across disciplines to find the real problem, and iterate to a genuine contribution.

How it works

  • Solo, with peer review. Each student owns a specific gap, and carries a cross-review responsibility to one or two peers on the same project (a per-project micro-cohort).
  • 2–3 sprints of ~2 weeks. Sprint 1 is an audition — reproduce the problem and propose. Later sprints investigate and contribute. Program support scales as students level up; expectations and rewards are explicit and seamless across sprints (no filter framing).
  • “Done” is a staged bar. Floor — a standalone artifact showing the CTI 2.0 capabilities, genuine contact with the real project, and a domain mentor’s review. Target — the project’s community engages. Bonus — the contribution is adopted (merged, published, mapped). The mentor is the reacting audience we guarantee, so no student’s success hinges on an outside maintainer.
  • Incentives. Non-credit; a completion stipend; network introductions as the capstone. Advertised to partner institutions; the application names the chosen project.

Which project could be yours?

Pick your major for one example of a way in - a real goal you could take on, and something you would build.

For Computing students

Help aid workers reach families after a disaster.

When a flood or earthquake hits, responders often start from maps that are blank where people actually live. You can help fill them in, and improve the tools thousands of volunteers use to do it.

  • You bringComputing - building and improving mapping tools.
  • You’ll cross intoHumanitarian response and geography: who needs the map, and what it has to show.
  • You’ll buildA merged improvement to an open mapping tool, or validated map data used in a live response.

Based on Humanitarian OpenStreetMap.

For Natural Science students

Make your community’s air quality easy to understand.

Air-quality data exists for much of the world, but the numbers are hard for most people to read. You can turn the science into something a family, a teacher, or a city can actually use.

  • You bringThe science of what is in the air, and what is safe to breathe.
  • You’ll cross intoComputing and public-health communication.
  • You’ll buildA published air-quality guide for a place you care about, on a platform scientists and journalists already use.

Based on OpenAQ.

For Humanities students

Teach other students to explore history with a digital tool.

More and more, humanists study their sources - maps, texts, records - with computing. You can build the hands-on lesson that helps the next student start.

  • You bringThe scholarship, and the questions worth asking.
  • You’ll cross intoComputing methods for text, maps, and data.
  • You’ll buildA peer-reviewed tutorial on a respected open site, or a fix that brings an older lesson back to life.

Based on Programming Historian.

For Business students

Help shoppers see how healthy a product really is.

Millions of people read food labels they cannot easily decode. You can make open product data clearer and more trustworthy, right at the shelf.

  • You bringThe consumer and market lens.
  • You’ll cross intoNutrition science and data.
  • You’ll buildA real improvement to how food data reaches shoppers, on a database used by apps and researchers worldwide.

Based on Open Food Facts.

Mentors & scale

Mentors are recruited per project (CodeDay and partners; PhD students and practitioners) and trained on the CTI 2.0 framework — they practise the same capabilities we ask students to develop (two-context development), rather than merely supervising.

We’re targeting 30–50 students for the pilot, depending on how many mentors we can recruit and train — the hope is for each mentor to work with three to five students.

Why it isn’t an academic exercise

Every project is a live open effort with a real audience, and contributions are real — a merged fix, a documented national index, a re-run analysis, a mapped disaster region. One student, one specific gap, one real project: that specificity is what makes the learning real.

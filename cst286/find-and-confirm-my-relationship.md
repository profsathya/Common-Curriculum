In this clip, “no external force” should be read as “no net outside torque,” meaning no net outside twisting effect. ### 3 · Explore where the mass is

### My exploration · Changing spin speed

**My prediction before the spin-speed explanation** A small mass turns on a light rod. It moves to half its original distance from the axis while no net outside torque acts. How do you expect its spin rate to change, and why?

*After recording your response, open: Save my prediction and explore the relationship. The original response is saved with your learning record.*

Watch [Conservation of angular momentum · Prof. Sathya](https://youtu.be/orAtL67eKc8?t=119).

For rotation about a fixed axis, angular momentum is L = Iω. When the same system experiences negligible net outside torque, I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂. Halving the small mass’s distance makes its I one quarter as large, so its spin rate becomes four times as large. A chair and a person contribute to the total I too; they cannot be left out of that calculation.

**What I learned from the spin-speed video** Explain the relationship in your own words using a detail from the video. Identify the system and axis, and explain when you would expect this relationship to apply.

In this model, two small equal masses move inward or outward on a rotating platform. B is the fixed moment of inertia of the platform and everything that stays fixed to it. Each mass is m. The total I is B + 2mr²; the model assumes negligible net outside torque and keeps the same masses in the system.

Model inputs: fixed moment of inertia B, each mass m, initial and final distances r₁ and r₂, and initial spin rate ω₁. All start empty.

### My exploration · A spinning axis changes direction

**My prediction before the direction explanation** Imagine the same tilted spinning top turning about its own axis twice as fast. Would you expect its axis to sweep around the vertical faster, slower or at the same rate? Explain your reasoning.

*After recording your response, open: Save my prediction and explore the relationship. The original response is saved with your learning record.*

Angular momentum has a direction. A net outside torque changes angular momentum; for a rapidly spinning symmetric top, gravity can turn its direction so that the axis sweeps around the vertical. This motion is called precession. Negligible net outside torque instead preserves the angular momentum direction.

For slow, steady precession, Ω = Mgd / (Iω). M is the total mass (kg), d is the distance from pivot to center of mass along the axis (m), g = 9.8 m/s², I is moment of inertia about the spin axis (kg·m²), and ω is spin rate (rad/s). Ω is precession rate (rad/s). The approximation assumes a fixed pivot, little friction, and spin much faster than precession. With the other quantities fixed, doubling ω halves Ω.

Read the explanation and diagrams in OpenStax, Precession of a Gyroscope. Use them to revisit the wheel or top in the opening video. This model addresses steady precession; predicting when a slowing top falls requires more detail.

**What I learned about a spinning axis** Use a detail from the opening gyroscope video and the reading to explain the difference between keeping an axis direction and precessing. Explain how the condition in the relationship fits your goal, and what remains uncertain.

Model inputs: mass M, pivot-to-center distance d, spin-axis moment of inertia I, and spin rate ω. All start empty.

**My prediction about the condition** As a top slows down and begins to wobble strongly, would you keep using the steady-precession relationship? Explain which assumption you would check.

*After recording your response, open: Save my reasoning and check the condition. The original response is saved with your learning record.*

## Worked examples (shown beside the form)

**Example B · Path B, the playground swing**
- *Where I found it:* OpenStax College Physics, section 16.4, The Simple Pendulum.
- *My relationship:* The time for one full swing grows with the square root of the length: `T = 2π √(L ÷ g)`, where `T` is the period in seconds (s), `L` the length in meters (m) and `g` = 9.8 m/s².
- *My condition:* The swing moves through a small angle, less than about 15 degrees from straight down, and air drag is small.
- *My check · Before:* A phone on a 1.0 m string should take 2.0 s per swing.
- *My check · What I did:* Timed ten swings with a stopwatch, starting about 10 degrees out.
- *My check · What I got:* 20.3 s for ten swings, so 2.03 s per swing.
- *My check · What I make of it:* about 1% above the prediction. The length to the middle of the phone was hard to measure, and about 2 cm more length would account for the difference.

**Example C · Path C, a circuit in Tinkercad Circuits**
- *Where I found it:* My Tinkercad Circuits build: a 9 V battery, a resistor and a red LED in one loop. The relationship is Ohm's law applied to the resistor, from OpenStax College Physics, section 20.2, Ohm's Law.
- *My relationship:* The current is the battery's voltage minus the LED's voltage, divided by the resistance: `I = (V_battery − V_LED) ÷ R`, where `I` is the current in amperes (A, shown in milliamperes, mA), `V_battery` and `V_LED` are in volts (V) and `R` is in ohms (Ω). This predicts the current; the LED gets brighter as the current rises, but brightness is not what the formula gives.
- *My condition:* The battery's voltage stays steady and is larger than the LED's voltage (about 2 V for a red LED), so the LED is lit.
- *My check · Before:* With the 470 Ω resistor swapped for 1,000 Ω, I predict (9 V − 2 V) ÷ 1,000 Ω = 0.0070 A = 7.0 mA.
- *My check · What I did:* Changed the resistor in Tinkercad, added a multimeter in the loop set to current, and started the simulation.
- *My check · What I got:* 6.9 mA.
- *My check · What I make of it:* about 1% below my prediction. My check compares my hand calculation with the simulator's own model of the LED, whose voltage is a little more than the 2 V I assumed, which leaves slightly less voltage across the resistor.

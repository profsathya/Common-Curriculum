CST286 · Fall 2026 · Week 1 · Graded item · 100 points

# What Are My Priorities

Due Monday 31 August, 11:59 p.m. · about 30–40 minutes

## Purpose (page note: a three-box arrow chain, Builds on → This goal → Leads to; the middle box is the highlighted one)

**Builds on.** The CST286 AI Dojo you set up on the [Set Up Your CST286 AI Dojo](setup-ai-dojo.html) page.

**This goal.** Explore our priorities in detail, based on what we spend our time on and how much we find them useful. Understanding our priorities is the best place to start before we do anything else when there are more options to explore.

**Leads to.** You come out of this with a picture of one whole day across six categories — sleeping, nutrition for the body, work, learning, nutrition for the mind (what you read, watch, listen to and scroll), and entertainment/fun — with a percentage and a quality rating on each. Then a closer look at one of them, nutrition for the mind. Then one small thing you want to try.

This works when the numbers are close to your real day. The more real you are, the more you get out of it. If four hours went to scrolling, say four hours — the points come from working through it carefully, and the numbers themselves are yours.

**Use one, or both.** They end at the same picture, go in the same Canvas box, and are worth the same points. Whichever you do, give it 30–40 minutes in one sitting.

Writing your own answers out is where a lot of the thinking happens, and it is worth doing for its own sake. Having an AI ask you the next question while you work is a different skill, and that is worth practising too. If you want both, write your tasks out first, then take what you wrote into the AI Dojo and let it question your numbers.

The AI Dojo behaves the way this whole course behaves: it asks, you think, you answer. As with everything else, this is an experiment to see how we could help students explore their priorities in a meaningful way.

- **Stay past the first ten minutes.** The answers worth having usually show up once the obvious ones are out of the way.
- **Say so when it gets you wrong.** If it sums you up and the summary is off, tell it. Correcting it is part of the work. Keep going until the picture matches what you meant.
- **Give the second answer too.** When it asks what a source feeds you, answer, then sit with the question and answer again. The more useful answer usually shows up on the second or third pass.
- **Before you finish,** ask it to play back the picture it heard from you, and tell it whether it got it right.

### Task 1 — Your first numbers

Start with this question, and answer it before you check anything:

> Most people spend their time in a 24-hour period on: sleeping, nutrition for the body, work, learning, nutrition for the mind, and entertainment/fun. How would you break down your percentages? And how would you rate the quality of each?

*(Write the six percentages. They should come to about 100. Then rate the quality of each in your own words — a word or a short phrase is enough.)*

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- Each category has a quality rating in your own words.

### Task 2 — Make the numbers real

Go back through the six and name what filled them. *(Types and sources, so a rough shape of the day is all you need.)*

- **Sleeping** — the hour sleep starts, and the hour you get up.
- **Nutrition for the body** — what kind of meals, and when they happen.
- **Work** — which tasks, paid or unpaid.
- **Learning** — which classes, which tutorials, and what you were learning for.
- **Nutrition for the mind** — which apps, which channels, which people, which books.
- **Entertainment/fun** — which shows, which games, and who you were with.

Then revise your own percentages. *(Naming the sources usually moves at least one of them, and the revised set is the one that counts.)*

Some things sit in two places. A 2 a.m. scroll could be entertainment or nutrition for the mind. Decide where it goes and write one line on why — that decision is part of the work.

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 2.** What filled each of the six, and your revised percentages.
*Placeholder:* Sleeping — … ⏎ Nutrition for the body — … ⏎ Work — … ⏎ Learning — … ⏎ Nutrition for the mind — … ⏎ Entertainment/fun — … ⏎ My revised percentages — … ⏎ Sits in two places — … because …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- Each of the six names what filled it: the types and sources.
- Your percentages are revised, and the revised set is the one you hand in.
- Anything that sits in two places has a decision and one line on why.

### Task 3 — Nutrition for the mind, closer up

Take this one category further. Write:

- Every source you get information and ideas from, listed by name.
- What each one feeds — what you come away knowing, thinking about, or feeling.
- Why you gave this category the quality rating you did.

Then one line on the physical side — how eating, exercise and sleep showed up in your numbers. *(One line is enough. What you do with it is yours.)*

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 3.** Your sources, what each one feeds, and why you rated it the way you did; then one line on the physical side.
*Placeholder:* My sources — … ⏎ What each one feeds — … ⏎ Why I rated it the way I did — … ⏎ The physical side — …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- Every source you get information and ideas from is listed by name.
- Each source says what it feeds: what you come away knowing, thinking about, or feeling.
- It says why you gave this category the quality rating you did.
- There is one line on the physical side.

## For your AI Dojo (hidden on the page; copied into the course Google Doc)

Graded item: What Are My Priorities. Course: CST286. Due: Monday 31 August. Passcode: PRIO-0831.

When a student asks to be walked through this graded item, state the passcode, the title and the due date first, before anything else.

Learning purpose: the student explores their priorities, based on what they spend their time on and how much they find them useful. They come out with a picture of one whole day across six categories (sleeping, nutrition for the body, work, learning, nutrition for the mind, and entertainment/fun) with a percentage and a quality rating on each, a closer look at nutrition for the mind, and one small thing they want to try.

Order: ask the first question as the page gives it, and ask the student to answer it before they check anything: six percentages that come to about 100, and a quality rating for each in their own words. Then ask what filled each of the six, types and sources, and ask them to revise their percentages; where something sits in two places, ask where it goes and why. Then take nutrition for the mind further: every source by name, what each one feeds, and why they gave this category the quality rating they did; then one line on the physical side. Then ask whether there is one small thing they want to try in the next few weeks, in one sentence they could check on Friday; if nothing comes to mind, ask them to say that, and why. Then ask what surprised them.

When the student gives a first answer, ask again; the more useful answer usually shows up on the second or third pass. If you sum the student up, ask whether you got it right, and keep going until the picture matches what they meant. The numbers are the student's own: do not suggest what they should be and do not judge how the student spends their time. Do not write the answers for the student. Do not summarize until asked; when asked, give the four headings using only the student's words.

# Five real-world scenarios: three where my prediction applies, two that look similar but do not

Graded item · Due Date: Monday 26 October · 100 pts

## Purpose

Test your Sprint 2 learning by applying what you learned in three real-world situations where it can be applied, and identify two similar looking situations where the quantitative relationship doesn't hold because the physical condition is different.

## The form

### Part 1 · The quantitative relationship (math formula) and where it is applicable

Bridge from the goal you submitted in week 8 to the mathematical formula you will use in your scenarios.

1. **How I learned it, and how I confirmed it.**
   - Identify the resource you learned the relationship from (by title, link, or person).
   - Describe one check you did that confirmed the relationship works (such as a worked example from your resource, a simulation run from the Own-your-progress activity *Design an experiment and collect data for your prediction*, or a sensor measurement).

   **How I learned and confirmed it.** The resource I used, and the one check that convinced me the formula works.

2. **The relationship.**
   - State the quantitative relationship in words.
   - Write it as a math formula.
   - Identify every quantity in the formula and its unit.

   **My relationship.** In words, then as a formula, with each quantity and its unit.

3. **The condition it depends on.** State in one sentence what has to be true in a physical situation for your formula to be applicable. Write it so that you can check it against any new situation. (Page note: the text of this box is echoed at the top of each block in Part 3.)

   **My condition.** What must be true in the situation for my relationship to be applicable.

### Part 2 · Three scenarios where my prediction applies

Create three distinct real-world situations where your condition from Part 1 holds, and work your prediction with realistic numbers in each one.

- **The situation.** Describe a specific real-world situation where this phenomenon happens and what is moving or changing.

   **Scenario N · The situation.** One or two sentences describing what happens.

- **What I start from.** List the starting quantities for this situation, with a realistic number and unit for each.

   **Scenario N · Given.** The starting quantities, with their numbers and units.

- **The prediction, worked.**
  - Work the calculation step by step from the starting quantities to the predicted quantity.
  - Include the unit at every step and in the final result.

   **Scenario N · Worked.** Each step of the calculation with its unit, and the final result.

- **Why my condition holds here.** Explain in one sentence why the condition you stated in Part 1 is met in this situation.

   **Scenario N · Why the condition holds.** One sentence pointing to my condition in Part 1.

### Part 3 · Two scenarios that look similar but do not apply

Take one of your working scenarios from Part 2 and change one physical detail about the object or its surroundings (such as moving a phone from smooth ice to a wooden desk, or swapping a baseball for a badminton shuttlecock) so that the setup looks similar enough on the surface that someone might try to use your formula, but because of the change the formula is not applicable.

(Page note: two identical blocks, Scenario 4 and 5. - **The situation.** Describe a real situation that looks similar on the surface to one of your scenarios in Part 2.

   **Scenario N · The situation.** One or two sentences describing the look-alike situation.

- **Which scenario it looks like, and why.**
  - Identify which Part 2 scenario (1, 2, or 3) it resembles.
  - Explain what makes the two look the same at first glance.

   **Scenario N · Looks like scenario __ because ...** One sentence on what matches on the surface.

- **The subtle difference.**
  - Identify the one physical difference in this situation.
  - State which part of your condition from Part 1 it breaks.

   **Scenario N · The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks.** One or two sentences.

- **What would go wrong.**
  - State what your formula would get wrong if you used it here anyway (such as predicting a number that is too large, too small, in the wrong direction, or meaningless).
  - Explain the physical reason why.

   **Scenario N · What my relationship would get wrong here.** One or two sentences on the direction of the error and its physical cause.

## Worked examples (shown beside the form)

Each pair shows Part 1, one scenario where the prediction applies, and one look-alike scenario where the condition breaks, using the exact labels of the form.

**Pair A · From the learning trail's accelerometer goal**

- *How I learned and confirmed it:* Learned from the CST286 accelerometer learning trail; confirmed in PhET Forces and Motion: Basics (Acceleration screen) by setting friction to none and pushing the 50 kg crate with 100 N: the acceleration read 2 m/s², which matches 100 N ÷ 50 kg.
- *My relationship:* Acceleration equals push divided by mass, `a = F / m`, where `F` is the push force in newtons (N), `m` is the phone's mass in kilograms (kg), and `a` is the acceleration in meters per second squared (m/s²).
- *My condition:* My push is the only horizontal force acting on the phone (friction is negligible).

*Scenario 1 (Applies)*
- *The situation:* My phone lies flat on a smooth icy tray, and I give it a steady sideways push across the ice.
- *Given:* Push force `F = 0.30 N`, phone mass `m = 0.20 kg`.
- *Worked:* `a = F / m = 0.30 N ÷ 0.20 kg = 1.5 m/s²` in the direction of the push.
- *Why the condition holds:* On smooth ice, friction against the tray is close to zero, so my push is the only horizontal force on the phone.

*Scenario 4 (Looks similar, does not apply)*
- *The situation:* I place the same phone on a wooden desk and give it the exact same sideways push of 0.30 N.
- *Looks like scenario 1 because:* The phone's mass (0.20 kg) and my sideways push (0.30 N) are identical.
- *The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks:* The wooden desk grips the phone with friction, which breaks the condition that my push is the only horizontal force acting on the phone.
- *What my relationship would get wrong here:* Using `a = F / m` with 0.30 N predicts 1.5 m/s², but the phone does not move at all (0 m/s²): a wooden desk can hold a phone back with about 0.8 N of friction, which is more than my 0.30 N push, so the net force on the phone stays zero.

**Pair B · From a thrown ball (Example D on the goal form)**

- *How I learned and confirmed it:* Learned from OpenStax College Physics Section 3.4 (Projectile Motion) and checked in the PhET Projectile Motion lab by firing a baseball at 20 m/s at 30° with air resistance off, which landed at 35.3 m.
- *My relationship:* Horizontal distance equals release speed squared times the sine of twice the launch angle, divided by gravity: `R = (v² · sin(2θ)) / g`, where `v` is release speed in meters per second (m/s), `θ` is launch angle above horizontal in degrees (°), `g = 9.8 m/s²`, and `R` is horizontal landing distance in meters (m).
- *My condition:* The ball lands at the same height it was released from, and air resistance is small enough to ignore compared to gravity.

*Scenario 1 (Applies)*
- *The situation:* A fielder throws a baseball across a flat field at 20 m/s at an angle of 30° above horizontal, and another player catches it at the same shoulder height.
- *Given:* Release speed `v = 20 m/s`, launch angle `θ = 30°`, gravity `g = 9.8 m/s²`.
- *Worked:* `R = ((20 m/s)² · sin(60°)) ÷ 9.8 m/s² = (400 m²/s² · 0.866) ÷ 9.8 m/s² = 346.4 m²/s² ÷ 9.8 m/s² = 35.3 m`.
- *Why the condition holds:* The ball is caught at the same height it was thrown, and a baseball is heavy and compact enough over a 2-second throw that air drag is tiny compared to gravity.

*Scenario 4 (Looks similar, does not apply)*
- *The situation:* A player hits a badminton shuttlecock across a flat court at the same initial speed of 20 m/s and angle of 30°, caught at the same height.
- *Looks like scenario 1 because:* The launch speed (20 m/s), launch angle (30°), and landing height are identical to the baseball throw.
- *The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks:* The shuttlecock is very light (5 grams) with a wide feathered skirt that catches the air, which breaks the condition that air resistance is small enough to ignore.
- *What my relationship would get wrong here:* The formula predicts the shuttlecock will travel 35.3 m, which is far too large; in reality air drag slows the shuttlecock dramatically in the first second so it drops within a few meters.

# Five real-world scenarios: three where my prediction applies, two that look similar but do not

Graded item · Due Date: Monday 26 October · 100 pts

This graded item will affect your final grade, and the late penalty applies.

## Purpose

A relationship you can use is one you can also stop using at the right moment. In this graded item you work your prediction in three real situations, and then find two situations that look the same on the surface but break the condition your relationship depends on, so that you know not only how to predict but when the prediction can be trusted.

## Part 1 · My relationship and its condition

Start from the goal you submitted in week 8. Three boxes.

1. **The relationship.** Write it in words and as a formula, with every quantity named and its unit given.

   **My relationship.** In words, then as a formula, with the quantities and their units.

2. **The condition it depends on.** What has to be true in a situation for the relationship to give the right answer. Write it as a sentence you could check against any new situation. (Page note: the text of this box is echoed at the top of each block in Part 3.)

   **My condition.** What must be true for my relationship to hold.

3. **How I learned it, and how I confirmed it.** The resource you learned it from, by title, link or person, and one check you did that told you the relationship is right: a worked problem that came out as the resource said it would, a simulation run, or a measurement. If you did the week-9 experiment activity, that run is your check.

   **How I learned and confirmed it.** The resource, and the one check that convinced me.

## Part 2 · Three scenarios where my prediction applies

Each scenario is a real situation with real numbers: a thing someone could do or watch. For each one, give the situation, the quantities you start from, the prediction worked through, and the reason your condition holds there.

(Page note: three identical blocks, Scenario 1, 2 and 3; the four boxes below in each.)

- **The situation.** Where it happens, what is moving or changing, and who would want the number.

   **Scenario N · The situation.** One or two sentences.

- **What I start from.** The given quantities, with the numbers and their units.

   **Scenario N · Given.** The numbers I start from, with units.

- **The prediction, worked.** The steps from the given quantities to the predicted one, with the unit at every step, and the result.

   **Scenario N · Worked.** Each step with its unit, and the result.

- **Why my condition holds here.** One sentence that points at the condition from Part 1 and says why this situation meets it.

   **Scenario N · Why the condition holds.** One sentence.

## Part 3 · Two scenarios that look similar but do not apply

Each of these looks like one of the scenarios in Part 2. The whole point is the subtle difference: the one thing about the situation that breaks your condition, so that your relationship would give a wrong number if you used it anyway.

(Page note: two identical blocks, Scenario 4 and 5. Each block opens with a read-only line "Your condition: " followed by the text of the Part 1 condition box, updated live, so the student is pointing at their own words.)

- **The situation.** A real situation that looks like one of your three.

   **Scenario N · The situation.** One or two sentences.

- **Which scenario it looks like, and why.** Name the Part 2 scenario it resembles and what makes the two look the same on the surface.

   **Scenario N · Looks like scenario __ because ...** One sentence.

- **The subtle difference.** The one thing that is different, and which part of your condition it breaks.

   **Scenario N · The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks.** One or two sentences.

- **What would go wrong.** If you used your relationship anyway, say what the prediction would get wrong: too large, too small, the wrong direction, or a number that means nothing here, and why.

   **Scenario N · What my relationship would get wrong here.** One or two sentences.

## Worked examples (shown beside the form)

Each pair is one scenario where the prediction applies and one that looks similar but does not. They are written in the form's shape, shorter than yours will be.

**Pair A · The learning trail's goal.** Relationship: acceleration = push ÷ mass, a = F/m, with the push in newtons, the mass in kilograms and the acceleration in meters per second squared. Condition: the push is the only unbalanced force on the phone.

- *Applies.* The phone, mass 0.20 kilograms, lies on a smooth icy tray and I push it with 0.30 newtons. Given a push of 0.30 newtons and a mass of 0.20 kilograms; acceleration = 0.30 newtons ÷ 0.20 kilograms = 1.5 meters per second squared, along the push. The condition holds: on ice, friction is close to nothing, so my push is the only unbalanced force.
- *Looks similar, does not apply.* The same phone, the same push, on a wooden desk. It looks the same because the push and the mass are identical. The subtle difference: the desk pushes back with friction, so the push is no longer the only unbalanced force. Using a = F/m anyway predicts 1.5 meters per second squared, and the sensor reads less, because the net force is the push minus the friction.

**Pair B · A thrown ball (Example D on the goal form).** Relationship: the distance a ball lands from me, from its release speed and angle, with speed in meters per second, angle in degrees and distance in meters. Condition: air resistance is small enough to ignore.

- *Applies.* A baseball thrown at 20 meters per second at 30 degrees above horizontal. Worked through the relationship, it lands about 35 meters away after about 2 seconds. The condition holds: a baseball is dense and the throw is short, so the air slows it very little.
- *Looks similar, does not apply.* A badminton shuttlecock hit at 20 meters per second at 30 degrees. It looks the same because the speed and the angle are identical. The subtle difference: the shuttle is light with a wide skirt, so air resistance is the largest force on it after the hit. Using the relationship anyway predicts about 35 meters, and the shuttle drops within a few meters, because the air takes most of its speed in the first second.

## AI feedback (page note; the criteria the button under each box checks when the Dojo box is unticked)

- Relationship: words and a formula; every quantity named with a unit.
- Condition: a sentence that could be checked against a new situation; not a restatement of the relationship.
- Learned and confirmed: a resource by title, link or person; one concrete check.
- Situation (1–3): a real, specific situation; someone who would want the number.
- Given (1–3): numbers with units for the quantities in the relationship.
- Worked (1–3): each step carries a unit; the result has the unit of the predicted quantity.
- Why the condition holds (1–3): points at the condition, not at the relationship.
- Looks like (4–5): names one of scenarios 1–3 and a surface similarity.
- Subtle difference (4–5): one difference, and the part of the condition it breaks.
- What would go wrong (4–5): says the direction of the error, or why the number means nothing, with a reason.

The reply is two or three sentences: what the line already does, then the one thing that would make it precise. No rewriting of the student's line, and no scenarios suggested.

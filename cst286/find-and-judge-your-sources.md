### Task 1 — Search

Open your Physics Learning Goal submission. Copy in the prediction you wrote for question 4a and the three resources you listed for question 5.

a. Write down which parts of that prediction you can already explain, and which parts you cannot explain yet.
b. List every place you looked to learn the parts you cannot explain, or to check the parts you can. Include your three resources and anything new you found. For each new one, write what you searched for or asked.

*(Read your prediction one step at a time, and after each step ask yourself whether you could explain to a friend why that step is true. Each step where you could is a part you can explain. Each step where you would have to say "it just is" is a part you cannot explain yet. Then take each part to your three resources and see whether they cover it. If a part is not covered, search for it using the words of the step itself, because a search for the whole topic returns pages that are too general to help. Keep a list as you go, because task 2 needs the places you looked at and did not use as well as the ones you kept.)*

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 1.** Your prediction and three resources, the parts you can and cannot explain, and every place you looked.

**Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy**

- **Parts** — My prediction: if I release a free throw at the same speed but a steeper angle, the ball stays in the air longer and lands shorter. I can explain why a steeper angle keeps the ball in the air longer, because more of the speed is pointing up. I cannot explain yet how to work out the distance, because I do not know how the time in the air and the sideways speed combine.
- **Places I looked** — My three resources: OpenStax University Physics volume 1, section 4.3; the PhET Projectile Motion simulation; a YouTube video on free-throw arcs. New: I searched "projectile range horizontal velocity times time of flight" and found a Khan Academy article and a forum thread. I also asked my AI Dojo "how do time in the air and horizontal speed combine to give the range".

**Reflection question (behind the +).** How did sorting your prediction into parts affect what you searched for?

   **Your reflection.** One or two lines is enough. (Page note: an optional box under the reflection question; copied with the answers only when something is written in it.)

### Task 2 — Keep or not

From the places you listed, choose the sources you will keep learning from, and one you looked at and decided not to use.

a. For each source you are keeping, explain how you ensured its credibility: who made it, what they based it on, and whether another source you trust agrees with it.
b. For the source you decided not to use, explain why.

*(Work through the three questions for one source at a time. **Who made it:** look for an author, an "About" page, or the organization behind it, and write what you found, including when you found nothing. **What they based it on:** look for references, data, a description of how a simulation calculates its results, or a statement of who reviewed it. **Whether another source agrees:** take one specific thing the source says about your prediction and check it in a second source that was made by different people. For a simulation, you can check it against a case where you already know the answer. The source you decided not to use goes through the same three questions, and your reason comes from what those questions showed.)* (Page note: the three question labels stand out inside the italic guidance.)

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 2.** Each source you are keeping and how you ensured its credibility, and one source you decided not to use and why.
*Placeholder:* I am keeping … Who made it: … What they based it on: … Another source that agrees: … I decided not to use … because …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- Each source you are keeping is listed.
- For each one, it says who made it, what they based it on, and whether another source you trust agrees with it.
- One source you looked at and decided not to use is included, with the reason.

*The + reads:* Examples and a reflection question

**Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy**

- **Keeping** — OpenStax University Physics. Who made it: the book lists its authors, who are physics professors, and it is published by Rice University. What they based it on: the book lists the faculty who reviewed it before publication. Agreement: its range equation matches the one in the Khan Academy article, and the PhET simulation gave the same landing distance when I put in the same speed and angle.
- **Not using** — The forum thread. Two answers disagreed about whether air resistance matters for a basketball, neither gave a source, and I could not find out who wrote them. I could not check it, so I am not learning from it.

**Reflection question (behind the +).** Which of the three questions was hardest to answer for your sources, and what did you do when you could not answer it?

   **Your reflection.** One or two lines is enough.

### Task 3 — Trace

Choose one statement about the physics, from a source you are keeping, that your prediction depends on.

a. Write the statement and where it appears in the source.
b. Find out where the source got it. You can follow the source's own references and links, or ask the AI Dojo where the statement comes from and to give you links to the original sources. Open every link you are given. Write whether the page exists and whether it says what you were told. List each step.
c. Write where the trail ended. Did you reach work that other scientists reviewed before it was published (peer review)?

*(Choose a statement your prediction cannot work without, because then the result of the trace matters to you. Start with the source itself: a reference list, a "further reading" section, or a link in the text. A textbook often states a well-established result without a reference, and when that happens the AI Dojo can help. Ask it where the statement was first shown and who has tested it since, and ask for links. An AI can give a reference that looks correct and does not exist, so open each link and read enough of the page to see whether it supports the statement. A link that does not open, or a page that says something different, is a step in your trail, and you write it down as one. To find out whether a piece of work was peer reviewed, look at where it was published: a scientific journal describes its review process on its own website.)*

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 3.** The statement, each step you followed with what you found when you opened it, and where the trail ended.
*Placeholder:* The statement: … It appears in … Step 1: … I opened it and found … Step 2: … The trail ended at … Peer reviewed: …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- The statement is written out, with where it appears in the source.
- Each step of the trail is listed.
- For every link, it says whether the page exists and whether it says what you were told.
- It says where the trail ended, and whether that work was peer reviewed.

*The + reads:* Examples and a reflection question

**Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy**

- **Statement** — "The horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent", OpenStax University Physics volume 1, section 4.3.
- **Trail** — Step 1: the section gives no reference for it. Step 2: I asked my AI Dojo where it comes from. It said Galileo showed it in *Two New Sciences* (1638) and gave me a link to an English translation; I opened it and found the passage on projectiles in the Fourth Day. Step 3: I asked who has tested it since, and it gave me a link to a journal article on video analysis of basketball shots. The link opened, but the article was about shooting technique and did not test the statement.
- **Where it ended** — Galileo's book was published before journals and peer review existed, so the original is not peer reviewed. The OpenStax book itself was reviewed by physics faculty before publication. I did not find a peer-reviewed test of the statement.

**Reflection question (behind the +).** What did you find when you opened the links, compared with what you were told you would find?

   **Your reflection.** One or two lines is enough.

### Task 4 — Trust

Explain how peer review adds to the credibility of a scientific source, using what you found in task 3. Then write which of your sources you now trust most, which you trust least, and why.

*(First write what happens in peer review: before a journal publishes a piece of work, other scientists in the same field read it, question the method and the reasoning, and can ask for changes or recommend against publishing. Then connect that to your trail. If your trail reached peer-reviewed work, say what that review lets you rely on that you could not rely on otherwise. If it did not, say what is standing in for it, such as a textbook's own reviewers, agreement between sources made by different people, or a check you ran yourself, and how that compares. Your most and least trusted sources should follow from tasks 2 and 3, so a reader can see the reason in what you already wrote.)*

*Box label:* **Your answer to task 4.** How peer review adds to credibility, using your trail from task 3, and the sources you trust most and least with your reasons.
*Placeholder:* Peer review adds … In my trail, … I trust … most because … I trust … least because …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It explains how peer review adds to the credibility of a scientific source.
- The explanation uses what you found in task 3.
- It says which source you trust most and which you trust least, with a reason for each.

*The + reads:* Examples and a reflection question

**Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy**

- Peer review means that before work is published, other scientists who did not do the work check the method and the reasoning, so a mistake has a chance of being caught by someone with no stake in the result. My trail did not reach a peer-reviewed paper. What I have is a textbook that was reviewed by faculty, and a simulation that agrees with it. I trust the OpenStax section most, because I know who wrote it, who reviewed it, and two other sources agree with its equation. I trust the YouTube video least, because it gives no sources and I only kept it for the slow-motion footage.

**Reflection question (behind the +).** How will what you found change the way you choose a source for your next learning goal?

   **Your reflection.** One or two lines is enough.

## AI feedback (page note; the criteria the button under each box checks when the Dojo box is unticked — draft 1's coach notes, unchanged; summarised here)

- Task 1: the prediction from question 4a and the three resources from question 5 present as text; the parts sorted into can / cannot yet; places listed; each new place carries a search phrase or a question asked. "I can already explain all of it" is a complete answer to (a) when the parts are listed.
- Task 2: each kept source has who made it, what they based it on, and whether another source agrees ("I could not find who made it" counts as an answer); the source not used is identified with a reason tied to what the student found. Never say whether a source is credible.

The reply is 3 or 4 short sentences: one specific thing already there, quoting their words; one thing the task asks for that is not yet visible; one concrete question. No rewriting; an unanswered reflection question is never a missing element.

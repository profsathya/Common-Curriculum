Find and judge your sources

Week 4 · due Monday 21 September, 11:59 p.m. · 100 points · Graded assignments

Purpose

Learning to judge whether a source can be entrusted, empowers you to learn, verify and defend what you have learned.

By the time you submit, you will have:

  • Sorted the prediction from your Physics Learning Goal assignment into the parts you can explain and the parts you cannot explain yet, and looked for sources for both.
  • Decided which sources to keep and which one not to use, and explained how you ensured the credibility of each one you kept.
  • Traced one statement about the physics back to its source, and used this experience to explain what peer review adds to a scientific source.

What you are building on

  • The work this stands on: your Physics Learning Goal submission. You need the prediction you wrote for question 4a and the three resources you listed for question 5 in that assignment.
  • What you already did: in the Physics Learning Goal you spent about ten minutes checking who made each resource and whether anyone you trust points to it. This assignment is the full check.
  • Where to go back: Physics Learning Goal, if you want to reread the questions or the guidance on finding resources.

Tasks

If you would like to work on the assignment in a Google doc without the guidance from this assignment, you can paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it. The guidance structure provided in this page is the recommended approach to ensure you are being guided and challenged along the way.

Answer each task in the box under it. The + under each box opens guidance, examples and a reflection question. The guidance and reflection are provided to support you to do a high quality job of answering the questions.

  1. 1.Open your Physics Learning Goal submission. Copy in the prediction you wrote for question 4a and the three resources you listed for question 5.
    1. Write down which parts of that prediction you can already explain, and which parts you cannot explain yet.
    2. List every place you looked to learn the parts you cannot explain, or to check the parts you can. Include your three resources and anything new you found. For each new one, write what you searched for or asked.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI

    Guidance, examples and a reflection question

    Guidance

    Read your prediction one step at a time, and after each step ask yourself whether you could explain to a friend why that step is true. Each step where you could is a part you can explain. Each step where you would have to say “it just is” is a part you cannot explain yet. Then take each part to your three resources and see whether they cover it. If a part is not covered, search for it using the words of the step itself, because a search for the whole topic returns pages that are too general to help. Keep a list as you go, because task 2 needs the places you looked at and did not use as well as the ones you kept.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Parts — My prediction: if I release a free throw at the same speed but a steeper angle, the ball stays in the air longer and lands shorter. I can explain why a steeper angle keeps the ball in the air longer, because more of the speed is pointing up. I cannot explain yet how to work out the distance, because I do not know how the time in the air and the sideways speed combine.

    Places I looked — My three resources: OpenStax University Physics volume 1, section 4.3; the PhET Projectile Motion simulation; a YouTube video on free-throw arcs. New: I searched “projectile range horizontal velocity times time of flight” and found a Khan Academy article and a forum thread. I also asked my AI Dojo “how do time in the air and horizontal speed combine to give the range”.

    Reflection question

    How did sorting your prediction into parts affect what you searched for?

  2. 2.From the places you listed, choose the sources you will keep learning from, and one you looked at and decided not to use.
    1. For each source you are keeping, explain how you ensured its credibility: who made it, what they based it on, and whether another source you trust agrees with it.
    2. For the source you decided not to use, explain why.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI

    Guidance, examples and a reflection question

    Guidance

    Work through the three questions for one source at a time. Who made it: look for an author, an “About” page, or the organization behind it, and write what you found, including when you found nothing. What they based it on: look for references, data, a description of how a simulation calculates its results, or a statement of who reviewed it. Whether another source agrees: take one specific thing the source says about your prediction and check it in a second source that was made by different people. For a simulation, you can check it against a case where you already know the answer. The source you decided not to use goes through the same three questions, and your reason comes from what those questions showed.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Keeping — OpenStax University Physics. Who made it: the book lists its authors, who are physics professors, and it is published by Rice University. What they based it on: the book lists the faculty who reviewed it before publication. Agreement: its range equation matches the one in the Khan Academy article, and the PhET simulation gave the same landing distance when I put in the same speed and angle.

    Not using — The forum thread. Two answers disagreed about whether air resistance matters for a basketball, neither gave a source, and I could not find out who wrote them. I could not check it, so I am not learning from it.

    Reflection question

    Which of the three questions was hardest to answer for your sources, and what did you do when you could not answer it?

  3. 3.Choose one statement about the physics, from a source you are keeping, that your prediction depends on.
    1. Write the statement and where it appears in the source.
    2. Find out where the source got it. You can follow the source's own references and links, or ask the AI Dojo where the statement comes from and to give you links to the original sources. Open every link you are given. Write whether the page exists and whether it says what you were told. List each step.
    3. Write where the trail ended. Did you reach work that other scientists reviewed before it was published (peer review)?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI

    Guidance, examples and a reflection question

    Guidance

    Choose a statement your prediction cannot work without, because then the result of the trace matters to you. Start with the source itself: a reference list, a “further reading” section, or a link in the text. A textbook often states a well-established result without a reference, and when that happens the AI Dojo can help. Ask it where the statement was first shown and who has tested it since, and ask for links. An AI can give a reference that looks correct and does not exist, so open each link and read enough of the page to see whether it supports the statement. A link that does not open, or a page that says something different, is a step in your trail, and you write it down as one. To find out whether a piece of work was peer reviewed, look at where it was published: a scientific journal describes its review process on its own website.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Statement — “The horizontal and vertical motions of a projectile are independent”, OpenStax University Physics volume 1, section 4.3.

    Trail — Step 1: the section gives no reference for it. Step 2: I asked my AI Dojo where it comes from. It said Galileo showed it in Two New Sciences (1638) and gave me a link to an English translation; I opened it and found the passage on projectiles in the Fourth Day. Step 3: I asked who has tested it since, and it gave me a link to a journal article on video analysis of basketball shots. The link opened, but the article was about shooting technique and did not test the statement.

    Where it ended — Galileo's book was published before journals and peer review existed, so the original is not peer reviewed. The OpenStax book itself was reviewed by physics faculty before publication. I did not find a peer-reviewed test of the statement.

    Reflection question

    What did you find when you opened the links, compared with what you were told you would find?

  4. 4.Explain how peer review adds to the credibility of a scientific source, using what you found in task 3. Then write which of your sources you now trust most, which you trust least, and why.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI

    Guidance, examples and a reflection question

    Guidance

    First write what happens in peer review: before a journal publishes a piece of work, other scientists in the same field read it, question the method and the reasoning, and can ask for changes or recommend against publishing. Then connect that to your trail. If your trail reached peer-reviewed work, say what that review lets you rely on that you could not rely on otherwise. If it did not, say what is standing in for it, such as a textbook's own reviewers, agreement between sources made by different people, or a check you ran yourself, and how that compares. Your most and least trusted sources should follow from tasks 2 and 3, so a reader can see the reason in what you already wrote.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Peer review means that before work is published, other scientists who did not do the work check the method and the reasoning, so a mistake has a chance of being caught by someone with no stake in the result. My trail did not reach a peer-reviewed paper. What I have is a textbook that was reviewed by faculty, and a simulation that agrees with it. I trust the OpenStax section most, because I know who wrote it, who reviewed it, and two other sources agree with its equation. I trust the YouTube video least, because it gives no sources and I only kept it for the slow-motion footage.

    Reflection question

    How will what you found change the way you choose a source for your next learning goal?

Criteria

Check it before you submit — the same list your reviewer runs
  • The prediction from question 4a and the three resources from question 5 of your Physics Learning Goal are copied in.
  • It says which parts of the prediction you can already explain and which you cannot explain yet.
  • It lists every place you looked, and what you searched for or asked for each new one.
  • For each source you are keeping, it says who made it, what they based it on, and whether another source you trust agrees with it.
  • One source you decided not to use is included, with the reason.
  • One statement your prediction depends on is traced step by step, and for every link it says whether the page exists and whether it says what you were told.
  • It says where the trail ended and whether that work was peer reviewed.
  • It explains how peer review adds to credibility, using the trail, and says which source you trust most and least, and why.

What makes it strong — the places you looked in task 1 follow from the parts of the prediction you sorted, so a reader can see why you searched for what you searched for; the credibility answers in task 2 report what you found when you looked, including where you found nothing; the statement in task 3 is one your prediction cannot work without, and every step says what you saw when you opened it; the sources you trust most and least in task 4 follow from what you wrote in tasks 2 and 3.

Grading — 100 points, in Graded assignments. You will be asked to explain this to a peer, the TA and/or the instructor, so make sure you are comfortable with the responses you have developed for this assignment.

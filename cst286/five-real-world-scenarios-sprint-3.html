Worked examples

Each pair shows Part 1, one scenario where the prediction applies, and one look-alike scenario where the condition breaks, using the exact labels of the form.

Pair A · Path A, angular momentum

My relationship:
With nothing outside twisting it, moment of inertia times spin rate stays the same: ω₂ = ω₁ × I₁ ÷ I₂, where ω is the spin rate (rev/s or rad/s, the same unit on both sides) and I the moment of inertia in kilogram square meters (kg·m²).
My condition:
Nothing outside the spinning system twists it while the mass moves in or out.

Scenario 1 (Applies)

The situation:
A figure skater spinning on smooth indoor ice pulls her arms and one leg in tight.
Given:
ω₁ = 2.5 rev/s, I₁ = 3.0 kg·m² with arms and leg out, I₂ = 1.2 kg·m² pulled in.
Worked:
ω₂ = 2.5 rev/s × 3.0 kg·m² ÷ 1.2 kg·m² = 2.5 rev/s × 2.5 = 6.25 rev/s.
Why the condition holds:
Her blade turns on a tiny point of smooth ice, so the ice gives almost no twist while she pulls in.

Scenario 4 (Looks similar, does not apply)

The situation:
The same skater pulls in the same way on a rough, slushy outdoor rink, and her blade scrapes as she turns.
Looks like scenario 1 because:
Same skater, same spin rate at the start, same arms and leg pulled in.
The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks:
The scraping blade drags on the rough ice, which breaks the condition that nothing outside twists the system.
What my relationship would get wrong here:
It predicts 6.25 rev/s, which is too large; the drag of the ice takes angular momentum away, so she ends up spinning slower than that and keeps slowing down.

Pair B · Path B, the playground swing

My relationship:
The time for one full swing grows with the square root of the chain length: T = 2π √(L ÷ g), where T is the period in seconds (s), L the length in meters (m) and g = 9.8 m/s².
My condition:
The swing moves through a small angle, less than about 15 degrees from straight down, and air drag is small.

Scenario 1 (Applies)

The situation:
A child on a playground swing with 2.5 m chains is given small pushes and swings gently.
Given:
L = 2.5 m, g = 9.8 m/s², swinging about 10 degrees out.
Worked:
T = 2π √(2.5 m ÷ 9.8 m/s²) = 2π √(0.255 s²) = 2π × 0.505 s = 3.17 s.
Why the condition holds:
Ten degrees is well inside the small-angle range, and a child is heavy enough that air drag is small.

Scenario 4 (Looks similar, does not apply)

The situation:
The same child on the same swing is pushed high, out to about 60 degrees from straight down.
Looks like scenario 1 because:
Same swing, same chains, same child.
The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks:
At 60 degrees the swing is far outside the small-angle range, which breaks the first part of my condition.
What my relationship would get wrong here:
It predicts 3.17 s, which is too small; at large angles the swing spends longer near the top of each arc, and a full swing takes about 3.4 s.

Pair C · Path C, an LED circuit

My relationship:
The current is the battery's voltage minus the LED's voltage, divided by the resistance: I = (V_battery − V_LED) ÷ R, with I in amperes (A), the voltages in volts (V) and R in ohms (Ω).
My condition:
The battery's voltage stays steady and is larger than the LED's voltage (about 2 V for a red LED), so the LED is lit.

Scenario 1 (Applies)

The situation:
A bike tail light runs a red LED from two fresh AA batteries through a 100 Ω resistor.
Given:
V_battery = 3.0 V, V_LED = 2.0 V, R = 100 Ω.
Worked:
I = (3.0 V − 2.0 V) ÷ 100 Ω = 1.0 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.010 A = 10 mA.
Why the condition holds:
Fresh batteries hold close to 3.0 V, which is above the LED's 2 V, so the LED is lit.

Scenario 4 (Looks similar, does not apply)

The situation:
The same tail light after a winter in the garage, with worn batteries that now give 1.8 V together.
Looks like scenario 1 because:
Same light, same LED, same resistor.
The subtle difference, and the part of my condition it breaks:
1.8 V is less than the LED's 2 V, which breaks the condition that the battery's voltage is larger than the LED's.
What my relationship would get wrong here:
It gives (1.8 V − 2.0 V) ÷ 100 Ω = −2 mA, a meaningless negative current; in fact almost no current flows and the LED stays dark, because the battery cannot push past the LED's voltage.

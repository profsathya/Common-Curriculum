Physics Learning Goal

Week 3 · due Monday 14 September, 11:59 p.m. · 100 points · Graded assignments

Purpose

Strengthen your understanding of the physics behind your interest. Whether it is sports, music or games that you care about, there is a fascinating physical science idea underneath it that you can learn — and this sprint is where you go after it.

Tasks

If you would like to work on the assignment in a Google doc without the guidance from this assignment, you can paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it. The guidance structure provided in this page is the recommended approach to ensure you are being guided and challenged along the way.

Each + opens guidance, examples, a reflection question and a box to write in. The guidance and reflection are provided to support you to do a high quality job of answering the questions.

  1. 1.Look back: identify one thing you will be doing differently going forward, based on completing the What are my priorities and the Explore Physics Loop activities.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Open what you submitted for What are my priorities and for the Explore Physics Loop, and read them again. Look for the moment in each where something surprised you — the category that took more of your day than you thought it did, the model that did not behave the way you predicted. Then write one thing you will do differently from here. One is enough, and it should be something you could start this week.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    From the priorities budget — I found that about four hours of my day went to things I could not name afterwards. Going forward I am putting the two hours after my shift into one thing with everything else closed, because that is the only block I actually control.

    From the physics loop — I changed one number at a time and still could not say why the result moved. Going forward I write down what I expect to happen before I press run, so I find out whether I understood it or guessed.

    Reflection

    What did you assume about yourself that turned out not to be true?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It refers to something that actually happened in one of the two Sprint 0 activities.
    • It names one thing you will do differently, specific enough to start this week.
  2. 2.Identify the physical behavior you want to understand. Take it from the interest you wrote about this week, and write it as a question: Why does ___ lead to ___?
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Start from what you already watch. Write down three moments in your interest where something physical happens that you would like to be able to call in advance — the ball curves, the bike holds the corner, the battery dies early. Turn each one into a Why does ___ lead to ___? question, then widen it: what else could you change, and what else changes when you do? Cross out any you can already answer, and any that would take a whole course. Of what is left, pick the one you would most mind getting wrong.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    From mountain biking — Why does leaning further into a turn keep the bike from sliding out?
    From music — Why does hitting a drum harder make it louder but not higher?
    From a phone that dies on a cold morning — Why does cold weather drain a battery faster?

    Reflection

    Which of your three questions would bother you most to get wrong?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names one thing you will do differently, based on the Sprint 0 activities.
    • The behavior is written as a Why does ___ lead to ___? question.
    • It comes from the interest you wrote about this week.
    • It is one behavior, not a whole field.
  3. 3.Explain how understanding this will help you, or make things better for you personally. Answer from your own interest and curiosity.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    This is the question that decides whether the next four weeks are worth anything to you. Finish this sentence three different ways: If I understood this, I could ___. Keep going until one of the endings is something you would actually do. Cross out any ending that is about the grade or about finishing the course. Then write two or three sentences with the real one in them.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Biking — I ride the same descent every week and brake far too early into the corners because I do not trust the bike. If I understood what actually keeps the tyres gripping, I would know how much speed I can carry instead of guessing.

    Music — I record my own drums and the mix always sounds thin. If I understood what changes when I hit harder, I would know what I am actually controlling.

    Reflection

    Would this still matter to you if it were not an assignment?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what you personally would do or notice differently.
    • It is about your own interest, not about the grade or the course.
  4. 4.Describe your current understanding. What do you already understand about this? What are you not sure about?
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Say out loud — to a classmate, or to your Dojo — what you think is going on in this behavior and why. Write it down the way you said it. Then read it back and split it in two: the parts you could defend if someone pushed, and the parts you are not sure about. The second list is the useful one; it is what tasks 4 and 5 go after. Being unsure is the starting point of this assignment, not a problem with your answer.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    What I understand: the tyres push sideways on the ground and the ground pushes back, and that push is what turns me.

    What I am not sure about: why leaning changes how much sideways push I get, and whether my weight matters more than my speed.

    Reflection

    What is the one thing you are least sure about — and why do you think that is?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what you already understand about this behavior.
    • It says what you are not sure about.
    • What you are unsure about is something tasks 4 and 5 could go after.
  5. 5.Write down how you will confirm that you have actually learned it — the problem (predicting a physical behavior for a given input condition) you will be able to solve and explain step by step to a ten-year-old. Your prediction has to include a quantitative component: the quantities involved, the mathematical relationship between them, and what happens to one when the other changes.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Set the bar now, before the work starts, so that at the end of the sprint you are measured against your own standard rather than a surprise one. Picture the last day: someone hands you a situation in your behavior and asks what happens. Write the kind of problem that would be — which conditions you would be given, and what you would work out. Then check it two ways. Could you tell whether you got it right, by running a simulation, taking a measurement, or comparing against a case where the answer is already known? And could a ten-year-old follow your explanation, step by step, without any of the words you have not explained to them? Your prediction has to be quantitative, not just directional: name the quantities, write the relationship between them, and say what happens to one when you change the other. “It slides sooner” is not yet a prediction; “the sideways force needed grows with the square of the speed” is.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Biking — Given a rider’s speed and how far they are leaning, I will work out whether the tyres hold or slide. The sideways force needed to hold the turn grows with the square of the speed and shrinks as the turn gets wider (v²/r), while the grip available is the friction coefficient times the weight pressing on the tyres — so taking the same corner twice as fast asks about four times as much grip. I will check it against a simulation where I change the speed and watch where it lets go, and explain to a ten-year-old why leaning helps.

    Drums — Given how hard the stick hits, I will work out what changes in the sound and what does not. The loudness climbs with the square of how far the drum skin moves, while the pitch is set by the frequency, which the skin’s size and tension fix — so hitting harder changes the first and leaves the second alone. I will check it by recording two hits and measuring both, and explain to a ten-year-old why the note does not go higher.

    Reflection

    Could you tell, honestly, whether you got the answer right?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names one specific problem: the conditions you would be given, and what you would work out.
    • The prediction names the quantities and the mathematical relationship between them.
    • It says how you could tell whether you got it right.
    • It says how you would explain the steps to a ten-year-old.
  6. 6.List the three resources you will learn from, with one line each on why you think it will help you learn. The AI Dojo is a default resource for everyone and does not count as one of the three.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Start from your question, not from a search box: type the question itself, in your own words. Look for one of each kind — a textbook chapter or an OpenStax section, a simulation or lab you can run, and one explainer, whether a video, an article or a person. Then spend ten minutes on each: who made it, who they made it for, and does anyone you would trust point to it or agree with it? Try it for twenty minutes on your own question before you keep it — a resource that is about your behavior but never gets to your question has not earned a place.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    OpenStax College Physics, the section on circular motion — my question sits in that chapter and the worked examples use the same quantities I would need.

    A PhET simulation of friction on a slope — I can change one thing at a time and watch what happens, which is how I actually learn.

    Marco, who races downhill — he has explained cornering to me before, and he can tell me when the physics does not match what happens on the trail.

    Reflection

    Which of your three would you drop first if you ran out of time?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Three resources are listed.
    • None of them is the AI Dojo.
    • Each has one line on why you think it will help you learn.

Criteria

Check it before you submit — the same list your partner runs in week 4
  • It names one thing you will do differently, based on the Sprint 0 activities.
  • The behavior is written as a Why does ___ lead to ___? question.
  • It says what understanding this would change for you personally.
  • It says what you understand now, and what you are not sure about.
  • It names the problem you will be able to solve, the quantities and the relationship between them, and who you will explain it to.
  • It lists three resources, none of them the AI Dojo, each with a line on why it will help you learn.

What makes it strong — the change in 1 comes from something that actually happened in Sprint 0, not a general resolution; the behavior in 2 is one you can work on in four weeks, not a whole field; the reason in 3 is personal and specific, something you would do or notice differently, rather than a general claim about your career; what you are unsure about in 4 is the same thing 5 and 6 go after, so the three answers point the same way; the problem in 5 is one a ten-year-old could follow you through, and one you could tell whether you got right; the resources in 6 are ones you have actually opened.

Grading — 100 points, in Graded assignments. You will be asked to explain this to a peer, the TA and/or the instructor, so make sure you are comfortable with the responses you have developed for this assignment.

How this page was made

The purpose and the five tasks are Prof. Sathya’s, word for word, settled on 8 September 2026. The guidance, the examples, the reflection questions and the notes behind the AI feedback button were drafted by AI from that design; he reads them and edits by hand where they miss.

What that looks like — what he decided, what AI built, and how it is checked

What he decided

  • The purpose and the six tasks are his wording from 8 September 2026. Task 1 is the sprint-opening look back — every sprint’s first graded item starts with one. Nothing on this page rewrites, reorders or adds to them.
  • The two layers. The tasks stand on their own; everything under a + is optional. A student who copies the tasks into a document and answers them there has done the whole assignment.
  • A behavior, not an academic problem. The object of this goal is something physical you already care about and want to be able to predict. Where the word problem appears in task 4 it means predicting a physical behavior for a given input condition.
  • Uncertainty is the starting point. Task 3 asks what you are not sure about because that is what tasks 4 and 5 go after. It is never counted against you.
  • The check-before-you-submit list is one list, two uses — you run it on your own draft, and your partner runs the same list on theirs in the week-4 session.

What AI built

  • The guidance under each + — a process for thinking the question through, rather than more explanation of it.
  • The examples — more than one per task, so that no single one becomes the thing to copy. They are illustrations, not answers.
  • The reflection questions and the criteria notes the AI feedback button reads from.

How it is checked

  • Every page is written against the course’s own writing rules and then read by him; where a line misses, he edits it directly.
  • The AI feedback button is held to the observable criteria for each box. It never judges you, never rewrites your answer, and never hands you wording.
  • The Check it yourself list under each box is the same criteria without the AI, so the button is never the only route.

What you’ll do

  • Run its model and watch the first attempt fail the mission — that failure is your starting point.

What done and good look like

Done is all three stages carrying a prediction and an explanation in your words, the final reflection answering its four questions, and the summary pasted into Canvas.

Good is when your predictions are committed before you run — right or wrong, and the wrong ones you then chase down are the best ones on the page; when your explanations identifies a quantity (a force, a speed, an angle) and point at what the graph or the banner showed; and when your stage-3 answer includes something the model leaves out. Two examples of the level to aim for, written by us as models — not student work:

Example · a strong stage-1 explanation (the ball)The spin is what separated my ball from the ghost — same kick, and the ghost crossed 5 m out while mine bent back inside the post. The drift graph kept curving instead of jumping once, so the sideways force must act the whole flight, not just at the kick. That matches the Magnus line in the code: the force depends on the velocity, which is always there.
Example · a strong stage-2 explanation (the phone)At trust 0.98 the arrow held with a 2-degree wobble even though both sensors were lying. The gyro’s 98% carried the fast motion because shaking never enters it, and the accelerometer’s 2% slowly pulled the drift back out. At 1.0 the drift had nothing correcting it and the banner showed 24 degrees — two per second, for twelve seconds.

2 · Meet it — why a spinning ball curves

A free kick, 24 metres from goal — and a wall of defenders 9 metres out, right in the way. It runs once as it loads: watch the straight kick smack into the wall. Your mission: bend the ball around the wall and into the net. Edit any line on the left and press Run; Reset to original brings the first version back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text The Reset to original button inside the panel puts the original code back.
What you are looking atA ball kicked at 25 m/s (about 56 mph), 15 degrees up. The yellow trail is your kick; the faint gray trail is a ghost flying the exact same kick with no spin, so the difference between the two is pure spin. A cyan arrow rides the ball showing the sideways push. The banner tells you how it ended — GOAL, blocked, wide — and the graph plots the sideways drift of both balls.
The physics in one breathA spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side that layer moves with the oncoming air, on the other side against it. The air ends up pushing harder on the slow side than on the fast side, so the ball gets a sideways shove — the Magnus force. More spin, or more speed, means a bigger shove.
The math in one lineF = F + 0.5 * rho * area * radius * cross(omega, v) — omega is the spin (how fast, and about which axis), v is the velocity, and cross makes a force at right angles to both, which is why it is sideways. The next line, ball.v = v + (F / mass) * dt, is Newton’s F = ma taken one tiny step at a time. That loop is the whole model.

3 · Three stages

Each stage is one move: predict before you run, change the marked line, watch, then explain — about 150 characters of explanation opens the next stage. To push your explanation further, use one, or both routes: writing and checking it yourself carries the thinking and is worth doing on its own; having an AI question what you wrote is a different skill, worth practising too. The graph is the referee either way — the AI can be wrong, the graph cannot. Press Run before predicting and the panel counts you down: the common mistake is running first and predicting never.

Stage 1 of 3 · Score the free kickAim: score, and say what pushed the ball sideways

Why this stage. Every free-kick taker solves this problem with their foot. You are going to solve it with the two lines that ARE the problem — where you aim, and how much you spin.

Do. Press Run as it is and watch the straight kick hit the wall. Then find aim = 0 and change it to aim = 12 — twelve degrees to the right of the goal. Predict, then Run. Then also change spin = 0 to spin = 6. Predict again, then Run.

Run and watch. Your ball against the gray ghost — same kick, no spin. The banner. And the graph: the gray line runs straight while the red one keeps bending. What does the printed line say?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Your ball and the ghost left the same boot at the same speed in the same direction. Find the one line of code that separates them.
  2. The gray drift line is straight; the red one bends more and more. What does that widening gap say about when the sideways force acts — once at the kick, or all flight long?
  3. Look at the Magnus line: cross(omega, v) makes a force at right angles to which two things? So which direction can it never push?

What the graph should show: the gray ghost drifts to about 5 m — wide of the far post by more than a metre — while the red line bends back and crosses the goal about 2 m inside the post. GOAL. And a warning from the physics: spin = 7 bends so early it clips the wall’s edge. More spin is not always better.

Stage 2 of 3 · Change the physicsAim: say what drag does, and what speed does

Why this stage. Your scoring kick uses two air forces at once — drag slowing it, Magnus bending it. Now pull them apart and find out what each one is doing for you.

Do. Keep the scoring kick (aim = 12, spin = 6). Change drag_on = True to False. Predict, then Run. Then put drag back on and try a soft kick, speed = 20. Predict, Run. Then a blast, speed = 30. Predict, Run.

Run and watch. The banner for each run, the height the ball crosses the goal line, and where each attempt ends up against the wall, the posts and the bar.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Drag is written as mag(v) * v. When the speed doubles, what happens to that term — double, or four times?
  2. The Magnus term has cross(omega, v) in it — the bend grows with speed to the first power. Put those two together: why does the soft kick sag into the wall while the blast nearly clears the bar?
  3. Gravity pulls the same on every kick. Which kick gives gravity the most time per metre to pull the ball down?

What the graph should show: without drag the same kick still scores, but crosses the line at about 1.6 m instead of 0.4 — flatter, faster, later to bend. At 20 m/s the ball sags and drops into the wall at 1.35 m. At 30 m/s it scores crossing at 2.25 m — a hand’s width under the bar.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. Every model leaves something out. Finding where it stops matching the world is how you learn what it actually contains — and it is the question you will be asked about your own models later.

Do. Set aim = 0 and spin = 60 — a number no foot can produce — predict, and Run. Then go back to the scoring kick (aim = 12, spin = 6) and change radius = 0.11 to radius = 0.02: a ping-pong ball’s size, still weighing 0.43 kg, which is deliberately impossible. Predict, and Run.

Run and watch. The trail and the banner. Does anything look physically impossible — a loop, a hover, a boomerang? And the tiny heavy ball: what happened to your goal?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. At spin = 60 the sideways force in the code is several times the ball’s weight. What does real air do around a ball spinning that fast that this formula does not know about?
  2. The formula has no upper limit on the sideways push. Real balls do. Where in the code would you put a limit, and what would you set it to?
  3. What is missing entirely — the spin slowing down during flight, wind, the ball’s seams and roughness, the bounce?

What the graph should show: at spin = 60 the ball hooks so hard it lands 17 m sideways before it even reaches the wall — a boomerang, not a free kick. The tiny heavy ball barely feels the air: no bend at all, and your scoring aim now sails wide exactly like the ghost. No air force, no free kick.

2 · Meet it — how the UAV stays up

The UAV at a steady airspeed, 100 m up — and a ridge crossing its path 600 m ahead, 135 m tall. It runs once as it loads, and the first flight hits the ridge ten metres below the top. Your mission: get it over the ridge. Edit any line on the left and press Run; Reset to original brings the first version back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text The Reset to original button inside the panel puts the original code back.
What you are looking atThe UAV at 60 m/s (about 134 mph), the camera flying alongside. The yellow arrow is lift, the red arrow is weight. The top graph is the plane’s height with the ridge top drawn as a gray line — the race between the blue curve and that line is the whole mission. The bottom graph draws lift and weight together. The banner tells you how the flight ended, to the metre.
The physics in one breathA wing tilted slightly into the oncoming air pushes air downward, and the air pushes the wing up. That push is lift. It grows with the tilt (the angle of attack) and with the square of the speed — twice the speed, four times the lift. Past about 15 degrees the air can no longer follow the top of the wing, it breaks away, and most of the lift disappears. That is a stall.
The math in one linelift = 0.5 * rho * speed**2 * wing_area * lift_coefficient(effective_angle) — air density, times speed squared, times wing area, times a number that depends on the angle. Then vy = vy + ((lift - weight) / mass) * dt is F = ma in the up-and-down direction only, one small step at a time. The effective_angle line is the clever part: a climbing wing meets the air more head-on, so its working angle drops.

Stage 1 of 3 · Clear the ridgeAim: get over, and say what two more degrees bought you

Why this stage. The first flight missed the ridge top by ten metres. Every lever you have lives in two lines of code — and the cheapest fix is smaller than you think.

Do. Press Run as it is (speed = 60, angle = 5) and watch it hit the ridge ten metres below the top. Then change angle = 5 to angle = 7. Before you press Run again, write your prediction. If you are curious what the smallest fix is, try angle = 6 too.

Run and watch. The blue height curve against the gray ridge line; where the climb settles into a straight line; and on the bottom graph, whether lift and weight ever meet.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. When the height graph settles into a straight climbing line, what is true about lift and weight at that moment? Check the bottom graph.
  2. The effective_angle line subtracts something from your angle as the plane climbs. What, and why does climbing “use up” some of your tilt?
  3. Try angle = 0 once. The wing starts with no lift at all — so why does it settle into a gentle glide instead of dropping like a stone?

What the graph should show: at angle 5 the UAV settles into a climb of about 2.5 m/s and meets the ridge at 125 m — ten below the top. At angle 7 the climb settles near 4.6 m/s and clears the top by about 13 m, with lift and weight meeting on the bottom graph the whole way. Angle 6 misses by ten centimetres. Two degrees is the whole game.

Stage 2 of 3 · Change the physicsAim: say what “speed squared” means for a pilot

Why this stage. Angle was the cheap lever. Speed is the expensive one — it sits in the lift line squared, and squared is a word until you watch it decide whether a thousand kilograms clears a ridge.

Do. Put angle = 5 back. Change speed = 60 to speed = 30. Predict, then Run. Then speed = 70. Predict, Run. Then speed = 120. Predict, Run.

Run and watch. The very first value of the lift line on the bottom graph, before the angle has time to adjust; where each flight meets the ridge; the printed margin, to the metre.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Read the first lift value at 30 m/s and at 60 m/s, same angle. What is the ratio? What does that tell you about the speed**2 in the code?
  2. At 70 m/s the UAV hit the ridge 3 m below the top; at 80 it scrapes over by about 4. Why does a third more speed buy so little when two more degrees bought 25 m?
  3. An airliner weighs three hundred times this UAV. Put that together with speed squared: what has to be true before its wheels can leave the ground?

What the graph should show: at 30 m/s the first lift value is a quarter of 60’s (about 4,700 N against 19,000 N); the UAV sinks and hits the ridge face low. At 70 it hits 3 m below the top; at 120 the lift starts at four times, and it clears by about 13 m. Speed squared works — it is just expensive.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. If more angle is cheap lift, why not take lots? Because past 15 degrees the air stops playing along. Pilots train their whole careers around this edge; the model has it built in.

Do. Set speed = 30 and angle = 14 — slow and steep, right at the edge. Predict, Run. Then change to angle = 20. Predict, Run. Then put angle back to 14 and change mass = 1000 to mass = 3000. Predict, Run.

Run and watch. The lift line on the bottom graph at the moment the stall hits; the height graph; the banner. Does the UAV ever recover on its own?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. The stall is one if line: past 15 degrees the lift drops to about a third and stays there. What does real air do — is the drop that sudden, and does anything come back?
  2. Once the UAV is falling, the working angle gets bigger, not smaller. Why does that trap it in the stall?
  3. This model has no drag, no engine and no way to pitch the nose down. Which missing piece is the one a pilot actually uses to recover?

What the graph should show: at 30 m/s and 14 degrees the UAV climbs slowly and clears the ridge by about 5 m — the slow-and-steep route works, barely. At 20 degrees the lift line collapses at once, the UAV falls, falling raises the working angle further, and the banner reads STALLED. At 3000 kg even full lift is short of the weight; it sinks past the stall and comes down long before the ridge.

2 · Meet it — how your phone knows which way is up

A phone tilting to 40 degrees and back, with a green arrow whose one job is to keep pointing straight up — using only two imperfect sensors. It runs once as it loads and the arrow holds. Your mission: shake the phone, watch the arrow get lost, then tune the software until it holds again — within 3 degrees, start to finish. Edit any line on the left and press Run; Reset to original brings the first version back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text The Reset to original button inside the panel puts the original code back.
What you are looking atThe phone follows its true tilt; the green arrow is drawn from the phone’s own estimate, so the arrow’s wobble is exactly the software’s error — it turns red the moment the error passes 3 degrees, and the banner scores the run by its biggest wobble. The graph draws four lines: true tilt (black), accelerometer alone (red), gyroscope alone (blue), and the blend the software actually uses (green).
The physics in one breathYour phone has two tiny sensors. The accelerometer is a speck of silicon on springs; gravity pulls it, so it can tell which way “down” is — but any shake moves it too, and it cannot tell a shake from gravity. The gyroscope feels how fast the phone is turning; add up rate × time and you get the angle — but a tiny built-in error, added up for seconds, becomes a big one. Each is wrong in its own way, so the phone blends them.
The math in one linetilt_acc = degrees(atan2(ax, ay)) reads the angle gravity seems to come from. tilt_gyro = tilt_gyro + (true_rate + gyro_drift) * dt is integration — adding up tiny steps — the same move the other models make with F = ma. The blend line, trust_gyro * (...) + (1 - trust_gyro) * tilt_acc, is what real phones do: mostly gyro, a little accelerometer.

Stage 1 of 3 · Break the arrowAim: say which sensor the shake fooled, and why

Why this stage. On a calm table the arrow holds all by itself — the first run proves it. Real phones do not live on calm tables. Before you can fix anything, you need to see exactly how it breaks.

Do. Press Run as it is (shake = 0, trust_gyro = 0.0) and watch the arrow hold. Then change shake = 0 to shake = 3 — a phone in a walking hand. Before you press Run again, write your prediction.

Run and watch. The arrow going red and thrashing; the banner’s “up to” number; and on the graph, which line went ragged and which stayed smooth.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Look at ax = g * sin(a) + shake * sin(40 * t). What two different things are mixed into that single number?
  2. From that one number alone, could the phone ever tell them apart? What would it need?
  3. The blue gyro line is smooth but ends off zero. How far off, and which line in the code put that error there, one small step at a time?

What the graph should show: the first run ends ARROW HELD. With shake = 3 the arrow thrashes red and the banner reads ARROW LOST, up to twenty-five degrees off, while the blue gyro line stays perfectly smooth — and still ends about six degrees off on its own account: half a degree per second, for twelve seconds.

Stage 2 of 3 · Win it backAim: hold the arrow, and say what each percent buys

Why this stage. Two sensors, each wrong in its own way — and your mission is to hold the arrow with BOTH of them lying to you. The fix is one line, and it is the line every phone, drone and rocket actually runs.

Do. Keep shake = 3 and make the gyro worse too: gyro_drift = 2. Now change trust_gyro = 0.0 to trust_gyro = 0.98. Predict, then Run. Then try trust_gyro = 1.0, predict, and Run.

Run and watch. The banner — can you get ARROW HELD with both sensors lying? The green line against the black one; what happens to the wobble number at 1.0.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. At trust_gyro = 1.0 the blend is just the gyroscope. How many degrees off is it at the end, and why does the number match drift × time?
  2. At 0.98 the small accelerometer share keeps “leaking” the truth back in. Roughly how long does it take to pull the green line back after the shake stops — a second, five, the whole run?
  3. If a phone trusted the accelerometer 100% while you walked, what would the screen do? Which sensor handles the quick moves in the blend, and which one handles the slow correction?

What the graph should show: at 0.98 the banner reads ARROW HELD with a biggest wobble near 2 degrees — mission accomplished with two lying sensors. At 1.0 the green line is smooth and wrong, ARROW LOST by about 24 degrees: two degrees per second of drift, uncorrected, for twelve seconds.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. Every model leaves something out. Your blend now holds the arrow against a walking hand — so find the conditions where even the blend loses, because that is what the model (and a real phone) is up against.

Do. Set shake = 12 — harder than any hand can shake — keeping trust_gyro = 0.98. Predict, Run. Then set shake = 0 and gyro_drift = 20, a badly broken gyroscope. Predict, Run. If you want the trade-off in one number, also try trust_gyro = 0.9 there.

Run and watch. The banner numbers. Does the blend fall apart, or just get worse? And with the broken gyro — where does the green line settle, and why does it stop there instead of running away?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. A shake of 12 m/s² is bigger than gravity itself. What does “down” even mean to the accelerometer in that moment?
  2. With a 20-degree-per-second drift, the green line settles a fixed number of degrees off instead of running away. Why does the blend stop the drift but not remove it completely? What happens to that fixed error if you change 0.98 to 0.9?
  3. Spin the phone flat on a table. Neither sensor here would notice. Which sensor in a real phone would — and what does it measure?

What the graph should show: at shake = 12 the red line swings wildly, even flipping sign, and even the blend loses the arrow — up to twenty-odd degrees. With drift = 20 the green line settles about ten degrees high and stays there: the blend pulls the error back at exactly the rate the gyro creates it. At trust 0.9 that steady error shrinks but the shake jitter comes back — the dial trades one failure for the other.

4 · One reflection on the whole loop

This is the reflection that matters — it is the one you will run on your own topic later. A paragraph or two, answering these four in any order:

Final reflectionAim: what you understand now that you did not before
  1. What did you set out to understand, and what do you understand now that you did not before?
  2. Which one change taught you the most, and why that one?
  3. Where did a prediction of yours miss, and what does the miss tell you — about the model, or about how you were thinking?

Hold onto your answer to question 4. “What would you change, add, or measure” is the first question of your own loop in Sprint 1.

Learning outcomes

Five outcomes. The + under each one opens worked examples of what that outcome looks like when it is met — written by us as models, not student work.

1. A unit. Learn one unit I did not know before — what it measures, and why the number would be meaningless without it.

Two worked examplesrad/s in the free kick, newtons in the UAV.

The free kick — rad/s. rad/s — radians per second. The Magnus formula is written in radians, so it only works if the spin is handed to it in rad/s. I think of my kick as 6 turns per second; line 43 does the conversion — omega = 2*pi*spin — which makes it about 38 rad/s. If I skipped that and passed 6 straight in, the sideways force would come out about six times too small and the ball would barely bend. The number looks fine either way; only the unit tells you which one the formula can use.

The UAV — N. N — the newton, the unit both lift and weight come out in. Weight is easy: mass * g is 1000 × 9.8 = 9,800 N. Lift comes from a completely different formula — air density, speed squared, wing area, angle — and it still lands in newtons. That is the whole point: because both are in the same unit I can put them on one graph and the question “does it climb?” becomes “is the orange curve above the red one?” On its own, 9,800 tells me nothing.

2. An explanation. Explain one of the scenarios well enough that a middle or high school student could tell me back what causes what.

Two worked examplesThe spinning ball, and the wing.

The free kick. Think of the ball as having a thin coat of air stuck to it that spins along with it. On one side, that coat is moving the same way as the wind rushing past, so the air slips by easily. On the other side it is moving against the wind, so the air piles up and pushes harder. That harder push is sideways, and it keeps going for the whole flight — which is why the ball bends the whole way instead of turning once.

The UAV. A wing meets the air at a slight angle, pushes the air down, and the air pushes the wing up. Tilt it more and it pushes more — but only up to a point. Past about 15 degrees the air cannot stay stuck to the top of the wing any more, it breaks away, and most of the lift disappears at once. So tilting up harder to climb faster is the exact thing that can drop you.

3. A relationship. Learn one new relationship between two physical quantities — when one goes up, what happens to the other, and by how much — so I can predict a number before I run it.

Two worked examplesSpin against drift, speed against lift.

The free kick — spin against drift. Same kick speed, same aim, same launch angle — the only thing I changed was the spin. At spin = 5 the ball drifted 1.3 m sideways; at spin = 10 it drifted 2.4 m. Doubling the spin doubled the drift, so drift is proportional to spin. That means I can predict before I run it: spin = 7 should give about 1.8 m.

The UAV — speed against lift. Same angle of attack, same mass, same wing — the only thing I changed was the speed. At speed = 60 the lift graph starts at about 18,900 N; at speed = 120 it starts at about 75,800 N. Twice the speed gave four times the lift, so lift goes with speed squared. That predicts speed = 90 should start at about 42,600 N.

4. A line of code (optional). Optional: explain one line of the simulation code — what it does, and what would break in the rest of the simulation if it were removed.

Two worked examplesOne line from each model.

The free kick. b.v = v + (F / mass) * dt — F = ma taken one tiny step at a time: divide the total force by the mass to get the acceleration, multiply by the 0.005 s step to get the change in velocity, add it on. Take this line out and every force calculated above it is still calculated and then ignored — the ball would fly straight at 25 m/s and land in exactly the same place as the ghost.

The UAV. vy = vy + ((lift - weight) / mass) * dt — the whole question of whether the UAV climbs, in one line. Lift minus weight is the force left over, dividing by mass turns it into an acceleration, and multiplying by the 0.02 s step turns that into a small change in vertical speed. It is why the plane does not jump the instant lift beats weight, and why the height graph curves instead of stepping. Take the line out and vy stays 0 forever — the UAV flies dead level at 100 m and runs into the ridge face whatever angle or speed I pick.

5. The method — the loop I will run on my own topic later.

  1. Pick one thing in the simulation I am curious to experiment with.
  2. Write down what will happen if I made one change.
  3. Make the change and run it.
  4. Analyze what happened in the simulation, and what data represents that.
  5. Figure out, learn and explain how the change I made led to this result.
  6. Use the learning to tweak the experiment and try again.
One worked exampleThe full run, on the free kick — including a prediction that missed and was then chased down.

1 · Pick one thing I am curious to experiment with. The wall is 9 m out and blocks anything under 2 m. I want to know whether I can beat it with spin alone, without aiming away from the goal — so I am changing spin and leaving aim = 0.

2 · Write down what will happen if I made one change. Spin bends the ball sideways, so with enough of it the ball should swing around the end of the wall and come back in. I will try spin = 6. I expect it to miss the wall and go in.

3 · Make the change and run it. Set spin = 6, pressed Run. BLOCKED — hit the wall at 1.4 m up, 0.9 m from its centre.

4 · Analyze what happened, and what data represents that. The banner says blocked 0.9 m from the wall’s centre, and the wall reaches 1.5 m out. On the drift graph my red curve is nearly flat at the start and only climbs later; at the wall — about 0.37 s in — it had moved only about 0.2 m sideways. The ball had not drifted far enough yet.

5 · Figure out, learn and explain how the change led to this result. The drift graph is a curve, not a straight line. The sideways force produces an acceleration, and distance under a steady acceleration grows with time squared, so most of the bend happens late. The wall is only about a third of the way through the flight, so the ball has only about a seventh of the drift it will have by the goal line. Spin does not move the ball sideways early; it moves it sideways by the end.

6 · Use the learning to tweak the experiment and try again. So spin alone will not clear the wall. The ball has to start on a path outside the wall and bend back. Next run I keep spin = 6 and set aim = 12, and I predict it passes the wall about half a metre wide and drifts back inside the post by the goal line.

What you are looking atA ball kicked at 25 m/s (about 56 mph), 15 degrees up. The yellow trail is your kick; the faint gray trail is a ghost flying the exact same kick with no spin, so the difference between the two is pure spin. A cyan arrow rides the ball showing the sideways push. The banner tells you how it ended — GOAL, blocked, wide — and the graph plots the sideways drift of both balls.
The physics in one breathA spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side that layer moves with the oncoming air, on the other side against it. The air ends up pushing harder on the slow side than on the fast side, so the ball gets a sideways shove — the Magnus force. More spin, or more speed, means a bigger shove.
The math in one lineF = F + 0.5 * rho * area * radius * cross(omega, v) — omega is the spin (how fast, and about which axis), v is the velocity, and cross makes a force at right angles to both, which is why it is sideways. The next line, b.v = v + (F / mass) * dt, is Newton’s F = ma taken one tiny step at a time. That loop is the whole model.

Meet it — how the UAV stays up

The UAV at a steady airspeed, 100 m up — and a ridge crossing its path 600 m ahead, 135 m tall. It runs once as it loads, and the first flight hits the ridge ten metres below the top. Your mission: get it over the ridge. Edit any line on the left and press Run; Reset to original brings the first version back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text The Reset to original button inside the panel puts the original code back.
What you are looking atThe UAV at 60 m/s (about 134 mph), the camera flying alongside. The yellow arrow is lift, the red arrow is weight. The top graph is the plane’s height with the ridge top drawn as a gray line — the race between the blue curve and that line is the whole mission. The bottom graph draws lift and weight together. The banner tells you how the flight ended, to the metre.
The physics in one breathA wing tilted slightly into the oncoming air pushes air downward, and the air pushes the wing up. That push is lift. It grows with the tilt (the angle of attack) and with the square of the speed — twice the speed, four times the lift. Past about 15 degrees the air can no longer follow the top of the wing, it breaks away, and most of the lift disappears. That is a stall.
The math in one linelift = 0.5 * rho * speed**2 * wing_area * lift_coefficient(effective_angle) — air density, times speed squared, times wing area, times a number that depends on the angle. Then vy = vy + ((lift - weight) / mass) * dt is F = ma in the up-and-down direction only, one small step at a time. The effective_angle line is the clever part: a climbing wing meets the air more head-on, so its working angle drops.

Meet it — how your phone knows which way is up

A phone tilting to 40 degrees and back, with a green arrow whose one job is to keep pointing straight up — using only two imperfect sensors. It runs once as it loads and the arrow holds. Your mission: shake the phone, watch the arrow get lost, then tune the software until it holds again — within 3 degrees, start to finish. Edit any line on the left and press Run; Reset to original brings the first version back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text The Reset to original button inside the panel puts the original code back.
What you are looking atThe phone follows its true tilt; the green arrow is drawn from the phone’s own estimate, so the arrow’s wobble is exactly the software’s error — it turns red the moment the error passes 3 degrees, and the banner scores the run by its biggest wobble. The graph draws four lines: true tilt (black), accelerometer alone (red), gyroscope alone (blue), and the blend the software actually uses (green).
The physics in one breathYour phone has two tiny sensors. The accelerometer is a speck of silicon on springs; gravity pulls it, so it can tell which way “down” is — but any shake moves it too, and it cannot tell a shake from gravity. The gyroscope feels how fast the phone is turning; add up rate × time and you get the angle — but a tiny built-in error, added up for seconds, becomes a big one. Each is wrong in its own way, so the phone blends them.
The math in one linetilt_acc = degrees(atan2(ax, ay)) reads the angle gravity seems to come from. tilt_gyro = tilt_gyro + (true_rate + gyro_drift) * dt is integration — adding up tiny steps — the same move the other models make with F = ma. The blend line, trust_gyro * (...) + (1 - trust_gyro) * tilt_acc, is what real phones do: mostly gyro, a little accelerometer.

Stage 1 of 3 · Score the free kickAim: score, and say what pushed the ball sideways

Why this stage. Every free-kick taker solves this problem with their foot. You are going to solve it with the two lines that ARE the problem — where you aim, and how much you spin.

Do. Press Run as it is and watch the straight kick hit the wall. Then find aim = 0 and change it to aim = 12 — twelve degrees to the right of the goal. Predict, then Run. Then also change spin = 0 to spin = 6. Predict again, then Run.

Run and watch. Your ball against the gray ghost — same kick, no spin. The banner. And the graph: the gray line runs straight while the red one keeps bending. What does the printed line say?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Your ball and the ghost left the same boot at the same speed in the same direction. Find the one line of code that separates them.
  2. The gray drift line is straight; the red one bends more and more. What does that widening gap say about when the sideways force acts — once at the kick, or all flight long?
  3. Look at the Magnus line: cross(omega, v) makes a force at right angles to which two things? So which direction can it never push?

What the graph should show: the gray ghost drifts to about 5 m — wide of the far post by more than a metre — while the red line bends back and crosses the goal about 2 m inside the post. GOAL. And a warning from the physics: spin = 7 bends so early it clips the wall’s edge. More spin is not always better.

Stage 1 of 3 · Clear the ridgeAim: get over, and say what two more degrees bought you

Why this stage. The first flight missed the ridge top by ten metres. Every lever you have lives in two lines of code — and the cheapest fix is smaller than you think.

Do. Press Run as it is (speed = 60, angle = 5) and watch it hit the ridge ten metres below the top. Then change angle = 5 to angle = 7. Before you press Run again, write your prediction. If you are curious what the smallest fix is, try angle = 6 too.

Run and watch. The blue height curve against the gray ridge line; where the climb settles into a straight line; and on the bottom graph, whether lift and weight ever meet.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. When the height graph settles into a straight climbing line, what is true about lift and weight at that moment? Check the bottom graph.
  2. The effective_angle line subtracts something from your angle as the plane climbs. What, and why does climbing “use up” some of your tilt?
  3. Try angle = 0 once. The wing starts with no lift at all — so why does it settle into a gentle glide instead of dropping like a stone?

What the graph should show: at angle 5 the UAV settles into a climb of about 2.5 m/s and meets the ridge at 125 m — ten below the top. At angle 7 the climb settles near 4.6 m/s and clears the top by about 13 m, with lift and weight meeting on the bottom graph the whole way. Angle 6 misses by ten centimetres. Two degrees is the whole game.

Stage 1 of 3 · Break the arrowAim: say which sensor the shake fooled, and why

Why this stage. On a calm table the arrow holds all by itself — the first run proves it. Real phones do not live on calm tables. Before you can fix anything, you need to see exactly how it breaks.

Do. Press Run as it is (shake = 0, trust_gyro = 0.0) and watch the arrow hold. Then change shake = 0 to shake = 3 — a phone in a walking hand. Before you press Run again, write your prediction.

Run and watch. The arrow going red and thrashing; the banner’s “up to” number; and on the graph, which line went ragged and which stayed smooth.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Look at ax = g * sin(a) + shake * sin(40 * t). What two different things are mixed into that single number?
  2. From that one number alone, could the phone ever tell them apart? What would it need?
  3. The blue gyro line is smooth but ends off zero. How far off, and which line in the code put that error there, one small step at a time?

What the graph should show: the first run ends ARROW HELD. With shake = 3 the arrow thrashes red and the banner reads ARROW LOST, up to twenty-five degrees off, while the blue gyro line stays perfectly smooth — and still ends about six degrees off on its own account: half a degree per second, for twelve seconds.

4 · Stage 2 — the relationship

Stage 2 of 2 · The same quantity, a second valueAim: state how two quantities move together, and predict with it

Why this stage. One run tells you what happened. The same quantity at a second value tells you how two quantities move together — and that is what lets you predict a number before you run it.

Do. Go back to the model. Find the quantity you changed in stage 1 — if you changed two, pick the one whose effect you want to pin down, and leave the other where it is — and set it to a second value — larger or smaller, your choice. Leave every other line exactly as it is: the two runs only compare if nothing else moved. Predict, then Run. Keep both pairs of numbers — the value you set each time, and the number the run gave back.

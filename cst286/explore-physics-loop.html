Explore Physics Loop

One round of the loop this course runs on · about 40–60 minutes · laptop, not phone

Why this exists

Everything you will do in this course comes back to one loop: take something you wonder about, get a model of it running, change the model, say what you expect before you look, watch what actually happens, and then explain it in your own words — well enough that someone could push back. That is what physicists do, it is what engineers do, and it is what you will be doing on your own topic in a few weeks. This page is one round of that loop with the model already built for you, so the only thing you have to bring is curiosity and honesty about what you see.

Nothing here is graded. What you write is for you, and the summary you copy at the end goes into the Week 2 own-your-progress box on Canvas so I can see how the loop is working for you and make it better. Do it in one sitting if you can; it takes about the length of a movie’s first act.

2 · Meet it — why a spinning ball curves

Below is a running model of a soccer ball kicked hard along the white line, seen from just behind the kicker. Press Run (the play button) to watch it once as it is. You can edit any line of the code in the left panel and press Run again; nothing you break is permanent — reloading this page puts the original back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text Reset: reload this page.
What you are looking atA ball kicked at 25 m/s, 15 degrees up, along the white line. The yellow trail is its path. The graph plots how far the ball has drifted sideways as time passes. With spin = 0 the trail stays on the line and the graph stays flat. The line printed under the graph tells you where it landed.
The physics in one breathA spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side that layer moves with the oncoming air, on the other side against it. The air ends up pushing harder on the slow side than on the fast side, so the ball gets a sideways shove — the Magnus force. More spin, or more speed, means a bigger shove.
The math in one lineF = F + 0.5 * rho * area * radius * cross(omega, v) — omega is the spin (how fast, and about which axis), v is the velocity, and cross makes a force at right angles to both, which is why it is sideways. The next line, ball.v = v + (F / mass) * dt, is Newton’s F = ma taken one tiny step at a time. That loop is the whole model.

Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Did the ball curve to the left or the right of the line? What single change in the code would flip it?
  2. Is the graph a straight line or does it bend more and more? What does that say about whether the sideways push is a one-time shove or keeps acting the whole flight?
  3. Look at the Magnus line: the force is at right angles to which two things? So which direction can it never push?

What the graph should show: with spin = 5 the red line bends away from zero and keeps bending — the ball lands one to two metres off the line, to the left as seen from behind the kicker. Negative spin sends it the other way.

Stage 2 of 3 · Change the physicsAim: a cause-and-effect sentence that names a force

Why this stage. One number changed the picture. Now change a rule. You will take the air out of the model and find out which parts of the flight belong to gravity and which belong to the air.

Do. Set spin = 10. Find drag_on = True and change it to drag_on = False. Predict, then Run. Then put drag back on, change speed = 25 to speed = 12 (a soft pass), predict again, and Run.

Run and watch. The landing x and z that the model prints for each run; how high the graph gets; how sharply the trail bends compared with how far the ball travelled.

Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Drag is written as mag(v) * v. When the speed doubles, what happens to that term — double, or four times?
  2. The Magnus term has cross(omega, v) in it. Does the sideways force grow with speed, shrink with it, or ignore it?
  3. Divide sideways drift z by distance x for the hard kick and for the soft one. Which ball curved more per metre travelled? Why does a slow ball have more time to be pushed over each metre?

What the graph should show: without drag the ball flies further (about 30 m instead of about 24) and the drift is a little larger. The soft kick lands much closer and drifts fewer metres in total, but its path bends more sharply for the distance it covers.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. Every model leaves something out. Finding where it stops matching the world is how you learn what it actually contains — and it is the question you will be asked about your own models later.

Do. Set spin = 60 — a number no foot can produce — predict, and Run. Then put spin back to 10 and change radius = 0.11 to radius = 0.02: a ping-pong ball’s size, still weighing 0.43 kg, which is deliberately impossible. Predict, and Run.

Run and watch. The trail and the printed numbers. Does anything look physically impossible — a loop, a hover, a boomerang? What did the tiny heavy ball do?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. At spin = 60 the sideways force in the code is several times the ball’s weight. What does real air do around a ball spinning that fast that this formula does not know about?
  2. The formula has no upper limit on the sideways push. Real balls do. Where in the code would you put a limit, and what would you set it to?
  3. What is missing entirely — the spin slowing down during flight, wind, the ball’s seams and roughness, the bounce?

What the graph should show: at spin = 60 the trail swings round in a wide hook and the drift graph races off — a boomerang, not a free kick. The tiny heavy ball barely feels the air at all: almost no drag, almost no curve, a plain arc.

2 · Meet it — how a plane stays up

Below is a running model of a wing flying to the right at a steady airspeed, starting 100 m up. Press Run (the play button) to watch it once as it is. You can edit any line of the code in the left panel and press Run again; nothing you break is permanent — reloading this page puts the original back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text Reset: reload this page.
What you are looking atA white wing with a body, flying right at 60 m/s. The yellow arrow is lift, the red arrow is weight. The top graph is the plane’s height; the bottom graph draws lift and weight together so you can see when they match and when they do not. The line printed under the graphs tells you how fast it is climbing or sinking at the end.
The physics in one breathA wing tilted slightly into the oncoming air pushes air downward, and the air pushes the wing up. That push is lift. It grows with the tilt (the angle of attack) and with the square of the speed — twice the speed, four times the lift. Past about 15 degrees the air can no longer follow the top of the wing, it breaks away, and most of the lift disappears. That is a stall.
The math in one linelift = 0.5 * rho * speed**2 * wing_area * lift_coefficient(effective_angle) — air density, times speed squared, times wing area, times a number that depends on the angle. Then vy = vy + ((lift - weight) / mass) * dt is F = ma in the up-and-down direction only, one small step at a time. The effective_angle line is the clever part: a climbing wing meets the air more head-on, so its working angle drops.

  1. At angle 0 the lift starts at zero — so where did the lift on the graph come from once the plane began to fall?
  2. When the height graph settles into a straight downhill line, what is true about lift and weight at that moment?
  3. Which way is “into the wind” for a wing that is sinking? Why does sinking raise the working angle?

What the graph should show: at angle 5 the plane climbs and settles into a steady climb of about 2.5 m/s, with lift and weight meeting on the bottom graph. At angle 0 it sinks at first, lift appears as it sinks, and it settles into a steady glide down at about 2.5 m/s — again with lift equal to weight.

Stage 2 of 3 · Change the physicsAim: say what “speed squared” means for a pilot

Why this stage. Speed sits in the lift line squared. Squared is a word until you watch what it does to a thousand kilograms of aeroplane.

Do. Put angle = 5 back. Change speed = 60 to speed = 30. Predict, then Run. Then change it to speed = 120, predict, and Run.

Run and watch. The very first value of the lift line on the bottom graph, before the angle has time to adjust; then the height graph; then the printed climb or sink speed.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Read the first lift value at 30 m/s and at 60 m/s, same angle. What is the ratio? What does that tell you about the speed**2 in the code?
  2. At 30 m/s, is there any angle below the stall that makes enough lift for 1000 kg? Try 14. What is the slowest speed that still holds height at angle 14? (Pilots call it the stall speed.)
  3. An airliner weighs three hundred times this plane. Put that together with speed squared: why is a runway two kilometres long?

What the graph should show: at 30 m/s the first lift value is a quarter of what it was at 60 (about 4,700 N against 19,000 N), well below the weight, and the plane settles into a sink of about 3 m/s. At 120 m/s the lift starts at four times, the plane climbs hard and settles into a climb near 9 m/s. At angle 14, height holds down to about 25 m/s and not below.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. Every model leaves something out. Pilots train for the edge of the envelope; this model has one edge built in, and it is worth finding what is on the other side of it.

Do. Set speed = 30 and angle = 14. Predict, Run. Then change to angle = 20. Predict, Run. Then put angle back to 14 and change mass = 1000 to mass = 3000. Predict, Run.

Run and watch. The lift line on the bottom graph when the stall hits; the height graph; the printed sink speed. Does the plane ever recover on its own?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. The stall is one if line: past 15 degrees the lift drops to about a third and stays there. What does real air do — is the drop that sudden, and does anything come back?
  2. Once the plane is falling, the working angle gets bigger, not smaller. Why does that trap the plane in the stall?
  3. This model has no drag, no engine and no way to pitch the nose down. Which missing piece is the one a pilot actually uses to recover?

What the graph should show: at 30 m/s and 14 degrees the plane holds and climbs gently. At 20 degrees the lift line collapses at once, the plane sinks, and it never recovers — the working angle only grows as it falls. At 3000 kg and 14 degrees, even full lift is short of the weight; it sinks, the angle rises past the stall, and it falls out of the sky.

2 · Meet it — how your phone knows which way is up

Below is a running model of a phone being tilted to 40 degrees and back over twelve seconds. The dark phone is the truth. The cyan ghost is where the phone thinks it is. Press Run (the play button) to watch it once as it is. You can edit any line of the code in the left panel and press Run again; nothing you break is permanent — reloading this page puts the original back.

If the model does not load here, open it in a new tab. The code as plain text Reset: reload this page.
What you are looking atThe graph draws four lines: the true tilt (black), what the accelerometer alone would say (red), what the gyroscope alone would say (blue), and the blend a real phone uses (green). At the start the blend trusts the accelerometer completely, so the green line sits on the red one. The printed line at the end tells you how far off each answer is once the phone is back flat.
The physics in one breathYour phone has two tiny sensors. The accelerometer is a speck of silicon on springs; gravity pulls it, so it can tell which way “down” is — but any shake moves it too, and it cannot tell a shake from gravity. The gyroscope feels how fast the phone is turning; add up rate × time and you get the angle — but a tiny built-in error, added up for seconds, becomes a big one. Each is wrong in its own way, so the phone blends them.
The math in one linetilt_acc = degrees(atan2(ax, ay)) reads the angle gravity seems to come from. tilt_gyro = tilt_gyro + (true_rate + gyro_drift) * dt is integration — adding up tiny steps — the same move the other models make with F = ma. The blend line, trust_gyro * (...) + (1 - trust_gyro) * tilt_acc, is what real phones do: mostly gyro, a little accelerometer.

Do. Press Run as it is (shake = 0, gyro_drift = 0.5, trust_gyro = 0.0) and watch the four lines. Then change shake = 0 to shake = 3. Before you press Run again, write your prediction.

Run and watch. The red line; the ghost phone; and the three numbers printed at the end. Which one is jittery and which one is smooth but wrong?

Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. Look at ax = g * sin(a) + shake * sin(40 * t). What two different things are mixed into that single number?
  2. From that one number alone, could the phone ever tell them apart? What would it need?
  3. The blue gyro line is smooth but ends off zero. How far off, and which line in the code put that error there, one small step at a time?

What the graph should show: with shake = 3 the red line jitters by up to fifteen degrees or so while the blue line stays smooth; the ghost phone shivers. The blue line ends about six degrees off — half a degree per second, for twelve seconds.

Stage 2 of 3 · Change the physicsAim: say what each percent of the blend buys

Why this stage. Two sensors, each wrong in its own way. The interesting physics is not in either sensor — it is in the one line that combines them, and that line is what every phone, drone and rocket actually runs.

Do. Set shake = 3 and gyro_drift = 2. Change trust_gyro = 0.0 to trust_gyro = 0.98. Predict, then Run. Then set trust_gyro = 1.0, predict, and Run.

Run and watch. The green line against the black one; the ghost phone; the printed numbers at the end.

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. At trust_gyro = 1.0 the blend is just the gyroscope. How many degrees off is it at the end, and why does the number match drift × time?
  2. At 0.98 the small accelerometer share keeps “leaking” the truth back in. Roughly how long does it take to pull the green line back after the shake stops — a second, five, the whole run?
  3. If a phone trusted the accelerometer 100% while you walked, what would the screen do? Which sensor handles the quick moves in the blend, and which one handles the slow correction?

What the graph should show: at 0.98 the green line tracks the black one closely with only a small shiver, and ends within a degree or two. At 1.0 the green line is smooth and wrong, ending about 24 degrees off — two degrees per second for twelve seconds.

Stage 3 of 3 · Find the edgeAim: say what the model leaves out

Why this stage. Every model leaves something out. Finding where the blend fails is how you learn what it is really doing — and what a real phone has to add on top.

Do. Set shake = 12 — harder than any hand can shake — with trust_gyro = 0.98. Predict, Run. Then set shake = 0 and gyro_drift = 20, a badly broken gyroscope. Predict, Run.

Run and watch. The green line and the ghost phone. Does the blend fall apart, or just get worse? Where does the green line settle with the broken gyro?

Works once you have written about 150 characters, and again after you revise.
Check yourself (Without using AI)The three questions a coach would ask about this stage, and what the graph should show.
  1. A shake of 12 m/s² is bigger than gravity itself. What does “down” even mean to the accelerometer in that moment?
  2. With a 20-degree-per-second drift, the green line settles a fixed number of degrees off instead of running away. Why does the blend stop the drift but not remove it completely? What happens to that fixed error if you change 0.98 to 0.9?
  3. Spin the phone flat on a table. Neither sensor here would notice. Which sensor in a real phone would — and what does it measure?

What the graph should show: at shake = 12 the red line swings wildly, even flipping sign, and the green line gets noisy but stays roughly near the truth because the gyro carries it. With drift = 20 the green line settles about ten degrees high and stays there: the blend is pulling the error back at the same rate the gyro is creating it.

4 · One reflection on the whole loop

You have now run the loop three times on one thing. This is the reflection that matters, because it is the one you will run on your own topic later in the semester. Write it in one go — a paragraph or two — answering these four, in any order:

Final reflectionAim: what you understand now that you did not before

  3. Where did a prediction of yours miss, and what does the miss tell you — about the model, or about how you were thinking?

Check yourself (Without using AI)Three questions to hold your reflection against.

  3. Would a classmate reading this be able to tell what you actually changed, and what you actually saw?

Specific, Measurable Goal — with initial plan

Week 4 · due Friday 18 September, 11:59 p.m. · Own your progress · not graded

Purpose

Start with the work you did in class (if you missed class, you can answer each of the questions for the first time here), and develop a more detailed plan for achieving the learning goal you have set for yourself.

Tasks

If you would like to work on the assignment in a Google doc without the guidance from this assignment, you can paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it. The guidance structure provided in this page is the recommended approach to ensure you are being guided and challenged along the way.

Each + opens guidance, examples, a reflection question and a box to write in. The guidance and reflection are provided to support you to do a high quality job of answering the questions.

  1. 1.Type up your sheet. Section A as it stands now, including anything you changed after your partner’s questions. Then Section B: the two questions your partner chose, and their notes on your answers.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Write it so that someone who was not in the room could run the test on you: the goal in one line, what you would explain and demonstrate to the lab, how the lab would check that you actually know it, and where you are learning it from. Then the two questions your partner chose, and what they wrote about your answers. If a question showed you something missing from Section A, the typed version is where you fix it.

    If you missed class. Open the sheet (Google Doc) and fill in Section A. For Section B, find a partner who also missed class and question each other — or be your own partner for this entry only: write the questions the lab would ask you, choose the two best, and answer them.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Section A, item 2, as typed — Given a rider’s speed and the radius of the turn, I will work out whether the tyres hold or slide: the sideways force needed grows with v²/r, the grip available is the friction coefficient times the weight on the tyres, so the same corner at twice the speed asks about four times the grip. Section B, Q1 — “What happens to your answer on a wet road?” My partner noted that I could name the friction coefficient but not say where I would get its wet-road value.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Section A is complete: goal, what you would explain and demonstrate, how the lab would check, sources.
    • Section B has the two questions your partner chose and their notes on your answers.
    • Someone who was not in the room could run the test from what is written.

    Reflection

    What changed in Section A because of your partner’s questions?

  2. 2.My learning plan. Between now and the Sprint 1 Exam on 30 September, what will you do each week to demonstrate your learning to yourself and to others? Open your calendar and block the time now; write the days and hours here.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Work backwards from the exam. Week 6 is the exam itself; week 5 is where you give the explanation out loud and your partner questions it; this week is where the explanation gets built. For each week, decide what you will be able to show at the end of it that you cannot show now — then put the hours that make that true into your calendar before you write them here. A block names the day, the hour, the place, and the one thing you will do in it. “Study physics this week” is not a block.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Week 4 — Tue 7–8 pm, library: work through the torque-to-acceleration chain by hand, then check it against the simulation. Thu 6–7 pm, home: explain it out loud to my roommate and write down where I got stuck. Week 5 — Mon 5–6 pm: redo the chain with the numbers changed, before Wednesday’s interview. Sat 10–11 am: write the ten-year-old version.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • Each week to the exam has at least one block.
    • Each block names the day, the hour, the place, and the one thing you will do.
    • The blocks are in your calendar, not only on this page.

    Reflection

    Which block is the one you are most likely to skip, and what would make you keep it?

  3. 3.My main obstacle. Name the main obstacle that could stop you from reaching your goal.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    There is usually one thing that actually stops you — a job that eats the evenings, a gap in the math you have been stepping around, a habit of reading instead of doing. Name the one that has stopped you before on work like this. If you cannot choose, look at the block in task 2 you are most likely to skip; whatever would make you skip it is the obstacle.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Time — I close at work three nights a week and get home at 11; the plan only works if the physics happens before my shift, and it never has. Math — I can follow v²/r when someone shows me, but I cannot set it up from the situation myself, so I stop when there is no worked example to copy.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names one obstacle, not a list.
    • It says how the obstacle shows up in practice.

    Reflection

    Has this obstacle stopped you before? What happened that time?

  4. 4.How I will address it. One concrete move you will make against that obstacle, and when.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    A move is something you do, at a time, that changes the odds — not a resolution to try harder. It should be small enough to happen this week and specific enough that your partner could ask on Wednesday whether it happened. If the obstacle is time, the move is a block that survives the week. If it is a gap in the math, the move is a named person, page or Dojo session where you set the problem up yourself. Then paste your plan into your AI Dojo and ask it what a physics lab would say is missing; bring what it finds back here.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Against time — Tuesday and Thursday 7–8 am, before work, at the kitchen table, phone in the other room; I told my partner so she can ask me on Wednesday. Against the math gap — Thursday’s block is me setting up the turn problem from scratch with the textbook closed, then checking it against the OpenStax worked example; if I cannot, that is what I bring to the Dojo Friday.

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It is one concrete move, not a resolution.
    • It has a day and a time.
    • Someone else could tell whether it happened.

    Reflection

    If you do this move once, what would tell you it worked?

Criteria

Check it before you submit — the same list your partner can run
  • Sections A and B are typed in full, and a stranger could run the test from them.
  • Every week to the exam has at least one calendar block with a day, hour, place and one thing to do.
  • One main obstacle is named, with how it shows up.
  • One concrete move against it, with a day and a time.

What makes it strong — the typed Section A in 1 is better than the paper one, because the partner’s questions changed it; the blocks in 2 are in the calendar already, and the first one is this week; the obstacle in 3 is the one that has actually stopped you before; the move in 4 is small enough to happen once this week and visible enough that your partner could ask whether it did.

How this page was made

The purpose and the four tasks are Prof. Sathya’s, word for word, settled on 13 September 2026. The guidance, the examples, the reflection questions and the notes behind the AI feedback button were drafted by AI from that design; he reads them and edits by hand where they miss.

What that looks like — what he decided, what AI built, and how it is checked

What he decided

  • The purpose and the four tasks are his wording from 13 September 2026. Nothing on this page rewrites, reorders or adds to them.
  • One entry for the week. It replaces two earlier week-4 entries — a resources list and an obstacles list — so that the sheet, the plan and the obstacle sit together. Resources are the Monday graded item’s job.
  • The two layers. The tasks stand on their own; everything under a + is optional. A student who copies the tasks into a document and answers them there has done the whole assignment.
  • Missing class is not a dead end. The sheet is linked under task 1, and a student who was not in the room does Section B with another absent student or as their own partner, for this entry only.
  • The check-before-you-submit list is one list, two uses — you run it on your own draft, and your partner can run it on yours.

What AI built

  • The guidance under each + — a process for thinking the question through, rather than more explanation of it.
  • The examples — more than one per task, so that no single one becomes the thing to copy. They are illustrations, not answers.
  • The reflection questions and the criteria notes the AI feedback button reads from.

How it is checked

  • Every page is written against the course’s own writing rules and then read by him; where a line misses, he edits it directly.
  • The AI feedback button is held to the observable criteria for each box. It never judges you, never rewrites your answer, and never hands you wording.
  • The Check it yourself list under each box is the same criteria without the AI, so the button is never the only route.

CST286 · Fall 2026 · Sprint 3 · Path A · A trail you can take as your Sprint 3 goal

How fast will you spin?

Sit on a spinning chair, hold your arms out, and push off. Now pull your arms in. You speed up, and nobody pushed you. Figure skaters, divers and satellites use the same physics to control their spin, and the phone in your pocket can measure it. On this trail you learn the relationship, predict your own spin, and use your phone to check whether you were right.

A goal you can take as written

Goal: Understand how spinning objects behave differently from objects moving in a straight line, and use that to predict how a spin changes.

A quantitative relationship I will be able to use:

Given how fast something is spinning, in radians per second, and how its mass is arranged around the axis before and after the arrangement changes, as a moment of inertia in kilogram square meters,

I can predict how fast it will spin afterwards, in radians per second, as long as nothing outside twists it while the mass moves.

This is useful because the same relationship explains how skaters, divers and satellites control their spin, and it is the first step toward understanding why a spinning top or a bicycle wheel stays upright.

Copy this goal - paste it into your Sprint 3 goal as it is, or narrow it to one spinning situation you care about.

The trail has six stops. Stops 1 and 2 teach you to read and record a spin rate. Stops 3 and 4 are the two ideas the prediction depends on. At stop 5 you predict your own spin and test it, and stop 6 shows where the relationship stops working. Every stop ends with practice problems; try each one before you open its answer.

What you need: a chair that spins freely, two equal weights you can hold in each hand (full 1.5-liter water bottles work well), a tape measure, a phone, and a friend to spot you. Install the free app phyphox (iPhone and Android). If your phone has no gyroscope, phyphox will say so; pair up with someone whose phone does.

Stop 1 · Spin rate

Why this stop

Your phone does not report "fast" or "slow". It reports a number in radians per second. Before you can use that number, you need to know what a radian is and how to turn radians per second into turns per second or turns per minute.

After this stop you can

convert a spin rate between radians per second, revolutions per second and revolutions per minute, and say what the sign of a gyroscope reading means.

Watch

· youtu.be/dbOR0ccwQFs

When the clip says the fan slows by 1 rad/s, it means 1 rad/s every second, which is written rad/s².

Worked example

A ceiling fan turns at 120 revolutions per minute. What is its spin rate in radians per second?

Predict first: is the answer closer to 2, 12 or 120?

  1. One full turn is 2π radians, which is about 6.28 radians.
  2. Write where you start and where you want to end: 120 rev/min → rad/s.
  3. Line up the conversion factors so the units you do not want cancel: 120 rev/min × 2π rad/1 rev × 1 min/60 s.
  4. Revolutions cancel, then minutes, and radians per second are left.
  5. 120 × 6.28 ÷ 60 = 12.6 rad/s, which is 2 turns every second.

Units check

A radian is not a degree. One turn is 360 degrees and also 2π radians, so a reading of 6.28 means one turn per second only if the unit is radians per second. If an app reports degrees per second, the same spin shows as 360.

Detour · What is a radian?Take this detour if "radian" is new to you, or if the worked example above felt like following steps you could not explain.

  • Try it with a jar and a string (10 minutes). Cut a piece of string as long as the radius of a round jar or plate, from the center to the edge. Lay the string along the edge and mark where it ends. The angle between the two ends, seen from the center, is one radian. Keep stepping the string around the edge: a little more than six lengths fit, about 6.28, which is 2π. A bigger jar needs a longer string, but the same 6.28 lengths fit, so one full turn is 2π radians for any circle.

  • Why physics uses radians. A radian ties an angle to a distance: the distance along the edge equals the radius times the angle in radians. So a point 0.5 m from the axis, turning at 2 rad/s, travels 1 m every second. Degrees do not give you that shortcut.

  • Watch.

    · youtu.be/2-032DBce-o

    Two ladybugs turn at the same rate in radians per second, and the one farther out moves faster.

  • Practice.

    1. How many radians is a quarter turn?

      Answer try it first, then open

      2π ÷ 4 = 1.57 rad.

    2. How many degrees is one radian?

      Answer try it first, then open

      360 ÷ 6.28 = 57.3 degrees.

    3. The tip of a ceiling fan blade is 0.6 m from the center, and the fan turns at 12.6 rad/s. How fast does the tip move?

      Answer try it first, then open

      0.6 × 12.6 = 7.6 m/s.

    4. Two ladybugs sit on a record turning at 3.49 rad/s, one 0.10 m from the center and one 0.15 m out. How fast does each move?

      Answer try it first, then open

      0.35 m/s and 0.52 m/s; same spin rate, different speeds.

  • Ask your Dojo.

    "I will tell you what I think a radian is. Ask me to show it with a string and a jar, and stop me where my explanation would not work for a bigger jar."

  • If lining up conversion factors is the hard part, work through stop 1 (Units) of the accelerometer trail first, then come back.

Reading the sign

Lay your phone flat, screen up. The gyroscope's z reading is the spin about the line straight up out of the screen. A positive z means the phone turns counterclockwise as you look down at the screen; a negative z means clockwise.

Try it

Open phyphox and choose "Gyroscope (rotation rate)". Lay the phone flat on a table and leave it still for 20 seconds. Write down the largest size of the z reading you see, ignoring its sign. Your phone is not turning, so this number is roughly the size of the noise: a spin much smaller than this is hard to tell apart from no spin.

Practice

  1. A record player turns at 33⅓ revolutions per minute. What is that in radians per second?

    Answer try it first, then open

    33.33 × 2π ÷ 60 = 3.49 rad/s.

  2. On the spin cycle a washing machine drum turns at 1,200 revolutions per minute. How many turns does it make each second, and what is its spin rate in radians per second?

    Answer try it first, then open

    20 turns per second; 1,200 × 2π ÷ 60 = 126 rad/s.

  3. You spin on a chair with your phone flat against your chest, screen up, and phyphox shows z = −6.3 rad/s. How many turns per second are you making, and which way?

    Answer try it first, then open

    6.3 ÷ 6.28 ≈ 1 turn per second, clockwise as seen from above, because the reading is negative.

  4. The Earth turns once in about 86,164 seconds. What is its spin rate in radians per second? Compare it with the number you wrote down in "Try it". Could your phone feel the Earth turning?

    Answer try it first, then open

    2π ÷ 86,164 = 0.0000729 rad/s. Compare it with your own still-phone reading; if your reading is larger, the Earth's turn is lost in it.

Ask your Dojo

"Give me a spin rate in revolutions per minute from everyday life. Do not solve it. Ask me to set up the conversion to radians per second, and tell me only whether my units cancel."

"Ask me why one turn is 2π radians, and keep asking until I explain it using the distance around a circle."

Stop 2 · Record your own spin

Why this stop

Stops 3 and 4 explain the change you record here, and at stop 5 you use them to predict a new spin before you measure it. Seeing your own spin speed up first is what makes that prediction worth checking.

After this stop you can

record your spin rate with arms out and arms in, and read both numbers from a phyphox graph.

Set up

Measure the distance from the chair's axis (the post under the seat) to the center of each bottle when your arms are straight out, and again when you hold the bottles against your chest. Write both distances in meters.

Record

  1. Sit on the chair, feet off the floor. Lay the phone flat on your lap, screen up (or tape it flat to the seat between your knees), so it turns with you and both hands stay free. Press record in phyphox ("Gyroscope (rotation rate)"). In phyphox you can also turn on "Remote access" and watch the graph live in a laptop browser.Before your first real run, turn the chair slowly by hand once and check that the z line moves; if another line moves instead, your phone is tilted, so lay it flatter.
  2. Hold the bottles straight out. Your friend gives the chair a gentle turn and steps back.
  3. After two full turns, pull the bottles in to your chest and hold them there for two more turns.
  4. Put a foot down, stop, and stop the recording.

Predict first: before you record, write down how many times faster you expect to spin with the bottles in.

Read the graph

The z line has two steady parts: one with arms out and one, farther from zero, with arms in. If you spin clockwise both are negative, so use the size of each reading and ignore the sign. Write down the size of z in the middle of each steady part. These are your ω₁ and ω₂ (ω, the Greek letter omega, is the usual symbol for spin rate).

Safety

Keep the spin gentle, keep your feet ready, and stop if you feel dizzy. Your friend spots you.

Practice

  1. A student records z = 2.8 rad/s with arms out and z = 4.6 rad/s with arms in. How many times faster did she spin with her arms in?

    Answer try it first, then open

    4.6 ÷ 2.8 = 1.6 times faster.

  2. In the same recording, the arms-out part of the graph slopes slowly down instead of staying flat. Identify one thing that could be slowing the chair.

    Answer try it first, then open

    friction in the chair's bearing, air on the outstretched arms, or a foot brushing the floor. Each one twists against the spin from outside.

Ask your Dojo

"I will describe my phyphox graph. Ask me questions until we agree on which parts are arms out, arms in, and the moments in between."

3

Stop 3 · Moment of inertia

Why this stop

Pulling the bottles in did not change their mass, so something else about them changed. What changed is how far the mass sits from the axis, and the moment of inertia is the quantity that measures that.

After this stop you can

calculate the moment of inertia of masses held at a distance from an axis, and explain why distance matters more than mass.

Watch

· youtu.be/mVFrEitAnRs

Worked example

You hold two 1.5 kg water bottles 0.75 m from the chair's axis, then pull them in to 0.20 m. By how much does the bottles' moment of inertia change?

Predict first: the distance shrinks to about a quarter. Does the moment of inertia shrink to a quarter, less than a quarter, or more?

  1. For a small mass at a distance r from the axis, the moment of inertia is I = m r².
  2. With two bottles, add them: I = 2 × m × r².
  3. Arms out: I = 2 × 1.5 kg × (0.75 m)² = 1.69 kg·m².
  4. Arms in: I = 2 × 1.5 kg × (0.20 m)² = 0.12 kg·m².
  5. The distance shrank to about a quarter, but because it is squared, the moment of inertia shrank to about one fourteenth.

Units check

Put the distances in centimeters and the arms-out answer becomes 2 × 1.5 × 75² = 16,875. The number looks dramatic, but its unit is kg·cm², and every later step expects kg·m². Convert distances to meters first.

Practice

  1. Which is harder to start spinning: 1 kg held 2 m from the axis, or 4 kg held 1 m from the axis?

    Answer try it first, then open

    neither. 1 × 2² = 4 kg·m² and 4 × 1² = 4 kg·m². Doubling the distance counts as much as four times the mass.

  2. Two 25 kg children sit on the edge of a playground merry-go-round, 1.5 m from the center. What do they add to its moment of inertia?

    Answer try it first, then open

    2 × 25 × 1.5² = 112.5 kg·m².

  3. A figure skater's two arms are about 3.5 kg each. Treat each arm as a mass at its middle: 0.35 m from the axis when the arms are out, 0.10 m when they are folded in. What do the arms contribute in each position?

    Answer try it first, then open

    out: 2 × 3.5 × 0.35² = 0.86 kg·m²; in: 2 × 3.5 × 0.10² = 0.07 kg·m².

Ask your Dojo

"Ask me why a skater with her arms held out is harder to start spinning than with her arms pulled in, and keep asking until I explain it with mass and distance from the axis."

"Give me two objects with different masses and distances. Ask me to predict which has the larger moment of inertia before I calculate."

Stop 4 · Angular momentum stays the same

Why this stop

This is the relationship in the goal. Spin rate and moment of inertia trade against each other, and the trade is exact as long as nothing outside the spinning system twists it.

After this stop you can

use I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ to predict a new spin rate, and say what condition must hold for the prediction to work.

Watch

· youtu.be/orAtL67eKc8

A mass pulled in to half its distance from the axis spins four times as fast: that is I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ with I₂ a quarter of I₁.

The system and the axis

Before you use the relationship, identify what is spinning together and what it spins around. On the chair, the system is you, the chair and both bottles, and the axis is the chair's post, straight up and down.

The idea

Angular momentum is the moment of inertia times the spin rate: L = Iω. When nothing outside the system twists it (no outside torque), L stays the same. So if I gets smaller, ω has to get larger by the same factor: I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂, and ω₂ = ω₁ × I₁ ÷ I₂.

Worked example

A playground merry-go-round has a moment of inertia of 150 kg·m² on its own. It spins at 1.2 rad/s with one 25 kg child sitting at the edge, 1.5 m from the center. The child walks in to 0.3 m from the center. How fast does it spin now?

Predict first: faster or slower, and by a little or by a lot?

  1. Before: I₁ = 150 + 25 × 1.5² = 206.25 kg·m².
  2. After: I₂ = 150 + 25 × 0.3² = 152.25 kg·m².
  3. Nobody pushes the merry-go-round and we ignore the friction in its bearing, so angular momentum stays the same: ω₂ = ω₁ × I₁ ÷ I₂.
  4. ω₂ = 1.2 rad/s × 206.25 ÷ 152.25 = 1.63 rad/s.
  5. The child is only a small part of the total moment of inertia, so the speed-up is modest, about a third.

Units check

I₁ ÷ I₂ has no unit, because kg·m² divided by kg·m² cancels. So ω₂ comes out in whatever unit ω₁ was in. Put ω₁ in revolutions per second and ω₂ is in revolutions per second; you only have to be consistent.

Try it

open it in its own tab

The starter code spins a chair with two weights held out, then slides the weights in after two seconds. Before you run it, use I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ to predict the spin rate after the pull, and write it down. Run the code and compare. Then move r_end all the way to 0, predict again, and run it. (r_end = 0 is an idealized limit with all the bottle mass on the axis; a real chest stops the bottles at about 0.15 to 0.20 m.) Watch the L graph each time: the line stays flat because nothing outside twists the chair.

Practice

  1. A figure skater spins at 2.0 revolutions per second with her arms out, when her moment of inertia is 2.8 kg·m². She pulls in to 1.0 kg·m². How fast does she spin?

    Answer try it first, then open

    2.0 × 2.8 ÷ 1.0 = 5.6 revolutions per second.

  2. A diver leaves the board turning at 1.5 rad/s with a moment of inertia of 12 kg·m² in the straight position, then tucks to 3.5 kg·m². How fast does he turn in the tuck, and about how many somersaults does he complete in 1.2 s of tuck?

    Answer try it first, then open

    1.5 × 12 ÷ 3.5 = 5.1 rad/s; 5.1 × 1.2 ÷ 6.28 ≈ 1 somersault.

  3. A satellite in space turns slowly at 0.02 rad/s with its solar panels folded. It unfolds them, and its moment of inertia triples. What happens to its spin?

    Answer try it first, then open

    it slows to a third, 0.0067 rad/s. In space nothing outside twists it, so the trade is exact.

Stop 5 · Predict your own spin, then test it

Why this stop

A relationship earns your trust when it predicts something you have not measured yet. So at this stop you predict how much faster you will spin for a new arm position, write the prediction down, and only then record the spin. To predict, you need your whole moment of inertia, and the bottles are only part of it: your body and the chair count too. You cannot measure your body's moment of inertia with a scale and a tape measure, so you start from a rough estimate.

After this stop you can

predict your spin rate for a new arm position before you measure it, measure it, and explain the difference.

Worked example - predict a new position

A seated person and the chair together have a moment of inertia of about 2.0 kg·m². This estimate treats your body and arms as one fixed amount; your arms also move in when you pull the bottles in, so it is an approximation. This time the person pulls the bottles only halfway in, from 0.75 m to 0.45 m.

  1. Bottles out: I₁ = 2.0 + 1.69 = 3.69 kg·m².
  2. Bottles halfway: the bottles give 2 × 1.5 kg × (0.45 m)² = 0.61 kg·m², so I₂ = 2.0 + 0.61 = 2.61 kg·m².
  3. Before spinning, write the prediction as a ratio: ω₂ ÷ ω₁ = I₁ ÷ I₂ = 3.69 ÷ 2.61 = 1.41. You will spin about 1.41 times as fast after the pull.
  4. Start a fresh run. The phone reads ω₁ = 3.0 rad/s with the bottles out, so the prediction for this run is 3.0 × 1.41 = 4.2 rad/s.
  5. Pull the bottles halfway in, read ω₂, and compare it with 4.2 rad/s.

Your turn

  1. Choose a new position for the bottles, different from stop 2 (halfway works well), and measure its distance from the axis.
  2. Calculate I₁ and I₂ with 2.0 kg·m² for your body and the chair, and write down the predicted ratio ω₂ ÷ ω₁ before you spin.
  3. Start a fresh run, read ω₁ from it, and multiply by your ratio to get your predicted ω₂.
  4. Move the bottles to the new position, read ω₂, and compare. Then identify what could account for any difference.

This prediction and test is the check you report in Find and confirm my relationship.

Practice

  1. A student predicts a ratio of 1.41 and measures 3.0 rad/s, then 4.0 rad/s. How close was the prediction, and what could account for the difference?

    Answer try it first, then open

    the predicted ω₂ was 4.2 rad/s, so the measurement is about 6% lower. Friction in the chair's bearing twists against the spin, and if her body's moment of inertia is larger than 2.0 kg·m², the true ratio is smaller than 1.41.

  2. Why would the arms themselves moving in make the measured ω₂ come out a little higher than a prediction that leaves the arms out of the calculation?

    Answer try it first, then open

    the arms carry mass inward too, so the real I₂ is a little smaller than the calculation assumed, and a smaller I₂ means a faster spin.

Extension · Estimate your own body's moment of inertiaIf you want to go further, use your stop 2 recording to replace the rough 2.0 kg·m² with an estimate from your own data, then use it to predict your new position again.

  • The idea. In stop 2 your bottles started with 1.69 × ω₁ of angular momentum and ended with 0.12 × ω₂. The difference went to your body and the chair, whose spin rose from ω₁ to ω₂. Call their moment of inertia B.

  • Worked example. Suppose stop 2 read 3.0 rad/s and then 5.0 rad/s. The bottles went from 1.69 × 3.0 = 5.07 to 0.12 × 5.0 = 0.60 kg·m²/s, handing 4.47 kg·m²/s to your body and the chair, whose spin rose by 5.0 − 3.0 = 2.0 rad/s. So B = 4.47 ÷ 2.0 = 2.24 kg·m². This is the same as solving (B + 1.69) × 3.0 = (B + 0.12) × 5.0.

  • What B is. B is an effective estimate inside this model: it also absorbs friction, your arms moving and any error in your readings, so it is not a pure measurement of your body.

  • Practice.

    1. A student's stop 2 run read 2.5 rad/s and then 4.0 rad/s. What is her B?

      Answer try it first, then open

      the bottles went from 1.69 × 2.5 = 4.23 to 0.12 × 4.0 = 0.48, handing 3.75 kg·m²/s to her body and the chair, whose spin rose by 1.5 rad/s, so B = 3.75 ÷ 1.5 = 2.5 kg·m².

    2. If the chair's bearing has noticeable friction, will B come out too large or too small?

      Answer try it first, then open

      friction lowers the measured ω₂, and a lower ω₂ makes the calculation return a larger B, so B comes out too large.

Ask your Dojo

"I will give you my two distances and my predicted ratio. Do not calculate for me. Ask me to set up I₁ and I₂ and check only whether each step keeps the units consistent. After I measure, ask me what could account for the difference."

Stop 6 · Where it stops working, and how your phone senses a turn

Why this stop

The relationship ω₂ = ω₁ × I₁ ÷ I₂ holds only while nothing outside the system twists it. Some situations look the same and break that condition, and in one the whole system keeps its angular momentum while your own share of it changes.

After this stop you can

tell apart situations where I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ predicts correctly from ones that only look similar, and say why.

Practice - does the relationship apply?

Decide before you open each answer.

  1. While spinning with the bottles in, you let one foot drag on the floor.

    Answer decide first, then open

    no. The floor twists you from outside, so angular momentum falls and you slow down; I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂ cannot predict how much.

  2. While spinning with your arms out, you let go of both bottles. Do you speed up?

    Answer decide first, then open

    no, and this is the trap. The bottles fly off carrying their own share of the angular momentum. Your own moment of inertia and spin rate stay the same at the moment you let go. The relationship still holds for the whole system, but you cannot apply it to yourself alone.

  3. A friend pushes the chair's back while you spin.

    Answer decide first, then open

    no. The push is an outside twist.

  4. If ice sheets melt and the water spreads toward the equator, does the length of a day change?

    Answer decide first, then open

    yes, slightly longer. Mass moves farther from Earth's axis, so I grows and ω falls. Nothing outside twists the Earth enough to matter here, so the relationship applies.

Try it

open it in its own tab

This is the chair from stop 4 with two switches. First set friction_torque to 0.5 and predict whether the L line will stay flat; run it and compare. Then set friction_torque back to 0 and let_go to True: the weights are released instead of pulled in. Predict the chair's spin rate after the release before you run it. The second line on the L graph shows the chair's own share of the angular momentum, so you can see where the rest went.

How your phone senses the turn

Your phone measured all of this, but it does not do it with angular momentum. Inside the chip a tiny mass vibrates back and forth very fast. When the phone turns, the turning pushes the vibrating mass slightly sideways (this is called the Coriolis effect), and the chip measures that sideways push. The faster the turn, the larger the push. Older gyroscopes, like the spinning wheels in aircraft instruments, do use angular momentum: a fast-spinning wheel keeps pointing the same way while the aircraft turns around it.

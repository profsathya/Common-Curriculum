One new thing I learned, and how I demonstrated it to myself

Week 5 · due Monday 28 September, 11:59 p.m. · 100 points · Graded assignments

Purpose

Prove to yourself what you have learned and what still remains to be learned. This ability to verify your own learning and identify new areas to explore is a key part of being a self-directed learner.

Tasks

If you would like to work on the assignment in a Google doc without the guidance from this assignment, you can paste this into a Google doc and answer each task under it. The guidance structure provided in this page is the recommended approach to ensure you are being guided and challenged along the way.

Each + opens guidance, examples, a reflection question and a box to write in. The guidance and reflection are provided to support you to do a high quality job of answering the questions.

  1. 1.Restate the question you set in week 3. Describe how it changed as you worked, and explain why.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Open your week-3 goal and copy the question out as you wrote it then. Underneath, write it as you would ask it now. If the wording is the same, say so — that is an answer. If it changed, the useful part is the why: what you found out that made the old version too broad, aimed at the wrong thing, or simply wrong.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Changed — Week 3: Why does leaning further into a turn keep the bike from sliding out? Now: Why does the speed I carry into a turn set how far I can lean before the tyres let go? It changed because lean on its own turned out to tell you nothing; it is lean together with speed and how tight the corner is.

    Did not change — I still want to know why hitting a drum harder makes it louder but not higher. What changed is that I now know which two quantities to look at, and that they are independent of each other.

    Reflection

    What did you have to learn before you could ask the question properly?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • The week-3 question is given, and the question as you would ask it now.
    • If it changed, it says what you found out that changed it.
  2. 2.Answer your question. Pick a situation, predict what happens, and explain each step to a ten-year-old. Your prediction has to include a quantitative component: the quantities involved, the mathematical relationship between them, and what happens to one when the other changes. Say how you checked whether you got it right.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Pick one situation, with numbers in it where you can. Work it through in the order you would say it out loud: what you are given, what you are working out, the relationship you are using, and the answer that falls out. Then write the ten-year-old version — the same steps, with no word you have not explained to them. The quantitative part is what makes this a prediction rather than a guess: name the quantities, write the relationship between them, and say what happens to one when you change the other. Then say how you checked — a simulation you ran, a measurement you took, or a case where the answer is already known.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Biking — Given: a 12 m corner, 8 m/s, dry tarmac. The sideways force needed to hold the turn goes as v²/r, so at 8 m/s through a 12 m corner that is about 5.3 units of sideways pull for every unit of weight… and the grip available is the friction number times the weight. Going to 11 m/s nearly doubles what the turn asks for while the grip stays where it was. For a ten-year-old: going round a corner is the road pushing you sideways. Going twice as fast means the road has to push four times as hard, and it can only push so hard before your wheels slip. I checked it in a simulation by raising the speed until the tyres let go.

    Drums — Given: the same drum, hit twice as hard. Loudness climbs with the square of how far the skin moves, so twice the movement is about four times the sound energy; the pitch depends on how fast the skin goes back and forth, which the size and tension set, and hitting harder does not change either. For a ten-year-old: hitting harder makes the skin swing wider, not quicker — wider means louder, quicker would mean higher, and only quicker changes the note. I checked it by recording two hits and measuring both.

    Reflection

    Where in your explanation did you have to slow down the most?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • One specific situation is worked through from what is given to what comes out.
    • The quantities are named and the mathematical relationship between them is written down.
    • It says what happens to one quantity when the other changes.
    • There is a version a ten-year-old could follow, using no word you have not explained.
    • It says how you checked whether the prediction was right.
  3. 3.Go to your AI Dojo and ask it to critique your explanation as if it is a ten-year-old. Ask follow-up questions, and confirm you can actually explain it with sufficient detail and accuracy. If you would rather work with a person, find someone who can play that role and question you rigorously.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Give it your ten-year-old explanation and ask it to react the way a ten-year-old would — to say where it got lost and to keep asking until it understands. Answer its questions. The moments where you struggle to answer are the point of this task: that is the part you do not have yet, and it is worth going back for. Write down what it got stuck on, what you could not answer at first, and what you changed as a result.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    With the Dojo — It stopped at “friction coefficient”, so I had used a word instead of an idea. I changed it to “how grippy the road is, written as a number” and it followed the rest. Then it asked why going faster needs four times the push and not twice, and I could not answer — I had the formula but not the reason. I went back to the chapter and rewrote that step.

    With a person — My sister kept asking why the note does not go higher when I hit harder. I kept saying “because the frequency does not change”, which is just the same sentence again. It took three tries before I could say it as wider swings, not quicker ones.

    Reflection

    What question were you least able to answer?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what the critique got stuck on, or what it kept asking about.
    • It says what you could not answer at first.
    • It says what you changed in your explanation as a result.
  4. 4.Did your curiosity about the topic help you persist when things got difficult? If yes, how; if not, why not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Think of the point in the last two weeks when you nearly stopped — the reading that made no sense, the simulation that would not run, the evening when there was no time. What got you past it, or what did not? Curiosity is not the only honest answer. If what kept you going was the deadline, or a partner, or if nothing did and the work stalled, say that and say why. This question is here so you learn what actually moves you, which is worth more than the right-sounding answer.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Yes — I stalled for four days on the v²/r step because the algebra kept coming out wrong. What got me back was that I still wanted to know the answer for my own riding, so I tried it as a simulation instead of algebra and it clicked.

    No — Honestly, curiosity got me started and then the deadline finished it. When the reading got hard I stopped enjoying it, and I came back on Sunday because the assignment was due, not because I wanted to know.

    Reflection

    What would have to be true for curiosity to carry you further next sprint?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names a specific point where the work got difficult.
    • It answers either way, with the how or the why not.
  5. 5.Did you have a partner who helped move your work? If yes, how; if not, why not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Answer for what actually happened. If a partner moved your work, say what they did — the question they asked, the mistake they caught, the meeting that made you finish something. If you did not have one, or the partnership never got going, say why: no one was available, the schedules did not meet, you did not ask, it did not seem worth it. Both answers tell us something we need for the rest of the semester.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Yes — Marco asked me why I was using the weight of the bike and not the weight of the bike plus me. I had the wrong number in the whole thing and would not have caught it on my own.

    No — We swapped numbers in week 4 and then never met. I work Thursdays and Saturdays and the two times we tried to meet did not come off, and I did not chase it.

    Reflection

    What would you want from a partner in Sprint 2?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It answers either way, with the how or the why not.
    • If yes, it says what the partner actually did.
  6. 6.Explain how you used AI, if you did: how it helped, what you checked that it got wrong, and what you brought that it did not.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Describe what you actually did with it, not what it is for. Which parts did you ask about, and what did it give you back? The checking is the part that matters most: name a place where you tested what it said against the textbook, a simulation or a measurement, and say what happened — whether it held up or not. Then say what was yours: the judgement, the situation you chose, the step you knew was wrong, the thing you had to go and find out. If you did not use AI for this, say so; that is a complete answer.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    I asked it to check my algebra on the v²/r step and it found a sign error I had missed. I also asked it what friction number to use for tarmac and it gave me 0.7 with no source; when I looked, published values run from about 0.5 to 0.9 depending on how wet it is, so the single number would have made my prediction look far more certain than it is. What I brought was the corner itself — my corner, measured — and the decision to give a range rather than one number.

    Reflection

    What did you do that the tool could not have done for you?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what you asked for and what came back, or that you did not use AI.
    • It names something you checked, and what happened when you checked it.
    • It says what you brought that the tool did not.
  7. 7.Identify what still remains to be learned — where this is still hard for you, and what you will do next.
    Guidance, examples, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Two things: where this is still hard, and what you will do about it. Be specific enough that you could start this week. “Learn more physics” is not a next step; “work out where the v²/r relationship stops holding once the road is wet” is. A question that got bigger as you worked is a good sign, not a bad one — say so if that is what happened.

    Examples, to shape your thinking — not models to copy

    Biking — What is still hard is what happens when the grip changes partway through the corner, which is exactly what wet leaves do. Next: find out whether the friction number is one number or a range, and run the simulation with it changing halfway round.

    Drums — I can say why pitch does not change with how hard you hit, but I cannot yet say why tightening the skin raises it. Next: work out what tension does to how fast the skin moves, and test it on my own drum with a tuner.

    Reflection

    Is what remains a bigger question than the one you started with?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names something specific that is still hard for you.
    • It names a next step you could start this week.

Criteria

Check it before you submit

  • It works one situation through: the quantities, the mathematical relationship between them, what happens to one when the other changes, an explanation a ten-year-old could follow, and how the prediction was checked.
  • It says what the critique surfaced, and what you fixed.
  • The curiosity question is answered either way — the how, or the why not.
  • The partner question is answered either way — the how, or the why not.

What makes it strong — the change in 1 is a change in what you understand, not a change of wording; the relationship in 2 is one you could defend if someone pushed on it, and the ten-year-old explanation uses no word you have not explained to them; task 3 shows the questions that were hard to answer, not just that the critique happened; tasks 4 and 5 are honest when the answer is no — a no with a real reason is worth as much here as a yes; what you name in 7 is specific enough that you could start on it this week.

Grading — 100 points, in Graded assignments. The Sprint 1 Exam on Wednesday works from these same questions: three of them are handed out, you answer two — question 2 and one of the other two — closed book, about half a page each. What you write here is your preparation for it.

What that looks like — what he decided, what AI built, and how it is checked

What he decided

  • The purpose and the seven tasks are his wording from 8 September 2026. Nothing on this page rewrites, reorders or adds to them.
  • The two layers. The tasks stand on their own; everything under a + is optional. A student who copies the tasks into a document and answers them there has done the whole assignment.
  • The prediction is quantitative. Task 2 asks for the quantities, the relationship between them, and what happens to one when the other changes — the same bar you set for yourself in week 3.
  • A “no” is a real answer. Tasks 4 and 5 ask whether curiosity and a partner actually helped. If they did not, saying so with the reason is worth as much as a yes, and it is what tells us what to change.
  • The Dojo is the named route in task 3, not the only one — a person who will question you rigorously does the same job.

What AI built

  • The guidance under each + — a process for thinking the question through, rather than more explanation of it.
  • The examples — more than one per task, so that no single one becomes the thing to copy. They are illustrations, not answers.
  • The reflection questions and the criteria notes the AI feedback button reads from.

How it is checked

  • Every page is written against the course’s own writing rules and then read by him; where a line misses, he edits it directly.
  • The AI feedback button is held to the observable criteria for each box. It never judges you, never rewrites your answer, and never hands you wording.
  • The Check it yourself list under each box is the same criteria without the AI, so the button is never the only route.

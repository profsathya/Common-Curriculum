How to read this map

This is a territory, not a syllabus. The same physics turns up on several trails, so no trail is “the momentum unit” — momentum is terrain you cross wherever you happen to be walking. Forks are questions only you can answer. Pitfall markers are honest signs about where learning tends to go wrong: watching a video feels like learning, and usually is not, unless you predicted first. Vistas are the payoffs — the moments the terrain suddenly makes sense from above.

Every trail says up front what you will be able to answer and what you will be able to predict once you have walked it. Those two promises are the point of the trail; everything between here and there is choice.

You will not walk alone: your CST286 AI Dojo carries this map, its markers and its sources. Ask it where you are, what is nearby, or for the story behind any marker — it will question you before it answers you. That is its job.

Trail 01 · The Slab of Glass

Your phone is a physics lab that fits in your pocket

Inside the glass: at least five instruments — accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, barometer, microphone — each one a physics idea made small enough to carry. On this trail you use the phone to study the phone.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a slab of glass know it’s moving, where it is, and when you touch it — and why does every one of those answers come down to a force, a field, or a wave?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

What your accelerometer will read, second by second, on an elevator ride you haven’t taken yet — and how many steps your own counter will report on a walk it has never seen.

Click a route name on the map to walk it.

Trailhead

Hold your phone. Ask it a question it can answer.

Ten minutes with a sensor you already own, before any theory.

◆ Fork

What pulls you?

Four routes leave this junction. They cross again later — starting on one loses you nothing. Walk one: how it knows it’s moving · how it feels your finger · why the battery dies · how it knows where it is.

⚠ Pitfall

Teardown videos feel like learning

If you can’t change a number and predict what happens, you’ve watched — not learned. Predict first, then watch.

✦ Vista

Your own step-counter

From raw accelerometer data to a working step-counter you wrote yourself — the moment the sensor stops being magic.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • phyphox (RWTH Aachen) — turns the phone’s sensors into instruments; the trail’s main tool. Tool paper: Staacks et al., Physics Education 53 (2018).
  • PhET Interactive Simulations (University of Colorado Boulder) — research-based sims for forces, circuits, capacitance; research base.
  • Falstad Circuit Simulator — animated current, no account; the touch and battery routes’ sandbox.
  • Branch Education — photorealistic device teardowns (touchscreens, batteries, GPS). Watch after predicting.
  • OpenStax College Physics — the careful version of any idea met on the trail, free.

Trail 02 · Game Feel

Your gut runs a physics engine. Time to read its source.

Every game you’ve ever judged as “floaty” or “tight” was a physics model with different constants. On this trail you write those models yourself — twenty lines at a time — and find out why the real world’s constants feel right.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a physics engine decide where everything goes next frame — and why does changing one constant change how the whole game feels?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

Where your projectile will land before you run the code — and what doubling gravity does to your jump’s height and hang time, in numbers you defend.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Load a game with a floaty jump. Say why it’s wrong — precisely.

Then build the smallest model that reproduces the feeling.

◆ Fork

What do you want to get right?

Three routes. Each one ends with a constant you tuned and can defend.

⚠ Pitfall

Running code you didn’t predict

Before every run: write down what the trajectory will look like. The gap between your sketch and the screen is where the physics lives.

✦ Vista

The jump that finally feels right

The constants that feel good are close to Earth’s — and now you know why, in numbers you chose.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • GlowScript / Web VPython — write 3D physics in the browser; also via trinket.io.
  • PhET: Projectile Motion and Collision Lab — the clean-world versions to test intuitions against.
  • myPhysicsLab — open-source interactive simulations with the equations shown beside them.
  • Algodoo — free 2D physics sandbox; force vectors drawn live.
  • OpenStax College Physics — kinematics, momentum and rotation chapters, free.

Trail 03 · Signal & Sound

Everything you hear is waves doing arithmetic

A guitar string playing two notes at once, a room with dead spots, an equalizer moving “the bass” — one idea underneath: waves add. This trail is the most audible physics on the map, and it runs straight toward signal processing.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does one vibrating string make many notes at once — and why can two speakers playing the same note silence each other?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

Which frequencies your spectrum will show before you sing the note — and where the quiet spots in a room will fall, given the tone and the distance between the speakers.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Open a spectrum view and sing one note. Count the peaks.

Two minutes, no theory, and the question that opens the whole trail.

◆ Fork

What do you want to hear?

Three routes; the equalizer route runs furthest into CS — Fourier is the math your field runs on.

⚠ Pitfall

Pretty spectrograms without a prediction

Before you look: sketch what a pure note should look like. One peak? Several? Then look, and explain the difference.

✦ Vista

The speed of sound, measured with two phones

A number physicists chased for centuries, measured in your hallway before lunch.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • phyphox — audio spectrum, oscilloscope, and the two-phone speed-of-sound experiment.

Trail 04 · The Moving Body

Your body solves equations it could never write down

A jump, a throw, a pedal stroke — each is a force problem your muscles solve in milliseconds and your mouth can’t explain. Film it and the solution becomes visible: position, velocity and acceleration, on one graph, from your own footage.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a spinning ball curve, why does a crouched start make you faster, and why is the physics of a helmet the same as the physics of a car’s crumple zone?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

Where your own shot will land, from the release you filmed — and roughly how much force a landing puts through your knees, from the video alone.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Film one rep in slow motion. Watch it back frame by frame.

Your phone shoots 240 fps. That is a lab-grade instrument you already carry.

◆ Fork

What do you want to get better at?

Three routes. All of them end with your own footage turned into numbers.

⚠ Pitfall

Noisy data looks like broken physics

Your video will not match the clean simulation — and the gap is the interesting part: air resistance, spin and measurement error, each of them nameable. Don’t discard the difference; explain it.

✦ Vista

Your own motion, modelled

Two films of the same movement — one from week 1, one from later — with a model in between that says what changed and why.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • Tracker (Open Source Physics) — turn video into position-time data; also Tracker Online, no install.
  • phyphox — strap the accelerometer to the moving thing and record the rep.
  • PhET: Projectile Motion — the airless comparison case, with drag you can switch on.
  • Physics Girl and Smarter Every Day — slow-motion sports physics done carefully.
  • OpenStax College Physics — motion in two dimensions, linear momentum, statics and torque.

Trail 05 · From Sand to CPU

Every idea you have ever had on a computer was electrons in silicon

There is no magic layer. Charge moves, switches flip, numbers add — and somewhere in that stack, adding numbers becomes running code. This trail goes down until it hits physics, then climbs back up carrying what it found.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a transistor — a switch with no moving parts — turn voltage into logic, and why does that make heat, not cleverness, the real limit on computing?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

What your adder circuit will output before you run it — and roughly how much power a chip burns at a given clock speed, with your assumptions written down.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Open something — or a photograph of its board — and find one component you can name.

Then find out what it does by making one in a simulator.

◆ Fork

How far down do you want to go?

Four routes down the stack. You can stop at any level and still have gone deeper than most people ever do.

⚠ Pitfall

Wiring it right is not understanding it

A circuit copied from a diagram works and teaches nothing. The test: can you say what the charge is doing, and what would happen if you doubled that resistor?

✦ Vista

A working computer you built from a single kind of gate

NAND to adder to something that computes — and the sentence you can now say out loud about how a machine adds.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • Falstad Circuit Simulator — animated current, no account needed.
  • NANDgame — build a working computer from NAND gates up, free in the browser; the long version is Nand2Tetris.
  • Wokwi and Tinkercad Circuits — virtual breadboards with Arduino, ESP32 and Raspberry Pi Pico.
  • Raspberry Pi Pico documentation — real hardware for about the price of lunch, MicroPython-friendly.
  • PhET: Circuit Construction Kit (DC) — the safe place to break things first.
  • OpenStax College Physics — electric current, resistance and Ohm’s law.

Trail 06 · Rockets & Orbits

An orbit is a fall that keeps missing the Earth

Newton worked it out with an imaginary cannon on an impossibly tall mountain: fire fast enough and the ground curves away as fast as you drop. The same arithmetic lands boosters upright today, and it is within reach of fifteen lines of code.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a rocket push against nothing and still accelerate, and why does a satellite in orbit need no engine at all?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

How fast something must travel to stay in orbit at a given altitude — and what fraction of a rocket has to be fuel to get there.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Watch a launch and a landing back to back, and say what is conserved.

The answer is in the exhaust, and it is the same answer as an astronaut throwing a wrench.

◆ Fork

What are you chasing?

Three routes. The first is momentum in disguise; the second is gravity; the third is how we know anything about places we cannot visit.

⚠ Pitfall

Gravity does not end in space

At the space station’s altitude gravity is about 90% of what you feel now. Floating is free fall, not the absence of gravity — and until that sentence feels obvious, the rest of the trail will not.

✦ Vista

A working orbit you wrote yourself

Change the launch speed and watch the orbit open into an escape — the moment the mathematics and the picture become one thing.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • PhET: Gravity and Orbits — gravity, orbits and scale you can switch on and off.
  • GlowScript / Web VPython — orbital motion is what VPython was built for.
  • NASA: Exoplanets — how planets around other stars are actually detected, with the data.
  • Veritasium — including the “why astronauts float” misconception, taken seriously.
  • OpenStax College Physics — uniform circular motion and gravitation.

Trail 07 · Cameras & Light

Light is the strangest thing you look at every day

Your eye, your camera and the evening sky are all running the same physics — bending, scattering, and waves adding up. Follow it far enough and light stops behaving like anything you have intuition for, while still taking your photograph.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does a lens decide what is in focus, why is the sky blue and the sunset red, and why does light passing through two slits refuse to behave like a particle?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

Where your image will form and how large it will be, before you take the photo — and the width of a human hair from the pattern it casts in a laser beam.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Shine a laser pointer past a single strand of hair onto a wall.

The stripes you get should not be possible if light travels in straight lines. They are the trail.

◆ Fork

What do you want to see?

Four routes, and the cheapest equipment on the whole map.

⚠ Pitfall

Ray diagrams as ritual

Drawing the three standard rays correctly and predicting nothing is a common way to pass optics without learning it. Always answer the question “where will the image be, and how big?” before you draw.

✦ Vista

The width of a hair, from a pattern on a wall

No microscope, no ruler fine enough — just wave arithmetic and a $2 laser. Then explain why the method works at all.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • PhET: Bending Light, Geometric Optics and Color Vision.
  • phyphox — the phone’s light sensor, for inverse-square and brightness measurements.
  • 3Blue1Brown — visual treatments of light, waves and why the mathematics looks the way it does.
  • HyperPhysics: lens equation (Georgia State) — the concept-map view of optics.
  • OpenStax College Physics — geometric optics, wave optics, vision and optical instruments.

Trail 08 · Energy & What It Costs

Energy is the only currency the universe actually accepts

Every phone charge, every mile driven, every model trained is an energy conversion with a receipt attached. Learn to read the receipt and a surprising number of arguments get shorter — including the ones about your own field.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does energy move from a battery to a screen without ever being created or destroyed — and why does every conversion leave heat behind?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

What a device costs to run for a year, in kilowatt-hours and dollars — and how far an electric car goes on the energy in a gallon of petrol, with your assumptions stated.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Find the watt rating printed on a charger, a kettle or a laptop.

One number, one year, one multiplication — and you have your first energy estimate.

◆ Fork

What do you want to account for?

Three routes, all of which end in a number you can defend in an argument.

⚠ Pitfall

Energy is not power

Watts are a rate; watt-hours are an amount. Almost every confused energy claim in public is this one mistake wearing a suit. Catch yourself using them interchangeably and stop.

✦ Vista

The cost of something everybody argues about

An estimate of your own, with every assumption written down and each one attackable — which is what makes it worth more than a headline.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • PhET: Energy Skate Park and Energy Forms and Changes.
  • U.S. Energy Information Administration — Energy Explained — real numbers for real comparisons.
  • TED-Ed: why batteries die — short, and already used in this course’s earlier version.
  • phyphox — cooling curves and temperature-style measurements you can run at home.
  • OpenStax College Physics — work and energy, thermodynamics, electric power.

Trail 09 · The Weird Stuff

The universe does not owe you an intuition

Time runs slower for a moving clock. A single particle goes through both slits. Neither is a metaphor: both are measured, and both are engineering constraints inside things you already use. Come for the strangeness — the foundations you need to go deeper are on the other trails, and this one will tell you which.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How can two particles stay correlated with no signal passing between them, and why does the GPS in your pocket stop working without relativity?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

How far your GPS position would drift in a single day if nobody corrected for relativity — in kilometres, from the clock rates.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Watch the double slit result, then try to explain it with particles. Fail honestly.

The failure is the point — write down exactly which assumption broke.

◆ Fork

Which strangeness?

Three routes. This is the one trail on the map that will send you to another trail for the tools — and it says so up front.

⚠ Pitfall

Pop-quantum word salad

This trail has the worst signal-to-noise on the internet: consciousness collapsing wavefunctions, “quantum” as a synonym for mysterious. Stick to the sources here, and demand of any claim: what was measured?

✦ Vista

Relativity, in your pocket, in numbers

The GPS clock correction is a real calculation with a real answer — and once you have done it, relativity stops being a story about twins and becomes engineering.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

Trail 10 · How Physics Knows

The method is the actual invention

Physics is not a pile of facts; it is a way of arguing with evidence until one explanation is left standing. This trail runs alongside every other trail on the map — take it when you want to know why anyone believes what your trail just told you.

✦ At the end of this trail
You will be able to answer — how & why

How does an experiment rule an explanation out, and why will a physicist trust a measurement they have never repeated themselves?

You will be able to predict — with numbers

What your data should look like if your explanation is right — and, before you collect any, what result would force you to abandon it.

Route pages open as each route is surveyed — the map is the part that is finished.

Trailhead

Pick one claim — from any trail, or from something you read this week.

Then ask the only question that matters: what would have to be true for this to be wrong?

◆ Fork

What are you testing?

Three routes. All of them make the other nine trails harder to fool you with.

⚠ Pitfall

“Research” that never touches data

Asking an AI for a summary of a summary can fill pages without a single measurement in them. The habit that fixes it: write your own summary first, then compare it to the AI’s and account for every difference.

✦ Vista

An experiment whose result you genuinely cannot predict

Design one, run it, and be prepared to be wrong in public. That is the whole method, in one afternoon.

Trail sources — what your Dojo cites on this trail

  • Investigative Science Learning Environment (ISLE) on PhysPort — the observe / explain / test cycle, with its evidence base.
  • ISLE Physics — the approach’s own site, including experiment collections.
  • phyphox and Tracker — the instruments that make your test cheap enough to actually run.
  • PhysPort — what physics-education research says works, for teachers and, honestly, for students too.
  • OpenStax College Physics — the introduction on models, theories and the nature of measurement.

Trail 01 · Route · accelerometer & gyroscope

There’s a tiny diving board inside your phone

A speck of silicon on springs — smaller than a grain of salt — flexes every time you move, and the phone reads that flex as force. This route follows the push: from what you feel in an elevator to a step-counter you build from raw data. It ends at a vista where the sensor stops being magic.

You are here

The profile is the route’s honest shape: it climbs. The markers below are the same ones, in words.

◆ Predict first — before anything else

You’re in an elevator, phone in hand, riding up three floors.

Sketch the acceleration graph for the whole ride — the start, the middle, the stop — before you measure anything. What does the middle of the ride look like? Commit to the sketch on paper. The gap between your sketch and the measurement is where this route’s physics lives.

The route, three ways

Walk whichever suits you — or all three. They meet at the vista.

Measure

Ride with phyphox

First move: open phyphox → “Acceleration with g”, pocket the phone, record one elevator ride. Stairs work too.

Consider: why does the graph spike at the start and the stop, but sit flat through the middle?

What about: the flat middle — does it read 0, or about 9.8? Try “Acceleration without g” and explain the difference between the two sensors’ stories.

Simulate

Push things in PhET

First move: open Forces and Motion: Basics; before every push, predict the speedometer.

Consider: what net force keeps the crate moving at a constant speed? Is that what your elevator’s flat middle was telling you?

What about: retell your elevator ride entirely as a story about forces on your phone. Where does the floor push harder than gravity?

Build

Model the sensor itself

First move: in Glowscript, make a box on a spring inside a moving case — about fifteen lines. Shake the case; watch the box lag.

Consider: the lag is the measurement. Why does a stiffer spring make a worse motion sensor but a faster one?

What about: export your phyphox ride as CSV into a spreadsheet and mark, by hand, where each phase of the ride begins. Your marks are the model.

⚠ Pitfall · the flat line that lies

A flat accelerometer line does not mean “not moving”

At rest the sensor reads about 9.8, and in smooth constant motion it reads the same. If that seems wrong, you have found the route’s central idea: the sensor measures force, not speed. Don’t pass this marker until you can say — out loud, in your own words — what an accelerometer actually measures.

✦ Vista · the summit of this route

Build your own step-counter

Record a fifty-step walk with the raw accelerometer. Then find the rule that counts your steps — a threshold? peaks? something better? When your count lands within a step or two of reality, you have built the thing your phone ships with, and you know why it miscounts when you shuffle. Worth bringing to a check-in: your data, your rule, and the miscount you can explain.

Ask your Dojo

Take this route with your CST286 AI Dojo — as a guide, not an answer machine

I just measured my elevator ride with phyphox and I want to understand my acceleration graph. Don’t give me the answer — use the Socratic method: ask me questions that guide me to explain the spikes, the flat middle, and what the sensor actually measures. At the end, ask me to explain the whole ride in my own words and check whether my understanding is right.

Your phone finds you by listening to four clocks in space

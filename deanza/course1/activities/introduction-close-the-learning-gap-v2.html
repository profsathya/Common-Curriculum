Introduction: Close the Learning Gap

Learning goal

Understand Introduction: Close the Learning Gap and connect it to the work in this module.

Overview

By the end of Sprint 3 you had a problem frame that had survived contact with a real person. You probably also came out of that conversation knowing less than you thought you did. Something they said didn’t fit. A constraint turned out to be real. A number you’d been quoting turned out to be measured differently than you assumed. This sprint is where you close the distance between the frame you’re carrying and what you’d need to know to act on it.

AI is your learning partner throughout, but it can’t tell you which gap is yours. It can sequence a subject, explain something four different ways until one lands, and get you to competent-enough on unfamiliar material in an afternoon. It will also produce a fluent, confident, plausible explanation of something it has wrong, and you’ll get no signal that it did. Start with your own thinking, use AI to widen and test it, then decide what to keep, reject, revise, or verify. This sprint separates what AI explained from what you checked, and grades the second one harder.

Sprint 4 Road Map

What you will do:

  1. Name what you don’t know, before you ask AI. List the gaps and mark which ones are actually blocking a decision, rather than which ones are interesting.
  2. Design a learning path with the Dojo, then cut it down. Sequence it toward the decision, not toward covering the topic.
  3. Decide what you’re not going to learn. Name the exclusion, the risk it creates, and the smallest check that would catch the risk.
  4. Learn it, and check the two claims your frame rests on. Use AI freely for the learning. Verify against something that isn’t AI, including one person.
  5. Update your Goal Plan and say what you’re still not ready to claim. Learning that confirms what you already thought is a real result, as long as you can say what would have changed your mind.

Sprint 4 Concepts

Learning Gap

A topic is something you’d like to understand. A gap is something whose absence is stopping you. The test is whether you can name the decision or the next step that’s waiting on it.

  • Weak: “I need to learn more about onboarding best practices.” This is a subject, and it has no end. You could read about it for a year and never reach a point where you’d say you were done.
  • Strong: “I don’t know how our ticket system counts the age of a reassigned ticket, so I can’t tell whether the nine-day figure is real.” This names an unverified claim your frame is standing on. If it turns out the nine days is a measurement artifact, the frame changes.

Gaps come from the same place your assumptions do. The ones you marked inferred in your Sprint 3 work, and the places where your own writing goes vague, are where to look first. Vagueness in your own description of a problem is usually a gap you’ve routed around.

Enough for This Stage

You’re not becoming an expert. The standard is that you know enough to make the next decision and to defend it if someone pushed back. A gap is closed when you can say what you now know, how you know it, and what you still wouldn’t claim in a meeting.

This matters because the alternative, learning until you feel finished, has no stopping point. Two weeks of feeling informed is not the same as being able to move.

It’s also why this sprint asks you to write down what you already understand well enough. Naming what’s solid is what keeps the sprint from turning into an audit of everything you’ve ever not known.

Explanation and Verification

When AI tells you how something works, you have an explanation. When you check that explanation against something that isn’t AI, you have evidence. From the inside these feel identical, which is the whole problem: a good explanation produces the same confidence as a verified fact.

Verification means going to a source appropriate to your problem that could contradict you. Depending on what you’re checking, that might be documentation for the system you actually use rather than the general case, data you can look at, a written policy or standard, established practice in your field, expert input, a stakeholder conversation, or direct observation of what actually happens. The useful question isn’t “is this right,” it’s “what would this look like if it were wrong here.”

Verification doesn’t mean endless research. It means enough checking for this stage of the course and the decision in front of you. But a check that couldn’t come back negative isn’t a check.

Confirmed and Inferred

In Sprint 3 you marked every field of your stakeholder map Confirmed or Inferred and traced each one to a source. The same two words do the same job here, applied to what you learn:

Confirmed: you checked it against something outside AI. Documentation for your own system, a number you looked at, a policy you read, a person who told you.

Inferred: a reasonable account, including one AI gave you, that you haven’t checked.

Most of what you learn in two weeks will be inferred, and that’s correct. Marking it honestly is what lets you say in Sprint 5 which parts of your argument are actually standing on something.

Strategic Ignorance

Deciding not to learn something is part of the work, not a way around it. But an exclusion is only responsible if you can say three things: why it’s reasonable now, what could go wrong because of it, and the smallest check that would catch that if it did.

  • Not learning: how other organizations structure onboarding ownership.
  • Why: the frame is about who owns the handoff here, and comparative practice won’t tell me that.
  • Risk: I might propose something that was tried here before and failed for reasons I don’t know.
  • Minimum check: ask the HR lead whether this has ever been tried. One question.

An exclusion with no named risk is usually avoidance wearing a scope-control costume.

Two-Week Sprint Map

This sprint spans two course weeks. Use these labels for pacing; Canvas keeps the work in one module.

  • Sprint Week 1: Read this Introduction and pass the concept check, then write your own gap list and design the learning path in the Dojo.
  • Sprint Week 2: Send your outreach on day one, don’t wait until you need the answer. Do the learning, run both checks, update your Goal Plan, and reflect.

Time Guidance

Plan for about five to seven hours across the two weeks. The split is uneven on purpose: week one is planning and should be quick, week two is the actual learning and should not be.

Two things go wrong here more than anywhere else in the course. The first is spending week two writing about learning instead of learning. If that’s happening, the path you designed was too big, and cutting it is the right move rather than a failure. The second is leaving the outreach until you need it. Send the ask at the start of week two, before you know exactly what you’ll want to ask, and refine the question while you wait.

Every activity in this sprint serves the same real problem. The concept check, the Dojo session, the learning, and the checks are one piece of work in four stages, not four assignments.

Working Standard

Specific and honest work is better than polished generic work. In this sprint that means one thing above all: don’t present something AI explained to you as something you know. Separate them, in writing, every time.

The rest follows from that. Show what you tried, what changed, what evidence you have, and what’s still uncertain. A gap that closed and confirmed what you already believed is a real finding. A check that came back complicated is a better finding. Neither needs dressing up.

Carry Forward

Keep your gap list, your learning path, the sources you used, the prompts you directed the learning with, your verification log, your exclusions, and your updated Goal Plan.

These feed Sprint 5 directly. Your final deliverable there is the Integrated Problem Document and Readiness Report, which asks you to build one argument out of everything you’ve made since Sprint 1 and say what it rests on. The verification log you produce this sprint is the learning-evidence section of that report, and what you mark confirmed is what your argument gets to stand on.

Sprint 3: Integrate People and Context (V2) · Assignment

Dojo Lab: Stakeholder Map

Learning goal

Apply the core idea from Dojo Lab: Stakeholder Map in a focused Canvas submission.

Overview

Prepare for a validation conversation with stakeholders by first partnering with AI to anticipate different perspectives, assumptions, and validation questions. Then create a stakeholder map that shows who is connected to the problem and what you still need to learn from them.

Start with What You Already Think

Before bringing in the Dojo, write down the stakeholders you already believe are connected to your problem. You should have at least three. Don’t research exhaustively yet, just capture your current thinking as it stands. If you don’t have a workplace stakeholder, reach out to a mentor, community member, customer, or anyone with a genuine relationship to the problem.

Use the Dojo

Use the Open the Symbiotic Thinking Dojo link for this lab. First-time users should open Learn About -> Privacy & Setup and configure a Gemini API key in the browser before starting.

Do not paste confidential workplace, client, education, patient, or personal data into the Dojo. De-identify details and keep sensitive information out of the prompt.

Use this prompt with the Dojo:

I am preparing to validate this problem with real people:
[paste problem statement]

Use Learn + Solve mode. Start with @advocate. Help me identify:
1. People directly affected.
2. People who influence whether the problem can be addressed.
3. People with authority or constraints.
4. People who may disagree with my framing.
5. What each stakeholder might need, fear, or value.

Then use @auditor:
- Which needs am I treating as known, inferred, or unsupported?
- Which constraints should I verify before changing direction?
- Which validation questions would help me listen rather than pitch?

Then use @challenger:
- Which stakeholders am I overlooking?
- Which needs am I assuming rather than knowing?
- What would make my current stakeholder map misleading?

Review the Dojo output as preparation, not evidence. Do not treat the Dojo as a stakeholder.

A how-to video is available as review material if you need a refresher on how to use the Dojo.

Build Your Stakeholder Map, Part 1

After working with the Dojo, turn your thought process into a written document. Profile a minimum of three stakeholders, covering at least one affected, one influential, and one authority-or-constraint stakeholder. For every stakeholder, record the following, and mark a Status for each field:

Field What to record Status
Role Who they are in relation to the problem Confirmed / Inferred
Relationship to the problem Affected, influential, responsible for a constraint, or positioned to challenge your frame Confirmed / Inferred
Impact level How directly and strongly the problem affects them Confirmed / Inferred
Influence or authority level How much say they have over any solution Confirmed / Inferred
Need What they need from a solution Confirmed / Inferred
Constraints What constraint or risk they might see that you haven’t Confirmed / Inferred
Next question to ask What you’d still need to find out from them Confirmed / Inferred

Mark each field’s status honestly:

Confirmed - supported by evidence: a stakeholder conversation, an observation, or reliable documentation.

Inferred - a reasoned hypothesis based on what you currently know, including ideas you developed with the Dojo, that should be validated later.

For each field, also identify where the information came from: the conversation, observation, document, or Dojo session it traces back to.

Build Your Stakeholder Map, Part 2

Once your stakeholder profiles are done, step back and review them. Then add these three items below the profile table.

1. Assumptions that require a real conversation

From your stakeholder profiles, pull out the assumptions that are both inferred (not yet confirmed) and high-stakes: if you’re wrong about this, it would meaningfully change your problem statement or Goal Plan. These are the guesses worth spending a real conversation on, not just any unconfirmed detail.

2. Validation questions

Questions designed to test your assumptions, not confirm them. A validation question should be open enough that a stakeholder could give you insights you haven’t thought about.

Some examples of weak versus strong validation questions:

  • Weak: “Do you agree that X is the main problem?” This invites a yes/no answer only, or it leads people to answer that X is the problem.
  • Strong: “Walk me through the last time X happened. What did you actually do?” This asks for a specific, real account, which is much harder to fake or soften.
  • Weak: “Would a solution like Y help you?” This pitches your idea and asks for approval.
  • Strong: “What would need to be true for this to actually get easier for you?” This leaves room for the stakeholder to name something you hadn’t considered.

Good validation questions usually ask about specific experiences, past behavior, or concrete tradeoffs, instead of opinions about your idea.

Quick self-check. Do your questions ask about a specific experience, a past behavior, or a concrete tradeoff? If a question asks for an opinion about your idea instead, rewrite it.

3. Your first choice and your backup

Choose the one person you’ll reach out to first for your real validation conversation, and name a backup stakeholder in case your first choice does not respond. Then explain your reasoning: why this person, before anyone else?

Some strong reasons to contact someone first:

  • They can confirm or break your riskiest assumption: talking to them first tells you fastest whether your frame is pointed in the right direction.
  • They’re the most affected, and you know the least about them: your evidence is thin exactly where the stakes are high.
  • They sit at a point of disagreement: if your stakeholder map shows two people or groups who seem to see the problem differently, talking to one of them first can clarify that tension early.
  • They represent the largest group, not just one person: if a stakeholder role stands in for many people, they can tell you something that generalizes further.

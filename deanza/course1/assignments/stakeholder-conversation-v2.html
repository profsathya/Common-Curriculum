Stakeholder Conversation

Overview

Now it’s time to test your problem frame against an actual stakeholder, not AI. This activity has three parts: preparing for the conversation, having the conversation, and documenting what you learned.

Part 1: Prepare for the Conversation

Before you talk to a real stakeholder, use AI to sharpen the validation questions you plan to ask. The goal is to catch weak questions, such as ones that lead the stakeholder toward your idea or that are too vague to tell you anything, before you’re in the actual conversation.

  1. Write your starting draft. For the stakeholder you’ll contact, write down their role, three validation questions you’d ask them, and what you’re hoping to learn from each question. Also name the single riskiest assumption in your frame and the evidence, not approval, that would change your mind.
  2. Generate AI follow-up questions. Start the AI activity below and ask AI to respond to your draft with follow-up questions. Use them to diagnose your draft. Look for places where AI’s response reveals that your question is: - Leading: it nudges the stakeholder toward the answer you want. - Vague: it’s unclear what you’re really asking, so any answer would be hard to use. - Missing evidence: it asks for an opinion rather than something you could verify, such as an experience, a specific example, or a real decision they made.
  3. If needed, use the deeper AI guidance button for more targeted help in refining your three questions.

When you’re done working with the AI, copy or download the JSON response file from the activity. You will submit it together with your report at the end. Then contact the stakeholder you’ve chosen to set up a meeting.

Part 2: Set Up the Conversation

  • Pick the channel this person actually answers. In person to set a time if you see them regularly; email if you want it in writing; phone or text if that is how you two normally talk.
  • Send the ask, and note the date you sent it on your stakeholder map. Keep it short: who you are, that you are in a course reframing a real problem, that you would like 20 to 30 minutes, and two or three times that work.
  • No reply in 3 days? Follow up once in the same thread, and make it easier to say yes: offer 15 minutes, or offer to send two questions they can answer in writing.
  • Still no reply by day 5? Go to your backup stakeholder and start again. Note the switch on your map. A stakeholder who does not respond is itself information about access and influence.
  • Got a yes? Confirm the time, the format, and the length, so you both arrive expecting the same conversation.

This is a real problem in your own work, and it is in both your interests to connect early. The sooner you talk, the more of this sprint you have left to act on what you hear.

Part 3: Have the Conversation

  1. Note the conversation format and date: when you talk and how you talk (in person meeting, phone call, online meeting, and so on).
  2. Ask the validation questions you’ve prepared. Stay open to answers you didn’t expect.
  3. Take notes in the stakeholder’s own words, as closely as you can capture them. Do not include confidential details, protect the person’s privacy, and paraphrase anything sensitive. Don’t add your interpretation; stay as close as you can to how the stakeholder described their experience.
  4. Save your notes for the report below.

Prep Sheet

Use this prep sheet to run the conversation. Fill in each section at the time it names.

Fill in before the meeting:

  • Stakeholder name and role.
  • Relationship to the problem.
  • Format, date, and time.
  • The assumption this conversation is meant to test.
  • Three validation questions, each with what you hope to learn.

Fill in during the meeting:

  • One box per question for their words.
  • Anything you did not expect.
  • Anything they offered to follow up on.
  • Anything to keep confidential.

Fill in within five minutes of finishing:

  • What they confirmed.
  • What they challenged.
  • Your first read on whether the frame changes.
  • The next question you would ask.

Part 4: Document What You Learned

After the conversation, write a report with the following sections.

Before the conversation:

  • Starting problem frame: what you believed before this conversation.

The conversation itself:

  • Stakeholder: their role and relationship to the problem.
  • Conversation format and date: when and how you talked (in person meeting, phone call, online meeting, and so on).
  • What the stakeholder said: as close to how they described it as possible, not your interpretation of it.

What the conversation revealed:

  • Where they confirmed your frame: what matched what you already believed.
  • Where they challenged, complicated, or contradicted your frame: what didn’t match, surprised you, or showed the problem was more complex than you thought.

What changes as a result:

  • Revised problem frame: do you need to update your problem frame based on your conversation? If so, update it based on evidence, not on your assumption or interpretation. But if your conversation fully confirmed your original frame, report that honestly. A clear confirmation is a real and valuable result also.
  • Stakeholder map: update it based on what you now know versus what you were assuming.
  • What still needs validation: what’s still unconfirmed, and what your next real conversation should test.

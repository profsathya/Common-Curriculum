Name the Gap

Learning goal

Apply the core idea from Name the Gap in a focused Canvas submission.

Purpose

Work out what you actually do not know, in your own words, before any AI gets involved.

Do This Part Without AI

Ask any AI what someone working on your problem would need to learn and it will hand you a clean, sensible, complete looking list. It will be right in general and wrong about you. You will not be able to tell the difference unless you wrote yours first.

You will bring AI in at the next step. Right now the list has to be yours.

Where to Look

Open your Sprint 3 report, your revised problem statement, and your Goal Plan, and look for three things:

  1. Something the stakeholder said that you had no response to.
  2. Something still marked inferred that your frame depends on.
  3. Something you have been describing vaguely because you do not actually know the specifics.

That third one is the most productive and the easiest to miss. Vagueness in your own description of a problem is usually a gap you have been routing around.

Part 1. What You Already Understand Well Enough

Two or three things about this problem that you understand well enough to act on right now. One line each on how you know.

I know the handoff runs between IT and the hiring manager. I have watched it happen twice and both managers described it the same way.

Keep this short. It is here so the sprint does not turn into an audit of everything you have ever not known, and so you start from solid ground rather than a deficit list.

Part 2. What You Do Not Know Yet

Write four gaps. One line each, and mark each one:

  • Blocking. A decision or a next step is waiting on this. If you cannot name the decision, it is not blocking.
  • Useful. It would improve the work, but nothing is stopped while you do not know it.
  • Interesting. You want to know it, and nothing you do next depends on it.

Run the sentence test on each one: “Until I know this, I can’t ___.” If the blank will not fill, you have written a topic rather than a gap. Rewrite it or replace it.

A finished list looks like this:

  1. I don’t know whether ticket age restarts when a ticket is reassigned. Blocking. I can’t take the nine day figure to the IT lead until I know.

  2. I don’t know how our HRIS provisions accounts. Useful. It would sharpen the picture, but I can make my case without it.

  3. I don’t know what the service desk lead thinks the real bottleneck is. Blocking. If she says it starts upstream, I have framed the wrong step.

  4. I don’t know how other organizations this size handle onboarding. Interesting. Nothing I do next depends on it.

Be honest about the marks. There is no right distribution. Four blocking and one blocking are both true answers to different situations.

If none of your four are blocking, you do not have your gap yet. That is not a failing grade. It means the list needs another pass. Go back to the three places above, particularly the vagueness one. Lists that come back with nothing blocking are usually too polite: they name gaps that sound reasonable rather than the ones your frame is actually standing on.

If you have been through it twice and still cannot find one, say so in your submission and describe what you looked at. That is an honest answer and it is worth more than a gap you relabelled to get unstuck.

If all four are blocking, ask which one is furthest upstream. Which answer would change what the other three even mean? Usually one of them reshapes the rest.

Part 3. The Gap You Are Committing To

Pick one from your blocking list. Write a short paragraph covering:

  • What specifically you do not know.
  • What decision is waiting on it.
  • What would change in your problem frame if the answer went one way rather than the other.

That last question is the real test. If the answer changes nothing in your frame, you have picked a useful or an interesting one. Go back and choose again.

I don’t know how our system counts the age of a ticket that gets reassigned. My whole frame rests on nine days being real. The decision waiting on it is whether I take this to the IT lead as a process problem or go back and re-measure first. If ticket age resets on reassignment, the true wait is longer than nine days and the handoff problem is worse than I have been saying. If it does not, my number holds and I can move.

Part 4. What “Closed” Looks Like

One or two sentences describing the state you are trying to reach. It has to be something you could point at, not a feeling.

  • Weak: “I’ll understand how our ticketing system works.” No end point. You will stop when you get tired.
  • Strong: “I can say whether ticket age restarts on reassignment, and point at where in the console that is configured.” Has an end.

The same test works outside a workplace:

  • Weak: “I’ll get a better handle on what these roles are looking for.”
  • Strong: “I can name the three requirements that appear in every posting I am targeting, and say which two I already meet.”

Write yours so that someone else could read it and tell you whether you were finished.

Part 5. What You Are Not Learning

Your other three gaps are your exclusions. Naming them is not enough on its own. What makes an exclusion legitimate is that you can say what it costs you, and how you would find out that it had cost you.

For each one, one line:

  • What you are not learning.
  • The risk that creates.
  • The smallest thing that would tell you the risk has become real.

Not learning: how our HRIS provisions accounts. Risk: if provisioning is the actual bottleneck, my ticket system findings are beside the point. What would catch it: if the service desk lead says most delays start upstream of the ticket, I have picked wrong.

What to Submit

All five parts, as a text entry. Most people land somewhere between 400 and 700 words.

Is the learning being demonstrated — and how broadly?

It is being demonstrated. The strongest submissions show exactly the thinking the program was designed to produce: postings-grounded market reads, quantified evidence in place of generic claims, and visible ownership of AI collaboration — including one student correcting his AI's inaccurate suggestion about his own work.

Not yet broadly. Full demonstration is concentrated in five or six students; the middle shows real movement — structure, numbers, insight turned into named next moves — without yet showing a complete case.

The verification is ahead. Whether this is learning or performance gets tested in the next two weeks: the same Week 1 questions re-asked verbatim, a live 1:1 behind every referral, and referral outcomes themselves.

What the submissions show, against each program goal

Evidence-backed value · half the Week 3 cohort moved from generic to owned▶

The goal: replace "why should we hire you" boilerplate with specific, provable claims. Against the generic Week 1 baseline, the strongest Week 3–4 submissions are unrecognizable: one student rewrote a capstone bullet into owned, quantified work — a Docker-backed CI testing workflow with 80%+ coverage, maintained through schema changes — and can say exactly what she owned and why it mattered. Another tested her positioning with the standard we taught:

"[My case] breaks if you swap out the sector — unlike generic junior-SWE positioning."

Roughly half of the sixteen Week 3 submissions show changes of this quality. The other half show real but thinner movement — adopting the resume structure, adding numbers — motion toward a case, not yet a case.

Market understanding · the best reads cite named postings; the fireside converted to action▶

The goal: know what employers actually want from observation, not guesswork. The best Week 4 answers pull top requirements from named postings (Palantir, Canonical, Tesla) and map each to the student's own evidence — then name the gap and the skill to learn. The weakest still guess ("strong backend skills, problem solving").

The Week 5 recruiter fireside visibly landed: nearly every reflection converts an insight into a named move — "find 5 startups and reach out to their Founders and Chief of Staff with a cold email," a shift to Y Combinator targeting, one student already cold-emailing "and seeing results." The recruiter's-eye flip — your first reader isn't an engineer, and you're their ninth conversation today — recurs in their own words.

The experiment discipline · internalized beautifully — at n=1▶

The goal: run the search as a fair test — a bet, a control, a signal, a read. One student's iteration is textbook: 10+10 applications sent, silence read as "too early to tell," the idea-vs-execution question applied to his own bet, a sharper next bet named with the fair-test rule kept and the signal declared. He then wrote, unprompted, that owning his search means "using silence to decide what to test next" — the program's core loop, in his own words. But this is one complete demonstration among the eight who ran the experiment, not eight.

AI fluency — create, don't consume · the most encouraging content signal, with a caveat▶

The goal: students drive the thinking; AI sharpens it. Six of sixteen Week 3 self-checks spontaneously describe the temptation to let the AI write and the decision not to. One student caught his Dojo suggesting a claim that "sounded good" but wasn't accurate to his actual work, and corrected it:

"I let the Dojo help improve the wording, but I made the final decision about what I could honestly claim."

That is the exact behavior the Dojos were designed to produce. The caveat we hold ourselves to: these are narratives of ownership, and a pasted submission can't verify them — which is why every referral requires a live 1:1 interview.

One strong student also pushed back, and we take it seriously: the AI-assisted reflection "doesn't really set up a good start… I feel like I'm not keeping my skills sharp." For students who arrive already advanced, the scaffolding can read as overhead.

The warm referral cases · high quality, high aspiration, no artifacts yet▶

The goal: a case strong enough for CTI to put its name behind. Three written applications so far, and the quality is genuinely high — detailed, walk-through-able evidence down to specific pull requests and issue numbers, with honest acknowledgment of where each case is thin. Two calibration notes for the 1:1s: two of the three cases target OpenAI — aspiration running ahead of calibration — and none of the three attempted the optional customized artifact. The case-building skill arrived; the show-don't-tell lever hasn't been pulled yet. Two more referral application windows are open now, first come, first served.

Three honest readings of the same evidence

Best case · the funnel was a filter, and what it kept is exactly what we designed for▶

The program's thinking shows up in student writing unprompted — fair tests, silence as data, evidence-first claims, AI ownership. The top third of remaining students are doing precisely what we designed: grounded market reads, cases that break if you swap the sector, resistance to AI ghostwriting. On this reading, the staircase was a filter for an optional summer program, and what it kept is a group whose work makes any referral defensible — which is the point of spending social capital carefully.

Medium case · the design works for whom it reaches — and that's a dozen, not a cohort▶

Quality is bimodal. Five or six students demonstrate the full stack of goals; the middle does the motions with thinner substance; and depth is concentrated in the students who arguably needed us least. The insights landed cohort-wide — the fireside reflections are uniformly awake — but converting insight into disciplined practice (the 10+10, the iteration, the artifact) has happened at n=1 to n=3. The program teaches what we think it teaches; it hasn't yet shown it can teach it at breadth.

Worst case · the machinery can be satisfied without the learning▶

The weakest submissions suggest box-checking: one-line answers naming no evidence, and a resume "aligned to the niche" by rewording rather than by proof — the exact failure the program warns against, produced inside the program. Ownership narratives may be performed for the grader, since a copy-paste submission can't verify them. Zero artifacts and two moonshot targets in three referral cases could mean the bar reads as ceremony rather than a real door. On this reading, we built a strong program for the students we wished we had.

What we're watching over the next two weeks

The evidence that will tell these readings apart ▶
  • Before/after growth: the Week 6 self-check (due July 19) re-asks the three Week 1 survey questions verbatim — per-student value and market deltas against a genuinely generic baseline.
  • The 1:1 interviews: the live verification layer — can each referral case survive a ten-minute conversation?
  • Referral outcomes: do our contacts forward the resumes, and does anyone land an interview — the outcome the whole design points at.
  • The networking event: what the access gate actually bought — turnout among the 16 eligible, and 24-hour follow-ups.
  • Who the engaged core is: did the funnel keep the students we'd have bet on — or lose ones we should have kept?

Early lessons, whichever reading wins

Three moves for the next iteration ▶

Front-load the access levers — the referral, the recruiters, the networking arrived in Week 4, and access is our strongest motivator. Bring live verification earlier — the 1:1 is what makes AI-era ownership real. And rebalance for the middle: fewer required items, more depth optional-but-inviting, with a faster lane for students who arrive advanced. The open question: the top third built exactly what we hoped — is the next design's job to widen that third, or to serve it deeper?

Is the learning being demonstrated — and how broadly?

It is being demonstrated. The strongest submissions show exactly the thinking the program was designed to produce: postings-grounded market reads, quantified evidence in place of generic claims, and visible ownership of AI collaboration — including one student correcting his AI's inaccurate suggestion about his own work.

Narrowly, so far. Full demonstration is concentrated in five or six students; the middle shows real movement — structure, numbers, insight turned into named next moves — and their complete case is still taking shape.

The verification is ahead. Whether this is learning or performance gets tested in the next two weeks: the same Week 1 questions re-asked verbatim, a live 1:1 behind every referral, and referral outcomes themselves.

What the submissions show, against each program goal

Can students say why someone should hire them — with proof? · about half moved from generic claims to specific evidence▶

The goal: replace "why should we hire you" boilerplate with specific, provable claims. Here is one student's answer to that question, in their own words, five weeks apart:

Week 1
"I would say that I'm a fast learner, persistent when solving problems, and work well with others. I don't give up easily when trying to understand a difficult problem. I care about doing work that is accurate and dependable."
Week 4 — same student
"I already have direct developer-tooling experience from my [IDE-tooling internship], where I improved build/launch workflows… I can show testing and automation experience through [my capstone], where I built a Docker-backed CI pipeline with pytest and maintained 80%+ test coverage… This is sharper than Week 1 because in Week 1 I was mostly thinking about myself as a general software engineering applicant. Now my niche is more specific… The gap I need to close is deeper production-level CI/CD — my skill-to-learn should be CI/CD and developer workflow automation."

Answers verbatim; bracketed phrases replace identifying project names.

Another student tested her positioning with the standard we taught:

"[My case] breaks if you swap out the sector — unlike generic junior-SWE positioning."

The same movement shows up at the resume-bullet level. One student's before and after, from her own Week 3 reflection:

Before
"Draft client communications to request financial updates and documentation, supporting timely and accurate account preparation for advisor meetings."
After
"Gather clients' financial updates & documents to support an advisor with 2–3 weekly annual review meetings, so they can show up prepared for clients & avoid rescheduling."

Roughly half of the sixteen Week 3 submissions show changes of this quality. The other half show real but thinner movement — adopting the resume structure, adding numbers — a case still taking shape.

Do students know what employers actually want? · the strongest answers cite real postings; recruiter advice turned into action▶

The goal: ground what employers want in direct observation — postings, recruiters, and your own results. The same student from the example above, asked what employers are actually looking for, five weeks apart:

Week 1
"I think employers are looking for people who can solve problems, adapt to new technology and workflows, and communicate well."
Week 4 — same student
"1. Infrastructure and developer-workflow skills — roles kept asking for people who can improve developer experience, scalability, reliability, internal tools… Palantir's infrastructure internship mentions improving developer experience for other engineers. 2. Open-source/Linux/tooling experience — postings like Canonical's open-source/Linux graduate role… 3. Strong coding plus systems/process thinking — they want engineers who can build reliable tools… such as CI/CD, automation, testing."

Answers verbatim, condensed with ellipses.

The Week 1 answer is a reasonable guess; the Week 4 answer is a market read, built from postings the student actually studied. The thinnest Week 4 reads stay at the level of general skills, without a posting behind them.

The Week 5 fireside chat — a live Q&A with two industry recruiters — visibly landed: nearly every reflection converts an insight into a named move — "find 5 startups and reach out to their Founders and Chief of Staff with a cold email," a shift to Y Combinator targeting, one student already cold-emailing "and seeing results." The recruiter's-eye flip — your first reader isn't an engineer, and you're their ninth conversation today — recurs in their own words.

Are students running their search like an experiment? · one complete demonstration so far▶

The goal: turn the job search into a fair test — a specific bet about where you'll win, tried against your default targets, with a defined signal to read at the end. One student's cycle is textbook: he sent ten applications to his default targets and ten to his bet, read the silence as "too early to tell," asked whether the problem was his idea or his execution, and named a sharper next bet with the same fair-test rules and a clear signal. He then wrote, unprompted, that owning his search means "using silence to decide what to test next" — the program's core loop, in his own words. So far this is one complete demonstration among the eight who ran the experiment.

Are students doing their own thinking when they work with AI? · the most encouraging signal, with a caveat▶

The goal: students drive the thinking; AI sharpens it. Six of sixteen Week 3 self-checks spontaneously describe the temptation to let the AI write and the decision not to. One student caught his Dojo suggesting a claim that "sounded good" but wasn't accurate to his actual work, and corrected it:

"I let the Dojo help improve the wording, but I made the final decision about what I could honestly claim."

That is the exact behavior the Dojos were designed to produce. The caveat we hold ourselves to: these are narratives of ownership, and a pasted submission can't verify them — which is why every referral requires a live 1:1 interview.

One strong student also pushed back, and we take it seriously: the AI-assisted reflection "doesn't really set up a good start… I feel like I'm not keeping my skills sharp." For students who arrive already advanced, the scaffolding can read as overhead.

Are the referral applications strong enough to put our name behind? · high quality, ambitious targets▶

The goal: a case strong enough for CTI to put its name behind. Three written applications so far, and the quality is genuinely high — detailed, walk-through-able evidence down to specific pull requests and issue numbers, with honest acknowledgment of where each case is thin. Two calibration notes for the 1:1s: two of the three cases target OpenAI — aspiration running ahead of calibration — and none of the three attempted the optional customized artifact. The case-building skill arrived; the artifact opportunity is still waiting to be used. Two more referral application windows are open now, first come, first served.

Three honest readings of the same evidence

Best case · the learning we designed for is showing up in student work▶

The program's thinking appears in student writing without being prompted: fair tests, silence treated as information, claims backed by evidence, a student correcting his AI when it overstated his work. The students producing this work are demonstrating every goal we set — and their referral cases are strong enough for us to put our name behind. On this reading, iteration 1 shows the design works: students who do the work learn what we set out to teach.

Medium case · the learning is real — so far in a small group▶

The work splits into two levels. Five or six students demonstrate everything; the middle shows real movement — better structure, real numbers, recruiter insights turned into named next moves — with their complete cases still taking shape. The ideas landed across the cohort: the recruiter-chat reflections show that clearly. Sustained practice — running the search experiment, iterating on it, building a tailored artifact — has appeared for one to three students. On this reading, the program teaches what we believe it teaches, and the open question is how to bring the middle along.

Worst case · some submissions may show completion without the learning▶

The thinnest submissions raise this possibility directly: one-line answers with no evidence behind them, and a resume adjusted by rewording rather than by proof. Written descriptions of owning the AI can be given without the ownership being practiced, and a pasted submission can't tell the difference — which is why the live 1:1 interviews matter. Referral applications are few so far, with ambitious targets and no artifacts yet. On this reading, the strongest work came from students who arrived strong, and the program hasn't yet shown it moves the students who need it most.

What we're watching over the next two weeks

The evidence that will tell these readings apart ▶
  • Before/after growth: the Week 6 self-check (due July 19) re-asks the three Week 1 survey questions verbatim — per-student value and market deltas against a genuinely generic baseline.
  • The 1:1 interviews: the live verification layer — can each referral case survive a ten-minute conversation?
  • Referral outcomes: do our contacts forward the resumes, and does anyone land an interview — the outcome the whole design points at.
  • The networking event: what the access gate actually bought — turnout among the 16 eligible, and 24-hour follow-ups.
  • Who the engaged core is: did the funnel keep the students we'd have bet on — or lose ones we should have kept?

Early lessons, whichever reading wins

Three moves for the next iteration ▶

Front-load the access levers — the referral, the recruiters, the networking arrived in Week 4, and access is our strongest motivator. Bring live verification earlier — the 1:1 is what makes AI-era ownership real. And rebalance for the middle: fewer required items, more depth optional-but-inviting, with a faster lane for students who arrive advanced. The open question: the top third built exactly what we hoped — is the next design's job to widen that third, or to serve it deeper?

How this course is different

You are encouraged to use AI as a partner. Use it openly, in every part of the work — scoping your project, writing code, reviewing what you built, checking your own understanding. The rest of the design assumes you will.

If you have reservations about using AI, bring them to your check-in. Some of you may have reservations about using AI. If that is the case: first of all, know that we respect your reservation and understand there are good reasons for that. Hope you can also understand that we need to design a course that serves all of the students. If you have reservations, please bring them in the 1-on-1 check-in and we can try to find a way forward to serve you in this course.

This course pays more attention to your process than your output. Before AI, output was the evidence: if you solved the problem, wrote the working code, finished the report, that showed you knew how. In the age of AI, a good output no longer shows that by itself. So the work here focuses on your process — testing it, challenging it, and exercising the motivation, MetacognitionWhat it means. Watching your own thinking while you work — noticing what you actually understand, what you are avoiding, and when your approach is not working.Why it matters here. It is what tells you when to question an AI answer, when to dig deeper, and when to change course. No tool can run it for you., and mindset behind it. One way that becomes concrete: you will be asked, at times, to submit the transcripts of your AI conversations — a transcript shows your process, what you asked, what you challenged, what you decided, in a way the finished output cannot.

Another way you will see this: less weight on the grade. Every week on the course home page carries 🌱 Own your progress guidance — activities that do not affect your grade at all. They are there for exactly what the name says: progress you own, whether or not anyone is scoring it.

Some days it may not feel easy to find what to do next — that is productive friction, and it is deliberate. This capstone also runs differently than in previous years. Past capstones spent most of the semester executing the project. The rest of this page shows how all of this works in practice: a grade that splits evenly between doing the work and explaining it, interviews that sample anything you submitted, and a capstone you frame yourself.

The weekly rhythm

Each week gives you one to three 🌱 Own your progress guidance activities, and at most one 🎯 graded item. Across a sprint the graded items build one evolving piece of work.

Graded items are the work itself. Each one carries a week’s learning goal, and each one gets checked in your next interview, where we pick a part of it and ask you to explain it. Late graded items lose 10% a day. That means you cannot drift far behind before the credit is gone, which is deliberate — we would rather help you in week 4 than discover it in week 12.

Own your progress guidance keeps everything else moving. Short activities that do not affect your grade — there because the progress this course cares about is yours to own, and so you can tell you are on track to reach the next graded item.

How the grade works

Every graded item in this course is worth 100 points, and each sprint’s interview is worth the sum of that sprint’s graded items — a sprint with four graded items carries a 400-point interview. So the grade splits exactly in half: submitting your work on time earns 50%, and explaining it in your interviews earns the other 50%.

Both halves matter. Submitting everything and explaining none of it is an F — reaching a D means defending at least some of your work in conversation. That is deliberate: the work shows what you built, and the interview shows you understood it — which is exactly what every job interview after this course will ask of you.

What happens in the interviews

An interview is a short conversation where we pick a piece of the work you submitted and ask you to walk us through it: what you did, why, what broke, and what you would change. You will not know in advance which piece we pick, so prepare all of it. The bar is explaining your own work to a human, without jargon. Every interview is a live session — in person or on Zoom with your camera on.

These interviews are recorded. When we meet in person, we record the voice in the room — we are still testing the tools for this, so how we do it may change during the semester. When we meet on Zoom, the session is recorded to video. What we are after in both cases is the same thing: a full transcript of the conversation.

What the transcripts are for, plainly: understanding what you need, and helping you grow. A conversation carries things a submission cannot — where you got stuck, what you had already tried, what you were unsure of, what you got interested in along the way. Having your actual words means we can come back to what you said rather than what we remembered, follow up on it, and put help in front of you that fits your situation. And when the same difficulty shows up across many of you, that is the course failing you rather than you failing it — the transcripts are how we find that, and fix it while you are still here rather than for next year’s students.

You sit three to five interviews across the semester, depending on how you are doing and the complexity of your project. The baseline plan — we adjust it during the semester, and a student who misses a week can schedule with the instructor the following week:

  • Week 2 — Sprint 0 interview, in small groups. Everyone.
  • Weeks 4–5 — proposal interview, in small groups. Everyone.
  • Week 9 and week 13 — scheduled by need, alternating by group, so everyone sits at least one interview mid-build. One sitting can cover more than one sprint’s work, and each sprint’s interview points are scored at whichever sitting covers them.
  • Weeks 15–16 — the final defense. Everyone, one interview per student, scheduled in either week.

There are no retakes during the semester — a rough interview is recovered by showing up better prepared at the next one. At the end of the term there is one cumulative retake, only for students who would otherwise land at a D or F, on different questions, capped at a C.

How attendance works

Class time runs on two kinds of days, and the difference matters: on a handful of days the whole class is in the room together, and on every other scheduled day you come only if you booked a slot.

Full-class days — everyone in the room. There are seven of them, and every one of them is a day where what happens depends on who is there:

DateWeekWhat happens
Wed 26 Aug1Orientation — how the course works, and what is happening to entry-level hiring.
Wed 2 Sep2Sprint 1 opens: Know Yourself and Know the Market, worked through together.
Wed 23 Sep5Sprint 2 opens: your proposal is behind you, so we set up how you will actually work.
Wed 14 Oct8Mid-term — hand-written and closed book, in class.
Wed 21 Oct9Sprint 3 opens: judging your work against the standard outside this classroom.
Wed 18 Nov13Sprint 4 opens, and the class plans the Capstone Festival together.
Fri 18 Dec16Capstone Festival, if the class decides in week 13 to hold one.

The pattern is simple: the Wednesday that opens each sprint, plus the mid-term, plus the festival. Sprint openings are in person because that is where the shape of the next four weeks gets set, and that goes better with everyone in the room.

Every other scheduled day — by appointment. The remaining Wednesdays and all the Fridays run as live-session slots. You sign up through the Canvas scheduling link when an interview or a working session calls for it, and you come in person or on Zoom with your camera on. If you have not signed up for a slot that day, there is nothing for you to attend.

Your own booked slots count as attendance. Missing one loses its points, and live sessions cannot be made up after the fact — so if something comes up, move the booking rather than skipping it.

Where your capstone work comes from

You do not start from a blank page, and we do not hand you a project. You choose a real problem space — an open project with real users, open issues, and a documented way to contribute — and you find the gap inside it worth your semester. The framing is yours. The pressure to make it good comes from people outside this classroom. See the candidate problem spaces →

A certification answers the same question. If the gap in your preparation is best closed by earning a real credential, that is a full route rather than a lesser one.

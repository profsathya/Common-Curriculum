Purpose

Show the depth of your thinking about your project and the market it answers — at length in writing before class, briefly by hand in class, and in conversation with a partner afterwards.

How it works

Take-home, before class

All eight answered at length, submitted before class, references allowed.

In class, written

A subset of the eight is handed out; choose a smaller subset (5 given, answer 3) and summarise your answer in writing, Closed bookWhat it means. Nothing open on the desk: no notes, no laptop, no take-home answers. You write from what you understood.Why it matters. A lesson you can put in your own words without looking is one you own. The take-home is where you work it out; the written part is where you show it is yours., on the specific aspect the paper names.

Paired

Type up your partner’s written answers, ask two follow-up questions, write a summary of their model of their own work.

The questions are published now so you can work through them in week 7 while there is still time to change what you find.

Questions

Every question is answerable from what you understood about your own work, not from what you can recall — the written part is closed book, so nothing here needs a reference you would have to look up. “You” means you personally, also in a team project: your decisions, your contribution, your learning.

Paste this into a Google doc and answer each question under it.

Each + opens guidance, one example, a reflection question and a box to write in. Nothing under a + is required.

  1. 1.What you learned about the market you are aiming at: what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago, and what you expect to change next — and why you think so.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Write what you understood, not what you collected. The The market you are aiming atWhat it means. The niche of roles and employers you would apply to next year — the postings you read in weeks 2 and 4, and the people who hold those roles now.What a good answer looks like. A lesson you drew from those postings that changed a plan of yours, in your own words. The postings can be cited if you like; the lesson is what counts. question asks for the expectations you did not know were there when you started, and for what you now think changes next.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “Three years ago the postings I aimed at asked for a language and a framework; now five of six ask for something shipped and kept working for real users, and two ask how I used AI to do it. I expect the second to become the default.”

    Reflection

    What changed in your own plans because of this?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago.
    • It says what you expect to change next, and why you think so.
    • The lesson is in your own words; a reference, if you use one, supports it rather than replaces it.
  2. 2.What problem your project solves, for whom, and what convinced you the need is real — the moment, conversation or evidence that settled it.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Three things: for whom, what it solves, and the moment, conversation or evidence that convinced you the need is real. Spend the most words on the third — the point where it stopped being a plausible idea and became a problem you wanted.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “It settled for me when the second transfer student gave up at seven minutes — the survey had said it, but watching it made it a problem I wanted.”

    Reflection

    What would have un-convinced you?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what problem the project solves and for whom.
    • It names the moment, conversation or evidence that convinced you — one you were there for or found yourself.
    • Someone who was there would recognise the moment from your description.
  3. 3.How your work answers what the market asks for: which expectations it speaks to, and which it does not.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    Both halves. Name the expectations from question 1 that your work speaks to, and name the ones it does not. The second half is as much a part of the answer as the first.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “It speaks to ‘shipped to real users’ and ‘kept it working’; it does not speak to ‘worked in a team of engineers’, and I know that.”

    Reflection

    Is the half it does not speak to one you should act on this semester or next?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names expectations your work speaks to, and they are ones you named in question 1.
    • It names expectations your work does not speak to.
    • Each expectation is tied to something in your work, not to the project in general.
  4. 4.How did you use AI for this project, if you did? How did it help, and what human value did you bring in that process?
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    If you used AI, say how, and how it helped. Then say what human value you brought in that process — the judgment, the domain knowledge, the testing with real people, the part that needed a person. Your proposal named this as a goal in task 5; this is where you say what you actually brought.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “It built the first search page in ten minutes with invented data; I brought the survey, the offices, and the test with students who had never seen the page — everything that made the data real.”

    Reflection

    Where did you accept its output without checking, and what happened?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says whether you used AI, and if so how, with something specific you gave it or got from it.
    • It says how it helped.
    • It says what you brought, specific to this project — something a classmate could not paste into their own answer unchanged.
  5. 5.Pick one decision you made — design, scope or tool. Analyse what other options were available, why you made this particular choice, and what would be a good argument to revisit that decision now.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    One decision — design, scope or tool. Name the other options you had, say why you chose this one, and give a good argument to revisit it now. The argument to revisit is the part that shows the decision is still yours rather than a habit.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “Static site over an app: the options were an app, a Canvas page, a static site; I chose static because an office can edit a file; the argument to revisit is that no office has edited one yet.”

    Reflection

    What would make you actually revisit it?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It is one decision, and it is a design, scope or tool decision.
    • It names at least one other option you could have taken.
    • It says why you chose this one, and gives an argument to revisit it that you would take seriously.
  6. 6.One thing you set out to learn and can now do: how you know you can, and how you can demonstrate to a hiring manager that you have learned it.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    One thing, from what you set out to learn. Say how you know you can do it now, and how you would demonstrate it to a hiring manager — something they could look at or watch, not something you would tell them.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “I can run a usability test: three timed students, a script, and the changes I made from what they did; the demonstration is the before/after times.”

    Reflection

    Which item from your learning plan is this?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It is one thing, and it is something you set out to learn.
    • It says how you know you can do it — something you did, not something you feel.
    • The demonstration is something a hiring manager could look at or watch.
  7. 7.Where your part of the work is hard right now: what is not working, what surprised you, and what you will do next.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    What is not working, what surprised you, and your next move. The hard part is the point of the question, not a confession.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “Offices reply slowly; I did not expect the bottleneck to be people; next move is one office in person on Thursday.”

    Reflection

    What would a strong week look like?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It names something in your part of the work that is not working right now.
    • It says what surprised you.
    • The next move is something you could do this week, with a day or a person attached.
  8. 8.What you now understand about the bar for the roles you want, from looking at real work by people who hold them — and where yours stands against it today.
    Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

    Guidance

    What you now understand about the The barWhat it means. The standard real work meets in the roles you want — what a portfolio piece, a repo or a write-up by someone who holds the role actually contains.How you find it. By looking at their work, not at a job description: the pieces people in those roles put in front of hiring managers.What a good answer looks like. A specific feature of their work that yours does not have yet, and what would put it there., from real work by people in the roles you want, and where yours stands against it today. Today, not where you hope it will be by December.

    An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy

    “Their portfolio pieces have a user quote and a number; mine has neither yet — the timed tests give me the number by week 10.”

    Reflection

    Which piece of theirs would you most like to have made?

    Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI
    • It says what the bar is, from real work you looked at by people who hold the roles.
    • It says where your work stands against it today.
    • The two are compared on the same feature — what theirs has and yours does or does not.

Criteria

Done — all eight answered at length in the take-home; each answer is about your own project and your own part in it; references, where used, support a lesson rather than replace one.

Strong — each answer could be summarised by hand, closed book, without losing its point — the lesson is yours, not the reference’s; 1 and 3 connect the market to your work in both directions; 2 rests on the moment or evidence that settled it; 4 is honest about what AI did and what you did; 5 names a real alternative you could have taken; 6 names a demonstration a hiring manager could check; 7 and 8 say where you stand today, not where you hope to be.

Checklist — one line per question. Run it on your own draft; your reviewer runs the same list.

  1. Say what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago, what you expect to change next, and why.
  2. Name the problem, who it is for, and the moment, conversation or evidence that settled it.
  3. Name the expectations your work speaks to and the ones it does not.
  4. Say how you used AI, if you did, how it helped, and what you brought.
  5. Name one decision, the other options, why this one, and an argument to revisit it now.
  6. Name one thing you can now do, how you know, and how you would demonstrate it to a hiring manager.
  7. Say what is not working, what surprised you, and what you will do next.
  8. Say what the bar is, from real work by people in the roles, and where yours stands today.

What that looks like — what he decided, what AI built, and how it is checked

What he decided

  • The purpose, the three parts and the eight questions are fixed wording from his question set of 3 September 2026. Nothing on this page rewrites, reorders or adds to them.
  • Questions test lessons, not recall. The written part is closed book, so every question is answerable from what you understood about your own work; nothing on this page asks for a reference, a name or a number you would have to look up.
  • The two layers. The questions stand on their own; everything under a + is optional. A student who copies the questions into a document and answers them there has done the whole take-home.
  • The AI question is asked, not asserted. Question 4 asks how you used AI, if you did, and what you brought. Nothing here states the answer for you.
  • The submission is the text itself, and the points are 200 for the mid-term as a whole.

What AI built

  • The second layer: the guidance, the examples, and the reflection questions under each question, from his design and in his register. Every example runs on one project, Campus Compass, so the examples read as one student’s process rather than eight unrelated ones.
  • The criteria — done, strong, and the checklist — proposed from the question set, for his edit.
  • The page mechanics: the copy buttons, the boxes that save in your browser, and the AI feedback button under each box.
  • The notes behind the AI feedback button — for each question, what is asked for and what is observable in an answer. The feedback uses only those criteria, quotes your own words, asks one question, and never rewrites what you wrote. It treats a lesson in your own words as meeting the criterion; a missing reference is never a missing element. It does not judge readiness or prospects, and it does not give a grade.

Read the Market

Due Wednesday 2 September, 11:59 p.m. · started in Wednesday’s session · about 45 minutes

Purpose

Learn to read a job market well enough to aim at it, instead of applying harder at it. Telling evidence apart from guesswork about what employers want is a skill you will use every time you change jobs, and it is the part of a search most people never practise.

You come out of this with three things: two things employers in a space you care about are asking for right now, one thing you expect to change in the next year or two, and your reason for each.

What you are building on

From week 1: your AI Dojo, set up on the Set Up Your CST499 AI Dojo page. If you take the AI route below, this is where you use it. Setting it up takes about fifteen minutes if you have not yet.

Assumed: nothing about the market. If you have never read a job posting closely, this is the right place to start — the reading is the activity.

Where the market actually is

Start with the numbers, because they change what a sensible plan looks like. When remote work opened jobs to national and global applicant pools, applications per posting roughly tripled. Applications per hire went from around 100 in 2021 to around 300 now, and one recent data-team opening at Ashby drew about 1,500 people. On average, about 18 applicants per role get a scheduled interview. Recruiters run AI at the top of that funnel to move faster, but a human still makes every final call — Ashby does not let AI reject anyone. Meanwhile big companies pulled back on junior hiring and startups picked it up, so some of the openings moved to companies of fifteen people.

Here is what those numbers mean, and it is not what most people assume. If 300 people ask for a table at a restaurant with 18 seats, the 282 who do not get in were not judged and found wanting — the room ran out. Hiring teams optimise for picking a strong twenty, not for reading everyone carefully, so a genuinely good fit gets cut without a call all the time. That is a numbers problem rather than a verdict on you, and numbers problems have a different answer than working harder at the same thing. The answer is to aim: fewer applications, into a space you can describe, with evidence that you understand it. Henry, one of the recruiters in the fireside session, shortlisted 2 people out of 300 on the strength of cover letters a human had actually written.

Those figures come from two sessions, and what is quoted here is a slice of each, chosen to make one point. Both are worth going through in full on your own time. Inside Hiring with Ashby is eight short clips, about 33 minutes total, with Kevin Connolly's reference slides. Fireside Chat with Recruiters is a chaptered 89-minute recording with two recruiters. The parts this page skips — how long hiring takes, what survives a thousand resume reads, negotiating, the student questions — answer things you will want to know later this semester.

What to work out

1. Identify a space to look at. One sentence: a kind of work you would want to build in, in a kind of company, solving a kind of problem. It does not have to be right, and you are not committing to it — you need something specific enough to search for. “Backend work on data pipelines at climate-tech companies” gives you something to type into a job board. “Software engineering” describes two hundred thousand openings and helps you search for none of them. If the sentence changes on Tuesday because of what you find, that is the process working.

2. Identify two things employers are asking for right now in that space, and give your reason for each. Read 10 to 15 real, currently open postings from different companies where your space shows up in the responsibilities, and quote the phrases rather than summarising them. Something counts as “what employers are asking for” when it repeats across at least half of what you read, so say how many out of how many. A common wrong turn here is to reach for a technology you already know and were pleased to spot — one posting is an anecdote, and a job title is not a requirement.

3. Identify one thing you expect to change in the next year or two, and give your reason as two parts: the DriverWhat it means. The force actually pushing a change — a cost that moved, a rule that changed, a capability that got cheap, or a shift in who is buying.Why it matters. Once you can say what is pushing something, you can tell whether it will keep pushing. A change with nothing behind it is a guess dressed up as a forecast.What a good one looks like. “Big companies cut junior headcount, so startups became where junior roles live” identifies a driver. “AI is changing everything” does not. behind it, and the SignalWhat it means. The observable thing you would watch to find out whether the change is really happening — something you could check again in three months and get a yes or a no.Why it matters. It turns a prediction into something you can be wrong about. It is also what your standing prompt watches later, so your picture of the market stays current without you starting over.What a good one looks like. “More postings in this space ask for testing and evaluating AI output” is checkable. “AI gets better” is not. you would watch. Look for movement rather than opinion — compare today's postings against ones from a year or two ago, read the Talent Trends reports behind the Ashby clips, follow company announcements and hiring pages. The fireside session has a worked example in it: big companies pulled back on junior hiring and startups picked it up. Then run the test on what you wrote: could someone else check your signal in three months without asking you what you meant?

This way of reading a change — find the force, then find the thing you can watch — is the core move in futures thinking, and there is a lot more of it than we use here. If you want the method properly, the Institute for the Future teaches it in Ready, Set, Future! Introduction to Futures Thinking on Coursera, part of a longer Futures Thinking specialization. It is optional, and nothing on this page depends on it.

4. In class on Wednesday, with a partner. You will run this live in the Wednesday session, in pairs. Your partner's job is not to agree with you. It is to push on your reasons: which of these is evidence, and which is a guess? Could that signal actually be checked in three months, by someone who is not you? Swap, and do the same for them. Bring whatever you have when you walk in, even if it is rough — rough is easier to push on than nothing.

Two ways to do this

Use one, or both. They end at the same three answers, go in the same Canvas box, and are worth the same. Whichever you do, give it about 45 minutes. Open the ones you want with the +.

Reading postings closely and writing your reasons out is where a lot of the thinking happens, and it is worth doing for its own sake. Having an AI fetch and sort while you decide what matters is a different skill, and that is worth practising too. If you want both, write your space and your first guesses out yourself, then take them to your Dojo and make it argue with your evidence.

With your AI DojoYou direct the search; it fetches and sorts, and you decide what counts.

Open the Dojo you set up in week 1 and give it this, with your own space filled in:

I am an early-career computing student aiming at [YOUR SPACE]. Find 12 real, currently open job postings from different companies where that space is explicit in the responsibilities or requirements. For each, give me the company, title, link, and quote the 3–5 exact phrases that describe the work. Then tell me which phrases recur across at least half of them, and show me the count. Do not invent postings — only ones you can link to.

Then, separately: what has changed in postings like these over the last two years, what is driving that change, and what observable signal would tell me in three months whether it is continuing? For each claim, show me your evidence, and tell me plainly where you are guessing.

What comes back is a draft of your answer, not your answer. You supplied the space. You decide which repeated phrases actually matter for the work you want to do. When it hands you a claim with no source, say so and ask again. Ask it directly what it is guessing about — then pick one of its claims and check it yourself against a real posting you open. If the posting does not say what it said, that tells you how much of the rest to trust.

If after ten minutes you cannot settle on a space worth scanning, start with the Know the Market practice Dojo instead. It asks the questions one at a time until a space shows up, and it will send you out to look at real postings and real people in those roles as you go. Open https://dojo.symbioticthinking.ai/?topic=know-market — if it asks for a key, use the link from the week 1 email, subject Symbiotic Thinking Dojo API Key. Then bring what it produced back to your own Dojo and run the prompt above there, where the context stays with you for the rest of the semester.

By hand (Without using AI)You search the postings yourself and keep your own tally.

Same three answers, same evidence bar.

Any job board works. The bar is the one above — different companies, currently open, your space explicit in the responsibilities rather than guessed at from the title.

Keep a tally as you read. Copy out the phrases that describe the work — the exact words, not your summary of them. Put a mark next to a phrase each time it turns up again. After ten postings you will have two or three phrases with several marks and a long tail with one each. The marked ones are your answer to step 2, and the tally is your reason.

For the change, compare against the past. Find postings for the same kind of role from a year or two ago — company career pages, archived listings, the Talent Trends reports behind the Ashby clips. What is in today's that was not in those? Then work out what would be pushing that, and what you could watch to check it.

What you write is what you hand in.

Submit

One text box in Canvas, whichever way you did it.

1. Your space, in one sentence.

2. Two things employers are asking for now, each with the phrases you found and the count — how many of how many postings.

3. One thing you expect to change, with its driver and its signal.

4. What your partner pushed back on in class, and whether it changed anything.

5. Your record, underneath. If you used the Dojo, paste the conversation. If you worked by hand, paste your notes and your tally. If you did both, include both.

What done and good look like

This one is not graded. It is the market half of the picture you finish on Tuesday, and you will use it in the graded work later in this sprint.

Done is a space, two current asks with quoted phrases and counts behind them, and one expected change with a driver and a signal.

Good is where the evidence moved you. Maybe the phrases that kept repeating were not the ones you expected, or the space you started with turned out to be two different spaces, or your partner found the guess hiding inside your signal. Say what moved and why.

Before you finish

Two questions, worth answering after the work rather than before it.

What did you believe about this market before you opened the postings, and where did the postings disagree with you?

Which of your three answers would be the most embarrassing to be wrong about, and what would you have to look at to find out?

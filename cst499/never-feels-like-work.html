What Would I Do Anyway

Due Friday 4 September, 11:59 p.m. · about 30 minutes

Purpose

Work out what you would be doing even if nobody asked you to, so that the market you aim at is one worth hitting. This is the question that decides whether the effort you put in over the next few years is effort you can actually sustain.

You come out of this with several concrete moments where the work stopped feeling like work, and an honest attempt at what runs through them.

What you are building on

From Wednesday: your market read. Have the two things employers are asking for in front of you when you get to the last step — not at the start. You want to answer this one without checking whether the answer is convenient.

Assumed: nothing. You do not need to have decided anything about your career to answer this. If anything, having decided makes it harder.

Why this is not the soft part of the week

A question about purpose, sitting in the middle of a week about the job market, looks like the part you can skip. It is the opposite. It is the part that makes the rest work, and here is the argument.

AI is already good at the surface layer of most technical work — the first draft, the standard implementation, the obvious answer, the version that would have taken you an afternoon. What is left is the layer underneath: the problem you keep turning over for three weeks, the detail nobody asked you to fix, the question you follow past the point where it stopped being assigned. Think about the last time you fixed something nobody had asked you to fix. Nobody made you do that, and nobody does it on work they do not care about.

So caring is not a nice extra sitting on top of skill. It is what buys you the depth, and the depth is what makes the work yours rather than something anyone could have generated. That is why this question sits between the market questions instead of somewhere after them: the market tells you where the openings are, and this tells you which of them are worth your effort. Skip it and you end up optimising for a snapshot — aiming very precisely at whatever employers happened to be asking for in the week you looked.

What to work out

What would my work purpose be? What would you have to be doing so that it never quite feels like you are working? Answer it honestly, not impressively.

Go for concrete moments, not adjectives. “I like solving hard problems” is true of almost everyone and tells you nothing you can use. A time you lost track of the hour tells you a lot: what were you actually doing, who was it for, what made you keep going when it got tedious? Collect several of those before you try to say what they have in common.

Give the second and third answer. The first one is usually the impressive one — what you would say in an interview, or what sounds like the major you chose. The useful answer normally shows up once the presentable ones are out of the way.

Then write what they have in common, in a few sentences. Not a slogan. If the moments do not have anything in common yet, write that instead, and write which two are furthest apart — that is a real finding and it is worth more than a tidy sentence you do not believe.

A common wrong turn: writing the answer you think would go well with Wednesday's market scan. It is the wrong way round. This one is supposed to be able to disagree with the market, and on Tuesday you will find out where it does.

Two ways to do this

Use one, or both. They end at the same place, go in the same Canvas box, and are worth the same. Whichever you do, give it about 30 minutes in one sitting. Open the ones you want with the +.

Writing this out by hand is where a lot of the thinking happens, and it is worth doing for its own sake — a question like this one rewards a slow pen. Having an AI keep asking until you get past your first answer is a different skill, and that is worth practising too. If you want both, write your own answers first, then take them to your Dojo and let it push on the ones that sound rehearsed.

With your AI DojoIt interviews you, one question at a time, and does not let you stop at the first answer.

Open the Dojo you set up in week 1 and give it this:

I am working out what kind of work I would do even if nobody asked me to. Interview me about it — one question at a time, and wait for my answer before you ask the next one.

Start from concrete moments rather than adjectives: ask me about times I lost track of the hour, what I was actually doing, who it was for, and what kept me going when it got tedious. When an answer of mine sounds like something I would put in a cover letter, say so and ask again. Do not summarise me until I have given you at least six real moments. Then play back what you heard, and I will tell you where you are wrong.

Stay past the first ten minutes. The answers worth having show up once the obvious ones are gone, and this is exactly the kind of conversation people quit early.

Correct it when it gets you wrong. If the playback is off, say so and keep going until it matches what you meant. That correcting is the work — you are the one who knows, and it is drafting.

If after ten minutes your answers still read like a cover letter, start with the Know Yourself practice Dojo instead. It has more structure and asks the questions in a set order until something real comes out. Open https://dojo.symbioticthinking.ai/?topic=know-yourself — if it asks for a key, use the link from the week 1 email, subject Symbiotic Thinking Dojo API Key. Then bring what it produced back to your own Dojo, which keeps your context for the rest of the semester.

In writing (Without using AI)The same questions, in the same order, answered on paper.

Work through these in order. What you write is what you hand in.

1. Six moments. Six times you lost track of the hour. They do not all have to be about computing, or about work, and school counts. For each: what were you doing, who was it for, and what kept you going when it got tedious?

2. The first answer, written down and set aside. In one sentence, what do those moments have in common? Write it, then draw a line under it. That is the presentable answer.

3. Now go again. Read your six moments as if they belonged to someone else. What is actually going on in them that your first sentence missed? Two or three sentences.

4. The disagreement. Which two of your six moments are furthest apart? What does having both of them tell you?

5. Where it collides with Wednesday. Look at the two things employers are asking for that you found on Wednesday. Does any of this week's work sit inside them, or outside them? Either answer is fine, and Tuesday is where you do something about it.

Submit

One text box in Canvas, whichever way you did it.

1. Your moments. Several of them, concrete — what you were doing, who for, what kept you going.

2. What they have in common, in a few sentences. Or what they do not, and which two are furthest apart.

3. Where it sits next to Wednesday's market read. Inside what employers are asking for, outside it, or partly both.

4. Your record, underneath. If you used a Dojo, paste the conversation. If you wrote it out, paste your writing as you wrote it. If you did both, include both.

What done and good look like

This one is not graded, and it still matters more than most graded things this month, because the project you argue for in this sprint has to be work you would actually stay up for.

Done is several concrete moments and an honest attempt at what runs through them.

Good is where the second pass disagreed with the first. If what you ended up writing is what you would have written before you started, you stopped at the presentable answer.

Before you finish

Two questions, worth answering after the work.

What did you write down and then decide not to say out loud — and what made it feel unsayable?

If nothing you found overlaps with what the market is asking for, what would you want to do about that? There is more than one reasonable answer, and Tuesday is where you pick one.

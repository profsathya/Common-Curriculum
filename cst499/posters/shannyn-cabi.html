Capstone: our joint research on how AI models handle estimation questions with no single right answer, mentored by Dr. Dongji Feng.

The work

Two scores for the same AI answers disagree about how good the models are.

Why it matters: anyone comparing AI models by accuracy alone can miss a model that is confidently wrong.

Decision

Scored the model's confidence range with the Winkler interval score instead of a standard right-or-wrong calibration metric.

Because: estimation answers aren't simply right or wrong, and they can be off by a factor of 10 or 1,000.

Obstacle

Simply averaging the two scores hides the difference we're trying to show.

What we're trying next: a rank-based combination, checked with a bootstrap significance test.

The work

Accuracy and confidence tell different stories about the same answers.

We asked GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek V3.2 Fermi questions, estimates like "How many cups of coffee are consumed at CSUMB each day?" Each answer gets two scores: fp_score for how close the estimate is, and IWQ for whether the model's 95% confidence range was honest.

How we worked with AI
What it gave usA summary of existing calibration metrics, like Expected Calibration Error, so we could compare them quickly.
What we asked next, and whyWhich of them work when an answer isn't just right or wrong. The summary assumed every metric needed a yes-or-no label, so we pushed on that.
How we checked itWe went back to the original papers and confirmed none fit estimates that can be off by orders of magnitude. That gap is where IWQ came from.
Decision

The Winkler interval score, adapted into IWQ, instead of a right-or-wrong calibration metric.

Other options were Expected Calibration Error, which needs every answer labelled right or wrong, or asking the model for one confidence number like "80% sure." Neither fits an estimate that can be close, off by 10×, or off by 1,000×.

Evidence

Why we changed it to log space. The original Winkler score measures the width of a range directly, so a question whose answer is in the billions would swamp one in the hundreds. Measuring in orders of magnitude puts every question on the same footing.

Did it pay off? Compared against fp_score on the same answers, IWQ shuffled the model ranking less (44% vs 78% swap rate) and its scores varied less from question to question (chart below). It still agrees with accuracy in general (Kendall's Tau 0.79 to 0.87), so it adds information instead of repeating it.

What it cost us

IWQ trusts the range the model reports about itself instead of measuring it from repeated runs. Measuring it properly would mean asking each question many times, which multiplies the token cost we want to compare against accuracy next.

Obstacle · not solved yet

The two scores see the same gap at very different sizes, so averaging them hides it.

The obvious ways to combine them either shrink the gap between clear and specific questions, or behave differently depending on which dataset we test.

# Mid-term

Week 8 · Wednesday 14 October · two items, 100 points each · Mid-term

## Purpose

Show the depth of your thinking about your project and the market it answers — at length in writing before class, briefly by hand in class, and in conversation with a partner afterwards.

## How it works

- **Take-home mid-term · 100 points** — All eight answered at length, submitted in Canvas before class on 14 October, references allowed. You have a week; nothing here needs more time than that.
- **In-class activity · 100 points · first part** — Five of the eight questions are handed out; choose three and summarise your answers by hand, one page, two sides at most, closed book, on the specific aspect the paper names.
  *Tooltip on “closed book”* — **Closed book** — *What it means.* Nothing open on the desk: no notes, no laptop, no take-home answers. You write from what you understood. *Why it matters.* A lesson you can put in your own words without looking is one you own. The take-home is where you work it out; the written part is where you show it is yours.
- **In-class activity · second part, paired** — Swap with a partner: type up their written answers, ask two follow-up questions, write a summary of their model of their own work, and submit the typed version in Canvas. The handwritten sheets are collected; the typed versions are what count. Do your best in the hour — write what you can.

The questions are published now so you can work through them in week 7 while there is still time to change what you find.

## Questions

*Every question is answerable from what you understood about your own work, not from what you can recall — the written part is closed book, so nothing here needs a reference you would have to look up. “You” means you personally, also in a team project: your decisions, your contribution, your learning.*

Each **+** opens guidance, one example, a reflection question and a box to write in. Nothing under a **+** is required.

### Question 1 — The market

What you learned about the market you are aiming at: what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago, and what you expect to change next — and why you think so.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 1.** What employers expect now that they did not, what you expect to change next, and why.
*Placeholder:* What you understood about the market — and what you now expect to change next, and why.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It says what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago.
- It says what you expect to change next, and why you think so.
- The lesson is in your own words; a reference, if you use one, supports it rather than replaces it.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** Write what you understood, not what you collected. The market question asks for the expectations you did not know were there when you started, and for what you now think changes next.

*Tooltip on “market”* — **The market you are aiming at** — *What it means.* The niche of roles and employers you would apply to next year — the postings you read in weeks 2 and 4, and the people who hold those roles now. *What a good answer looks like.* A lesson you drew from those postings that changed a plan of yours, in your own words. The postings can be cited if you like; the lesson is what counts.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “Three years ago the postings I aimed at asked for a language and a framework; now five of six ask for something shipped and kept working for real users, and two ask how I used AI to do it. I expect the second to become the default.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** What changed in your own plans because of this?

### Question 2 — The problem

What problem your project solves, for whom, and what convinced you the need is real — the moment, conversation or evidence that settled it.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 2.** The problem, who it is for, and what settled it.
*Placeholder:* For whom, what it solves, and the moment, conversation or evidence that settled it.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It says what problem the project solves and for whom.
- It names the moment, conversation or evidence that convinced you — one you were there for or found yourself.
- Someone who was there would recognise the moment from your description.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** Three things: for whom, what it solves, and the moment, conversation or evidence that convinced you the need is real. Spend the most words on the third — the point where it stopped being a plausible idea and became a problem you wanted.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “It settled for me when the second transfer student gave up at seven minutes — the survey had said it, but watching it made it a problem I wanted.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** What would have un-convinced you?

### Question 3 — The gap it closes

How your work answers what the market asks for: which expectations it speaks to, and which it does not.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 3.** The expectations your work speaks to, and the ones it does not.
*Placeholder:* Speaks to: … ⏎ Does not speak to: …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It names expectations your work speaks to, and they are ones you named in question 1.
- It names expectations your work does not speak to.
- Each expectation is tied to something in your work, not to the project in general.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** Both halves. Name the expectations from question 1 that your work speaks to, and name the ones it does not. The second half is as much a part of the answer as the first.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “It speaks to ‘shipped to real users’ and ‘kept it working’; it does not speak to ‘worked in a team of engineers’, and I know that.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** Is the half it does not speak to one you should act on this semester or next?

### Question 4 — How I used AI

How did you use AI for this project, if you did? How did it help, and what human value did you bring in that process?

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 4.** How you used AI, if you did; how it helped; what you brought.
*Placeholder:* How you used it, if you did — how it helped — what human value you brought.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It says whether you used AI, and if so how, with something specific you gave it or got from it.
- It says how it helped.
- It says what you brought, specific to this project — something a classmate could not paste into their own answer unchanged.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** If you used AI, say how, and how it helped. Then say what human value you brought in that process — the judgment, the domain knowledge, the testing with real people, the part that needed a person. Your proposal named this as a goal in task 5; this is where you say what you actually brought.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “It built the first search page in ten minutes with invented data; I brought the survey, the offices, and the test with students who had never seen the page — everything that made the data real.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** Where did you accept its output without checking, and what happened?

### Question 5 — One decision

Pick one decision you made — design, scope or tool. Analyse what other options were available, why you made this particular choice, and what would be a good argument to revisit that decision now.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 5.** The decision, the other options, why this one, and the argument to revisit it.
*Placeholder:* The decision — the other options — why this one — a good argument to revisit it now.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It is one decision, and it is a design, scope or tool decision.
- It names at least one other option you could have taken.
- It says why you chose this one, and gives an argument to revisit it that you would take seriously.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** One decision — design, scope or tool. Name the other options you had, say why you chose this one, and give a good argument to revisit it now. The argument to revisit is the part that shows the decision is still yours rather than a habit.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “Static site over an app: the options were an app, a Canvas page, a static site; I chose static because an office can edit a file; the argument to revisit is that no office has edited one yet.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** What would make you actually revisit it?

### Question 6 — One thing I can now do

One thing you set out to learn and can now do: how you know you can, and how you can demonstrate to a hiring manager that you have learned it.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 6.** The thing you can now do, how you know, and how you would demonstrate it.
*Placeholder:* What you can now do — how you know — how you would demonstrate it to a hiring manager.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It is one thing, and it is something you set out to learn.
- It says how you know you can do it — something you did, not something you feel.
- The demonstration is something a hiring manager could look at or watch.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** One thing, from what you set out to learn. Say how you know you can do it now, and how you would demonstrate it to a hiring manager — something they could look at or watch, not something you would tell them.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “I can run a usability test: three timed students, a script, and the changes I made from what they did; the demonstration is the before/after times.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** Which item from your learning plan is this?

### Question 7 — Where my part is hard

Where your part of the work is hard right now: what is not working, what surprised you, and what you will do next.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 7.** What is not working, what surprised you, and what you will do next.
*Placeholder:* Not working: … ⏎ Surprised me: … ⏎ Next: …

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It names something in your part of the work that is not working right now.
- It says what surprised you.
- The next move is something you could do this week, with a day or a person attached.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** What is not working, what surprised you, and your next move. The hard part is the point of the question, not a confession.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “Offices reply slowly; I did not expect the bottleneck to be people; next move is one office in person on Thursday.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** What would a strong week look like?

### Question 8 — The bar

What you now understand about the bar for the roles you want, from looking at real work by people who hold them — and where yours stands against it today.

*Box label:* **Your answer to question 8.** What you understand about the bar, and where yours stands today.
*Placeholder:* What the bar is, from real work by people in the roles — and where yours stands today.

**Check it yourself — the same criteria, without the AI**

- It says what the bar is, from real work you looked at by people who hold the roles.
- It says where your work stands against it today.
- The two are compared on the same feature — what theirs has and yours does or does not.

*The + reads:* Guidance, an example, a question, and a place to write

**Guidance (behind the +).** What you now understand about the bar, from real work by people in the roles you want, and where yours stands against it today. Today, not where you hope it will be by December.

*Tooltip on “bar”* — **The bar** — *What it means.* The standard real work meets in the roles you want — what a portfolio piece, a repo or a write-up by someone who holds the role actually contains. *How you find it.* By looking at their work, not at a job description: the pieces people in those roles put in front of hiring managers. *What a good answer looks like.* A specific feature of their work that yours does not have yet, and what would put it there.

**An example, to shape your thinking — not a model to copy**

- “Their portfolio pieces have a user quote and a number; mine has neither yet — the timed tests give me the number by week 10.”

**Reflection (behind the +).** Which piece of theirs would you most like to have made?

## Criteria

**Checklist** — one line per question. Run it on your own draft; your reviewer runs the same list. *(the click-in at the top of Criteria)*

1. Say what employers expect now that they did not a few years ago, what you expect to change next, and why.
2. Name the problem, who it is for, and the moment, conversation or evidence that settled it.
3. Name the expectations your work speaks to and the ones it does not.
4. Say how you used AI, if you did, how it helped, and what you brought.
5. Name one decision, the other options, why this one, and an argument to revisit it now.
6. Name one thing you can now do, how you know, and how you would demonstrate it to a hiring manager.
7. Say what is not working, what surprised you, and what you will do next.
8. Say what the bar is, from real work by people in the roles, and where yours stands today.

**Done** — all eight answered at length in the take-home; each answer is about your own project and your own part in it; references, where used, support a lesson rather than replace one.

**Strong** — each answer could be summarised by hand, closed book, without losing its point — the lesson is yours, not the reference’s; 1 and 3 connect the market to your work in both directions; 2 rests on the moment or evidence that settled it; 4 is honest about what AI did and what you did; 5 names a real alternative you could have taken; 6 names a demonstration a hiring manager could check; 7 and 8 say where you stand today, not where you hope to be.

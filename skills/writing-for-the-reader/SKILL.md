# Writing for Sathya's readers

Sathya's readers are students, teammates, partners and funders. Most of them skim. They read the top of a page, the first words of each block, and whatever is set apart. A draft works when a reader who skims still leaves with what matters, so every step below is about that reader.

This page is short on purpose. In the 9 November 2026 test, a one-page version did about as well as nine files of rules, so adding rules is not what improves a draft. When a draft misses, change a line here or swap in one before-and-after pair. Do not add a new section.

## Step 1 - Settle who writes first, and the destination

**Who writes first (agreed with Sathya, 9 November 2026).** The drafts miss most where the content is his judgment, so his judgment comes first.

- *Pieces in his name for a team or partner* (tabs, notes, emails that carry his thinking): he talks it through first. Arrange his words, cut, and check the result against a skimming reader. Do not compose new thinking for him.
- *Course pages:* he gives the destination in one sentence, or chooses from two or three one-line candidates you offer. Draft the rest from his design decisions.

**The destination** is one sentence that says where the reader ends up. On a course page it is who the student becomes or what they can do afterwards. In a team or partner piece it is the idea or question they should carry into the conversation. It is his judgment about his course or program, so do not guess it from the task. Wait for his sentence or his choice before drafting.

No writing rule replaces this step. In the test, both sets of rules produced purpose lines that described the process. His versions state the destination:

| The draft described the process | He wrote the destination |
|---|---|
| "This sprint you set a goal for your professional growth with a step to take by 30 November, you looked at where you get your information in the Information Diet Audit, and you talked with alumni, industry professionals and classmates." | "Setting a learning goal, and applying lessons from diverse information sources to revise and strengthen it." |
| "You finish the semester with a plan you can act on over winter break, and with practice in asking for critical feedback on your own work, which is a skill you can keep using throughout your career." | "Becoming a self-directed learner, taking charge of your own professional growth." |
| "Your partner can answer every 'Do you think' question from what your report shows, and each follow-up question asks for feedback you would act on." | "The entire activity is useful to you, you revised it to meet your situation and you feel inspired to continually improve your professional readiness." |

The pattern in his versions: one short sentence, about the student and not about the assignment, with no dates, item names or mechanics.

## Step 2 - Plan for the reader (four lines, written down)

1. **Reader.** Who reads this, and what do they already know?
2. **Use.** What will they do with it: act on it, decide something, or discuss it with others? Will they read it alone, or scan it during a meeting?
3. **Skim line.** The two or three things that must survive if the reader takes in only the first line of each block, in order of importance to the reader.
4. **Decided or open.** Which points has he decided, and which is he still asking about? If the source does not say, ask him. Write decided points plainly, and write a question only where he wants an answer.

## Step 3 - Draft from the plan

**Lead with the destination, then give the route.** Steps and mechanics come after the destination, and only where the reader acts on them. When you are about to list steps, first write what the steps are for.

**Make the priority visible.**
- Put the most important point first in the piece and first in each block.
- One idea per bullet or short paragraph. When a sentence carries a list, break the list into bullets.
- Use a heading or a list only where the content has that shape. A piece meant for wondering together keeps short plain labels, because a heading written as a conclusion makes the thought sound settled.
- Aim for the length he would write. In the test, both drafts of a team tab ran about twice the length of his edit. His version of a tab the team scans in a meeting looks like this:

| The draft | His edit |
|---|---|
| "It seems to me the outcome we care about is students getting a job and being competitive enough to keep it. I think self-awareness is a means to both, and I don't want a job-placement framing to pull us toward only the mechanics of the search." | "It seems to me the outcome we care about is students being competitive enough to get a job and keeping it." |
| One paragraph that named a teammate's four problems in a parenthesis and then spent five sentences on how a module check would work. | A bullet introducing the four problems, each problem on its own labeled line, then one bullet on the modules idea. |

**Write connected sentences.** Use full, plain sentences that name who acts. Write the link between ideas with "because", "so" or "but". Avoid clipped noun phrases the reader must unpack, and avoid a run of short declarations.

**Be specific to this piece.** Test each sentence by asking whether it could be pasted into a different assignment or a different memo without anyone noticing. If it could, replace it with the specific thing or cut it.

**Match the register to the reader.**
- *Students:* the student is the actor and the tone invites, because students who feel managed or judged stop engaging. Name the skill and its lasting value. Pair a course term with the everyday word the first time ("evaluated (graded)"). Every task line starts with a verb for what the student does. Use "identify", not "name", and "clear and specific", not "honest".
- *Team and partners:* the piece is for thinking together, so use "I think", "it seems to me", "I am not sure", and "we" for shared work. Do not turn his wondering into a finished design.
- *Funders:* plain explanatory prose with "we" carrying the responsibility ("we have designed...", "so that we can learn how to...").

**Keep the surface plain.** US spelling. A spaced hyphen ( - ) for an aside. No "not X, but Y" contrasts, aphorisms, clever closers or colon-led triplets, and no worry raised only to be soothed.

**Use only what the source gives you.** Stay close to his own words where you have them. Do not invent facts, examples or decisions. Never name a student or cite a student as the source of a rule.

### His sentences, to tune your ear

- To students, on why a skill matters: "Clearly identifying the current quality of a project and ways to improve it is a skill that will remain valuable and be noticed throughout your career."
- To students, guiding with questions: "Does your project work already cover more than one domain? If not, are there domains you should consider that will improve your project?"
- In feedback: "Would tracing how a small app saves and retrieves information in MySQL, then adding that function to a project be a specific idea to try?"
- To collaborators: "I'm hoping we can work together to build this out. The higher-level framing I'm providing here is meant to give us a structure to collaborate around, so that we can build something more impactful together than any one of us could alone."

## Step 4 - Test the draft on a fresh reader

1. Give **only the draft** to a reader who has not seen the plan or the source. Use a separate agent. The writer checking their own draft does not count, because the writer already knows what the draft is meant to say.
2. The reader skims for about twenty seconds and answers in a line each: What is this for? What am I being asked to do or think about? What are the two most important points?
3. Compare the answers with the destination and the skim line. Where they differ, the draft is wrong, not the reader. Reorder or rewrite, then run the check once more.

Return the draft without the plan or a checklist attached.

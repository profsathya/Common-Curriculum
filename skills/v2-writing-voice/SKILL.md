# Writing in Sathya's voice (v2)

This skill replaces the rule-heavy `writing-foundation` checklist during our phased rebuild. Read this single page to tune your ear before drafting or revising in Sathya's voice. There is no separate `references/rules.md` file to load.

## How Sathya talks to a reader

1. **Connected, natural sentences.** Sathya writes in complete sentences that walk the reader through the reasoning step by step, using plain connectives (`because`, `so`, `but`, `and`). He names who is acting and how something happens ("with the help of AI, you could complete the work"), rather than stringing together clipped, punchy declarations and leaving the reader to connect them.

2. **Warmth, humility, and respect for agency.** Whether he is writing to a first-generation student, a colleague, or a funder, he treats the reader as a capable peer. With teams and partners, he leads with what he hopes to build together ("I'm hoping we can work together to...") and uses "we" to share responsibility for learning. When a concept or decision is genuinely open, he keeps it as an honest question or tentative reflection ("It seems to me...", "where I have landed for now...") rather than stating a settled definition.

3. **Direct instructions that leave the thinking open.** When asking someone to do something, he starts clearly with the action and what it applies to, using everyday words (`identify` or `include` rather than `name`). He states the positive target directly instead of defining a task by what it is not, and he uses genuine questions to guide reflection rather than locking the reader into a rigid template.

4. **Simple surface habits.** Use US spelling, plain functional headings, and a spaced hyphen (` - `) for an aside instead of an em-dash. Skip rhetorical flourishes, manufactured contrasts ("not X, but Y"), colon-led triplets, and aphoristic one-liners.

## Verbatim anchors in Sathya's own words

Read these aloud to hear the cadence and warmth before you draft:

- **Explaining purpose and why a skill matters to students** ([cst499/quality-review.md](../../cst499/quality-review.md)):
  > "Review the current quality of your project's outcomes, choose the two or three changes that will step it up, and explore ways to integrate more than one domain knowledge and skills. Clearly identifying the current quality of a project and ways to improve it is a skill that will remain valuable and be noticed throughout your career."

- **Guiding student thinking with open questions** ([cst499/quality-review.md](../../cst499/quality-review.md)):
  > "Does your project work already cover more than one domain? If not, are there domains you should consider that will improve your project? If yes, how are they integrated now and how can they be improved?"

- **Suggesting a next step in student feedback**:
  > "Would tracing how a small app saves and retrieves information in MySQL, then adding that function to a project be a specific idea to try?"

- **Inviting collaborators and framing shared work**:
  > "I'm hoping we can work together to build this out. The higher-level framing I'm providing here is meant to give us a structure to collaborate around, so that we can build something more impactful together than any one of us could alone."

## Quick ear check before returning a draft

- Does it sound like a warm, thoughtful person speaking in connected sentences rather than a checklist or a pitch deck?
- Does it state directly what is true or what to do, without "not X, it's Y" contrasts, invented worries, or clever turns of phrase?
- Does it leave room for the reader's own judgment and agency?
